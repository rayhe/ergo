Financial Crimes & Pediatric Dentition

The Treasury Department’s Own Anti-Money-Laundering Framework Identifies the Tooth Fairy as the Nation’s Most Prolific Structuring Suspect

The Bank Secrecy Act requires reporting of cash transactions exceeding $10,000. The Tooth Fairy disburses an estimated $388 million annually across 66.5 million individual cash transactions, every one of them below the reporting threshold, every one of them conducted in the dark, under a pillow, without documentation. No Suspicious Activity Report has ever been filed. FinCEN has not opened an investigation. The operation continues nightly.

By Daphne Whitford-Nakamura · April 23, 2026 · 14 min read

I. The Statutory Framework

The Bank Secrecy Act of 1970, codified at 31 U.S.C. § 5311 et seq., was enacted “to require certain reports or records where they have a high degree of usefulness in criminal, tax, or regulatory investigations or proceedings, or in the conduct of intelligence or counterintelligence activities.”1 Congress found that large cash transactions were being used to facilitate drug trafficking, tax evasion, and organized crime, and that the absence of a paper trail made these activities invisible to law enforcement.

The statute’s implementing regulations, promulgated by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) and codified at 31 CFR Chapter X, require financial institutions to file a Currency Transaction Report (CTR) for each transaction involving currency in excess of $10,000.2 The CTR captures the identity of the person conducting the transaction, the nature and amount of the transaction, and the financial institution involved. In fiscal year 2024, financial institutions filed 20.5 million CTRs, for an average of 56,160 filings per day.3

Congress recognized, however, that sophisticated actors would attempt to circumvent the reporting system by breaking large cash amounts into smaller transactions. To address this, the Money Laundering Suppression Act of 1994 added 31 U.S.C. § 5324, which makes it a federal crime for any person to “structure or assist in structuring, or attempt to structure or assist in structuring, any transaction with one or more domestic financial institutions” for the purpose of evading the CTR requirement.4 Structuring is defined as “breaking a single sum of currency exceeding $10,000 into smaller sums” or “conducting a transaction or series of currency transactions at or below $10,000 each” to avoid triggering a report.5

Separately, 31 U.S.C. § 5331 and Internal Revenue Code § 6050I require any person engaged in a trade or business who receives more than $10,000 in cash in a single transaction, or in two or more related transactions, to file Form 8300 with the IRS within 15 days.6 The form requires the filer to identify the payor, the amount of cash received, the nature of the transaction, and the method of payment.

The reporting architecture is comprehensive. It covers financial institutions, businesses, casinos, money services businesses, and any other entity that handles cash in quantities the government considers noteworthy. Its purpose is to ensure that no large-scale cash operation can function in the United States without generating a documentary record.

There is one large-scale cash operation that has functioned in the United States without generating a single documentary record since at least 1908. Its annual disbursements approach $400 million. Its transaction volume exceeds 66 million per year. It has never filed a CTR, a SAR, a Form 8300, or any other report required under the BSA. It operates exclusively in cash, exclusively at night, and exclusively without the knowledge or consent of the transaction’s ostensible beneficiary, who is typically asleep.

II. The Entity Under Investigation

The Tooth Fairy is a folkloric figure of early childhood in Western and Western-influenced cultures. The earliest known record of the tradition in the United States is a “Household Hints” item published in the Chicago Daily Tribune on September 18, 1908, by columnist Lillian Brown, who advised parents that “many a refractory child will allow a loose tooth to be removed if he knows about the tooth fairy” and recommended visiting “the 5 cent counter” to “lay in a supply of articles to be used on such occasions.”7

The entity’s operating model is consistent across all known accounts. A child places a recently lost deciduous tooth beneath a pillow before sleep. The Tooth Fairy arrives during the night, removes the tooth, and deposits a cash payment in its place. The child discovers the payment upon waking. No receipt is issued. No invoice is generated. No record of the transaction exists except in the memory of the child, who is not a reliable witness under the Federal Rules of Evidence and who, in any case, was unconscious at the time of the exchange.8

A 1984 study conducted by Rosemary Wells at Northwestern University found that 74 percent of survey respondents believed the Tooth Fairy to be female, 12 percent believed the entity to be neither male nor female, and 8 percent believed it could be either.9 The entity’s physical form, legal domicile, and taxpayer identification number remain undetermined. Wells was unable to locate a business registration in any U.S. state or territory.

What is known is that the entity operates in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories. It has been in continuous operation for at least 118 years. It employs a cash-only business model with no known banking relationship. Its activities are conducted entirely at night. And it has achieved a scale of financial operations that, in any other context, would have triggered a FinCEN investigation before the end of its first fiscal quarter.

III. The Transaction Data

The Delta Dental Plans Association has conducted the Original Tooth Fairy Poll® annually since 1998, surveying approximately 1,000 parents of children ages 6 to 12 regarding Tooth Fairy compensation practices. The 2026 poll, conducted between January 5 and January 15, 2026, found that the average value of a single lost tooth increased 17 percent from the prior year, from $5.01 to $5.84.10 The first lost tooth commands a 23 percent premium, averaging $7.17. Since the poll’s inception, the average cash gift has increased 349 percent, from $1.30 in 1998 to $5.84 in 2026.

The same organization’s August 2025 survey found that the Tooth Fairy visited more than 83 percent of homes with age-eligible children, up from 78 percent in 2024.11 Market penetration is expanding.

The United States Census Bureau and the National Center for Health Statistics report approximately 3.66 million live births annually in the United States.12 Children possess 20 primary (deciduous) teeth, which are lost over an approximately seven-year period between the ages of 6 and 13.13 This produces a standing population of approximately 25.6 million children in active tooth-loss at any given time (3.66 million births per year × 7 years of deciduous tooth loss). Each child loses an average of 2.86 teeth per year (20 teeth ÷ 7 years).

Applying the 83 percent participation rate yields approximately 21.3 million children receiving Tooth Fairy payments in a given year. At 2.86 teeth per child per year, this produces approximately 60.9 million individual cash transactions annually. At $5.84 per transaction, the estimated aggregate annual disbursement is $355.7 million.14

When the first-tooth premium is factored in (approximately 3.04 million first-time recipients per year at the elevated $7.17 rate), the adjusted annual total rises to approximately $359.7 million. Accounting for parents who report leaving more than the average, the Delta Dental data implies an upper-bound estimate approaching $400 million.

Every dollar of this amount is disbursed in physical currency. No check has ever been written to a child by the Tooth Fairy. No wire transfer has ever been initiated. No Venmo, Zelle, PayPal, or other electronic payment platform has ever processed a Tooth Fairy transaction. The operation is, by every available measure, the largest all-cash disbursement enterprise in the United States that is not a branch of the federal government.

The estimated aggregate annual disbursement is $359.7 million. Every dollar is disbursed in physical currency. No check has ever been written. No wire transfer has ever been initiated. The operation is the largest all-cash enterprise in the United States that is not a branch of the federal government.

IV. The Structuring Analysis

Under 31 U.S.C. § 5324, structuring occurs when a person breaks a single sum of currency exceeding $10,000 into smaller sums, or conducts a series of transactions at or below $10,000, for the purpose of evading the BSA’s reporting requirements. The Supreme Court addressed the knowledge element in Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135 (1994), holding that the government must prove the defendant knew that structuring was unlawful, not merely that the defendant intended to break up the transactions.15 Congress subsequently amended the statute to lower this mens rea threshold.

The Tooth Fairy’s transaction pattern is, by any forensic accounting standard, a textbook case of structured disbursement. The entity’s total annual cash outflow is approximately $359.7 million. Rather than disbursing this sum in a single transaction, or in a small number of large transactions, the entity breaks it into 60.9 million individual payments averaging $5.84 each. Not a single one of these transactions reaches the $10,000 CTR threshold. Not a single one reaches even $10. The ratio of average transaction size to the reporting threshold is 0.000584-to-1. If a FinCEN analyst were presented with this ratio without context, the structuring determination would take approximately four seconds.

The pattern is further aggravated by the consistency of the transaction amounts. The Delta Dental data shows a remarkably narrow distribution, with regional averages ranging from $5.27 (Midwest) to $6.45 (Northeast).16 This uniformity is, in the parlance of anti-money-laundering compliance, a “red flag indicator” suggesting centralized control over disbursement amounts. A single entity sending cash payments of uniform size to millions of recipients is a pattern that FinCEN has specifically identified as suspicious in its advisory guidance.17

The question under Ratzlaf is whether the Tooth Fairy knew that structuring was unlawful. The BSA was enacted in 1970. The Tooth Fairy has been operating since at least 1908. For 62 years, its small-batch cash distribution model predated the reporting requirement and was therefore not structuring. But since 1970, the entity has continued the same pattern without modification, despite the enactment of comprehensive anti-money-laundering legislation that specifically targets the conduct in which it has engaged for more than half a century. The failure to adjust operations in response to 56 years of federal financial regulation is either an extraordinary coincidence or evidence of willful blindness. FinCEN does not generally credit the former.

V. The Suspicious Activity Indicators

FinCEN requires financial institutions to file Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) when they detect transactions that “involve funds derived from illegal activity,” are “designed to evade any requirements of the Bank Secrecy Act,” or “have no business or apparent lawful purpose.”18 In fiscal year 2024, financial institutions filed 4.7 million SARs, for an average of 12,870 per day.19

The Tooth Fairy’s operations exhibit at least nine of FinCEN’s published suspicious activity indicators:

1. Cash-intensive business with no apparent legitimate source of funds. The Tooth Fairy disburses approximately $359.7 million in cash annually. It has no known revenue stream, no known employer, no known investment portfolio, and no known inheritance. The source of its cash holdings has never been identified. A private entity distributing hundreds of millions in cash without a documented funding source is, under FinCEN guidance, presumptively suspicious.20

2. Transactions conducted at unusual hours. Every known Tooth Fairy transaction occurs between approximately 9:00 PM and 6:00 AM local time. FinCEN has specifically noted that transactions conducted outside normal business hours may indicate suspicious activity.21 The Tooth Fairy has never conducted a transaction during daylight hours. Its entire business model depends on the transaction counterparty being unconscious.

3. Transactions inconsistent with the customer’s known business or profile. The recipients of Tooth Fairy payments are children between the ages of 6 and 13. Children in this age group do not typically receive large cash disbursements from unidentified entities operating under cover of darkness. Any bank processing a deposit of Tooth Fairy proceeds would be required to assess whether the transaction is consistent with the depositor’s customer profile. It is not.

4. Unwillingness to provide identification or other documentation. The Tooth Fairy has never presented a government-issued identification document to any party. It has never provided a Social Security number, an Employer Identification Number, or a foreign passport number. It has not completed a W-9. It has not been verified through any Customer Identification Program (CIP) as required by 31 CFR § 1020.220.22

5. Use of multiple transactions to avoid reporting thresholds. Addressed in Section IV above. The ratio of 60.9 million transactions to what could be accomplished in approximately 35,970 transactions of $10,000 each speaks for itself.

6. Third-party intermediaries conducting transactions on behalf of others. In many households, one parent acts as the Tooth Fairy’s agent, withdrawing cash from a bank account and placing it under the child’s pillow. These parents are, under the BSA’s framework, unregistered agents of a money transmission enterprise, facilitating cash payments on behalf of a principal whose identity they have never verified. They are, in effect, 21.3 million unlicensed sub-agents of a money services business that is itself unlicensed.

7. Transactions involving the exchange of goods of indeterminate value. The Tooth Fairy receives a human deciduous tooth in exchange for each cash payment. Deciduous teeth have no established fair market value. They are not traded on any commodity exchange. They are not listed in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule. The IRS has published no guidance on the tax treatment of deciduous tooth proceeds. The exchange of cash for goods of no recognized market value is a classic indicator of potential money laundering.23

8. Lack of a business nexus to the geographic areas served. The Tooth Fairy operates in all 50 states and the District of Columbia but maintains no registered office, warehouse, distribution center, or mailing address in any of them. No state secretary of state has ever issued a certificate of formation, authority, or registration to any entity operating under the name “Tooth Fairy” or any known DBA thereof.

9. Patterns of activity with no apparent economic rationale. The Tooth Fairy pays cash for a biological byproduct that the recipient was going to lose regardless. No rational economic actor pays for goods that the counterparty cannot retain. The teeth are not resold. They are not processed into a secondary product. They are not displayed, cataloged, or otherwise used in any known commercial enterprise. The economic purpose of the transaction is, by every metric available to financial investigators, nonexistent.

A private entity distributing hundreds of millions in cash without a documented funding source is, under FinCEN guidance, presumptively suspicious. The Tooth Fairy has never presented a government-issued identification document to any party.

VI. The Know Your Customer Failure

The USA PATRIOT Act of 2001 (Pub. L. 107-56, § 326) required the Secretary of the Treasury to prescribe regulations setting forth minimum standards for financial institutions to verify the identity of customers opening accounts. The resulting Customer Identification Program (CIP) rule, codified at 31 CFR § 1020.220, requires financial institutions to obtain, at minimum, the customer’s name, date of birth, address, and an identification number (Social Security number for U.S. persons; passport or other government-issued document for non-U.S. persons).24

The Tooth Fairy has not been subjected to any KYC process by any party at any point in its 118-year operating history. Its name is a colloquial designation, not a legal name. Its date of birth is unknown. Its address is variously reported as “Fairyland,” “the sky,” and “under your pillow,” none of which are recognized by the United States Postal Service or any address verification database.25 It possesses no Social Security number, no Individual Taxpayer Identification Number, and no passport from any recognized sovereign nation.

More troubling from a compliance perspective is the KYC failure on the recipient side. The Tooth Fairy’s customers are minors between the ages of 6 and 13. Under the CIP rule, financial institutions must collect a date of birth for every customer. The Tooth Fairy’s intermediaries (parents) do, in fairness, know the child’s date of birth. But no CIP record has ever been created. No beneficial ownership form has been filed. No Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) has been performed on the estimated 3.04 million new customers onboarded annually, despite the fact that the entire customer base is composed of minors, a category that FinCEN has identified as requiring heightened scrutiny when used as conduits for financial activity.26

The bilateral KYC failure is complete. Neither the payor nor the payee has been identified under any framework recognized by federal financial regulation. The transaction exists in a documentary void. It produces no CTR, no SAR, no Form 8300, no 1099, no W-2, no K-1, and no evidence whatsoever that it occurred, apart from a small sum of cash and the absence of a tooth.

VII. The Money Services Business Registration Problem

Under 31 U.S.C. § 5330 and 31 CFR § 1022.380, any person doing business in the United States as a “money services business” (MSB) must register with FinCEN.27 The regulations define MSBs to include, among other categories, “money transmitters.” A money transmitter is any person who provides “money transmission services,” defined as “the acceptance of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency from one person and the transmission of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency to another person or location, by any means.”28

The Tooth Fairy accepts value (a deciduous tooth, which the child and the child’s parents clearly value, as evidenced by the ritual surrounding its loss) from one person (the child) and transmits currency (U.S. legal tender) to the same person. The fact that the transmission occurs in the reverse direction from typical money transmission services does not exempt it. FinCEN has consistently held that the form of the transaction is less important than its substance.29

As an entity that transmits currency to persons in all 50 states and the District of Columbia, the Tooth Fairy meets the definition of a money transmitter under 31 CFR § 1010.100(ff)(5). It is required to register with FinCEN, implement an anti-money-laundering (AML) program, file SARs for suspicious transactions, maintain transaction records, and comply with the Funds Transfer Rule and the Funds Travel Rule. It has done none of these things.

The failure to register as an MSB is itself a federal crime under 18 U.S.C. § 1960, which imposes penalties of up to five years’ imprisonment for operating an unlicensed money transmitting business.30 The Tooth Fairy has been operating an unlicensed money transmitting business for 56 years (counting from the BSA’s enactment in 1970). If each year of operation constitutes a separate violation, the aggregate potential incarceration period is 280 years, which would represent the longest federal sentence ever imposed on a mythological entity, though not the longest ever proposed.

VIII. The Source of Funds Problem

Every AML compliance program begins with a fundamental question: where does the money come from? The Tooth Fairy’s source of funds has never been identified.

The entity disburses approximately $359.7 million in U.S. currency annually. This currency must originate somewhere. It is not printed by the Tooth Fairy; the Bureau of Engraving and Printing has confirmed no special currency orders from any non-governmental entity matching the Tooth Fairy’s disbursement profile.31 It is not counterfeited; the United States Secret Service, which investigates currency counterfeiting under 18 U.S.C. § 485, has never seized Tooth Fairy currency or identified it as fraudulent.32

The practical reality, which does not improve the compliance picture, is that the cash originates from the bank accounts of the parents who serve as the Tooth Fairy’s agents. These parents withdraw cash from ATMs, bank branches, or cash-back retail transactions and redistribute it under the Tooth Fairy’s name. This is, from a BSA perspective, a massive agency-based cash distribution network operating without any of the controls, documentation, or reporting that federal law requires of such networks.

Consider the aggregate cash flow. Approximately 21.3 million households withdraw cash for Tooth Fairy purposes each year. The average withdrawal is $16.70 per child per year (2.86 teeth × $5.84 per tooth). This is a modest sum per household, but in aggregate, it represents $359.7 million in cash migrating from the regulated financial system into a completely undocumented parallel distribution channel. The cash enters a child’s possession under a false attribution (the Tooth Fairy, rather than the parent), with no paper trail connecting the withdrawal to the disbursement. From a financial intelligence perspective, this is a gap in the BSA’s coverage large enough to fly a reindeer through, and that particular airspace violation is already the subject of a separate Ergo investigation.

IX. The Tax Implications

Internal Revenue Code § 61(a) defines gross income as “all income from whatever source derived,” including “compensation for services.”33 The Supreme Court affirmed in Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co., 348 U.S. 426 (1955), that gross income includes any “accession to wealth, clearly realized, and over which the taxpayer has complete dominion.”34

A child who receives $5.84 in cash under a pillow has experienced an accession to wealth. The cash is clearly realized. The child has complete dominion over it upon waking. The payment is not a gift from a family member in the conventional sense; it is presented as payment from an independent third-party entity in exchange for a biological product (the tooth). Under the Glenshaw Glass framework, this is taxable income.

If the transaction is properly characterized as a sale of property (the tooth) rather than a service or gift, the child may be entitled to a basis in the tooth, reducing the taxable gain. The child’s basis in a deciduous tooth is, however, zero. The tooth was produced by the child’s body at no cost, over a developmental period of approximately two to three years, using calcium, phosphorus, and fluoride obtained through the child’s diet.35 The child did not purchase the tooth. The child did not commission its manufacture. The child’s body produced it involuntarily, in the same way that a tree produces fruit. Under IRC § 1001, the amount realized ($5.84) minus the adjusted basis ($0.00) yields a gain of $5.84 per tooth.

At 60.9 million transactions per year and $5.84 per transaction, the total unreported gross income attributable to Tooth Fairy proceeds is approximately $355.7 million annually. The IRS has not issued guidance on whether children should file Schedule C (Profit or Loss from Business) or Schedule D (Capital Gains and Losses) for tooth sale proceeds. No child has ever filed either. The resulting tax gap, while modest in per-capita terms, is structurally persistent and has been accruing since the institution of the modern federal income tax in 1913.

X. The Enforcement Precedent Problem

FinCEN and the Department of Justice have demonstrated willingness to prosecute structuring at scales far smaller than the Tooth Fairy’s operations. In United States v. Ratzlaf, the defendant structured $100,000 in cash transactions at a casino to avoid CTR requirements. The amount involved was 0.028 percent of the Tooth Fairy’s estimated annual disbursement. The government considered this sum sufficient to warrant prosecution to the Supreme Court of the United States.36

In 2015, the IRS Criminal Investigation division came under Congressional scrutiny for seizing the bank accounts of small business owners under civil asset forfeiture provisions targeting structuring, in cases where the account holders had made a series of deposits just below $10,000. The Institute for Justice documented cases involving small businesses whose total structured deposits were as low as $33,000.37 The Tooth Fairy’s annual disbursement is approximately 10,900 times this amount.

In 2022, FinCEN imposed a $140 million civil money penalty on USAA Federal Savings Bank for failures in its AML program, including inadequate suspicious activity monitoring and reporting.38 The bank’s failures involved, among other things, an insufficient number of AML compliance staff relative to the volume of transactions it processed. The Tooth Fairy employs zero AML compliance staff for $359.7 million in annual cash transactions. Its compliance infrastructure is not inadequate. It is absent.

The Department of Justice’s prosecution guidelines for structuring cases, set forth in the Justice Manual at § 9-108.200, instruct prosecutors to consider the amount of currency involved, the number of transactions, and the sophistication of the structuring pattern.39 By every criterion in these guidelines, the Tooth Fairy’s operations exceed the thresholds that have triggered federal prosecution in prior cases. The amount is in the hundreds of millions. The number of transactions is in the tens of millions. The pattern has been sustained, without variation, for 56 years. The sophistication is, admittedly, low. The entity places cash under a pillow. But the DOJ guidelines do not contain a de minimis exception for operational simplicity, and a pillow is, after all, just an unusually soft safe deposit box.

XI. The Penalty Exposure

The Tooth Fairy’s regulatory exposure, calculated across the full range of applicable statutes, is difficult to state without appearing to engage in hyperbole. The figures are presented here without editorialization.

Structuring (31 U.S.C. § 5324): Each structuring violation carries a potential criminal penalty of up to $250,000 in fines and up to five years’ imprisonment. If each of the 60.9 million annual transactions constitutes a separate violation, the annual fine exposure is $15.2 trillion and the incarceration exposure is 304.5 million years.40

Failure to register as an MSB (18 U.S.C. § 1960): Up to $250,000 and five years per violation. If each year of unregistered operation since 1970 is a separate violation, the penalty is $14 million and 280 years.

SAR filing failure (31 U.S.C. § 5322): Willful violations carry penalties of up to $250,000 and five years. The Tooth Fairy has never filed a SAR despite 56 years of operations exhibiting multiple FinCEN-identified suspicious activity indicators.

Civil money penalties (31 U.S.C. § 5321): FinCEN may impose civil penalties of up to $100,000 per violation for negligent BSA violations and up to $100,000 per day for willful violations. At 60.9 million violations per year and $100,000 each, the annual civil penalty exposure is $6.09 trillion.

Tax liability (IRC § 6651 et seq.): The aggregate unreported income of $355.7 million per year, even at a zero effective tax rate for most minor recipients (due to the standard deduction), generates filing penalties for the estimated 2.1 million children whose annual Tooth Fairy income, combined with other sources, might require a return.

The combined annual penalty exposure, summing criminal fines and civil penalties across all applicable statutes, exceeds $21 trillion. This is approximately 78 percent of the current U.S. national debt.41 It is approximately 12,000 times the amount the Tooth Fairy actually disburses. The regulatory cost of the enterprise, if enforced, would exceed its operating budget by four orders of magnitude. No AML compliance program in history has been designed to address a penalty-to-revenue ratio of this magnitude. No AML compliance program in history has needed to, because no other entity has operated at this scale, for this long, with this level of noncompliance.

The combined annual penalty exposure exceeds $21 trillion. This is approximately 78 percent of the current U.S. national debt and approximately 12,000 times the amount the Tooth Fairy actually disburses.

XII. Conclusion

The Bank Secrecy Act was enacted to ensure that large-scale cash operations in the United States produce a documentary trail sufficient for law enforcement purposes. The Tooth Fairy operates the largest cash-only disbursement enterprise in the country that is not a government agency. It distributes approximately $359.7 million per year across 60.9 million individual transactions, every one of them below the reporting threshold, every one of them in physical currency, every one of them conducted between the hours of 9 PM and 6 AM, every one of them involving a minor who is unconscious at the time of the exchange.

The entity has never registered with FinCEN. It has never filed a CTR, a SAR, or a Form 8300. It has never submitted to a KYC review. It has never identified its source of funds. It has never appointed a compliance officer. It has never implemented an AML program. It has been operating in this manner for 56 years since the BSA’s enactment and for 118 years in total.

Its transaction pattern exhibits nine of FinCEN’s published suspicious activity indicators. Its structuring ratio is 0.000584-to-1 against the reporting threshold. Its penalty exposure exceeds $21 trillion annually. Its compliance infrastructure consists of a pillow.

Financial institutions filed 4.7 million Suspicious Activity Reports in fiscal year 2024. Not one of them concerned the Tooth Fairy. FinCEN employs approximately 300 staff to combat financial crime in the United States.42 None of them are assigned to this case. The Department of Justice has prosecuted individuals for structuring amounts as small as $33,000. The Tooth Fairy structures $359.7 million.

The operation continues tonight. Approximately 166,800 children will lose a tooth today. By morning, approximately 138,400 of them will find cash under their pillows, deposited by an unregistered, unregulated, unidentified entity operating without oversight, without documentation, and without the slightest concern for the comprehensive regulatory framework that Congress spent half a century constructing to prevent precisely this kind of activity.
