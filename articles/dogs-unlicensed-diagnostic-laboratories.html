Medical Device Regulatory Law & Canine Olfactory Diagnostics

The FDA’s Own Definition of “Device” Classifies Every Domestic Dog in the United States as an Uncleared Medical Diagnostic Laboratory Operating in Continuous Violation of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, and 21 CFR Part 820’s Quality System Regulation, Despite Peer-Reviewed Diagnostic Accuracy That Exceeds Multiple FDA-Cleared Instruments in at Least Six Cancer Types, SARS-CoV-2, and Hypoglycemia, and the Agency Has Not Issued a Single 510(k) Clearance, Warning Letter, or Establishment Inspection Across 87.3 Million Unregistered Units in Continuous Diagnostic Operation

Under 21 U.S.C. § 321(h)(1), a “device” is any “instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, contrivance, implant, in vitro reagent, or other similar or related article” that is “intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions.” The domestic dog possesses between 125 million and 300 million olfactory receptors, an olfactory bulb approximately forty times larger than that of a human, and the demonstrated ability to detect volatile organic compound signatures associated with lung cancer, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, melanoma, SARS-CoV-2, and hypoglycemia at accuracies that rival or exceed the FDA-cleared diagnostic instruments they have never been compared against in a regulatory submission. There are 87.3 million pet dogs in the United States. Not one holds a 510(k) clearance. Not one has been assigned a Unique Device Identifier. Not one operates under a CLIA certificate. The FDA Center for Devices and Radiological Health has not conducted a single establishment inspection. The compliance rate is zero.

By Victoria Pemberton · June 28, 2026 · 16 min read

I. The Statutory Definition

In 1976, Congress passed the Medical Device Amendments to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, establishing for the first time a comprehensive framework for the regulation of medical devices in the United States. The definitional foundation of this framework is found at 21 U.S.C. § 321(h)(1), which defines “device” in language that has governed every subsequent regulatory action in American medical device law.1

The definition reads: “an instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, contrivance, implant, in vitro reagent, or other similar or related article, including any component, part, or accessory, which is—(A) recognized in the official National Formulary, or the United States Pharmacopeia, or any supplement to them, (B) intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions, or in the cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, in man or other animals, or (C) intended to affect the structure or any function of the body of man or other animals, and which does not achieve its primary intended purposes through chemical action within or on the body of man or other animals and which is not dependent upon being metabolized for the achievement of its primary intended purposes.”

This is a functional test. It asks not what the article is made of, nor who manufactured it, nor whether it plugs into a wall. It asks what the article does. The word “contrivance” is the broadest term in the enumeration. Black’s Law Dictionary defines it as “a device or mechanism; something contrived for a particular purpose.” The domestic dog, Canis lupus familiaris, was contrived for particular purposes beginning approximately 15,000 years ago through the deliberate human manipulation of wolf populations, a process of artificial selection that has produced over 400 recognized breeds optimized for specific functional roles.2 Many of these roles involve the detection of substances invisible to human perception. The contrivance was designed. The diagnostic function emerged. Congress did not exempt it.

The statute further specifies that the article need only be “intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions.” Trained medical detection dogs are intended, by their trainers, by the organizations that certify them, and by the physicians who refer patients to them, for precisely this purpose. The intent is documented in peer-reviewed literature, in nonprofit mission statements, in training curricula, and in clinical trial protocols registered with the National Institutes of Health. The dogs themselves may not possess intent, but neither does a pulse oximeter.

II. The Sensor Specifications

A diagnostic device is only as useful as its sensor. The canine olfactory system is, by any engineering measure, the most sensitive volatile organic compound detection instrument in the known biological world.

The domestic dog possesses between 125 million and 300 million olfactory receptor neurons, depending on breed, compared to approximately 5 to 6 million in humans.3 The bloodhound sits at the upper end of this range, with approximately 300 million receptors spread across an olfactory epithelium the size of a pocket handkerchief. The human olfactory epithelium is approximately the size of a postage stamp. The portion of the canine brain devoted to analyzing olfactory information is approximately forty times larger, proportionally, than the corresponding region in the human brain.4

The practical result of this hardware is detection sensitivity at concentrations of approximately one part per trillion. The commonly cited analogy, offered by the Auburn University College of Veterinary Medicine, is that a dog can detect the equivalent of a single drop of liquid in twenty Olympic-size swimming pools.4 No commercially available electronic nose, ion mobility spectrometer, or gas chromatography-mass spectrometry system achieves this detection threshold in real-time field deployment without sample preparation, reagent consumption, or an electrical outlet.

The dog achieves it while also wagging its tail.

The domestic dog possesses up to 300 million olfactory receptors and can detect volatile compounds at one part per trillion. No FDA-cleared diagnostic instrument achieves this sensitivity in real-time field deployment. The device also fetches.

The canine nose further incorporates a design feature that no commercial instrument has replicated. When a dog inhales, a fold of tissue inside the nostril separates the incoming airstream into two pathways: one for respiration and one for olfaction. When the dog exhales, spent air exits through lateral slits in the nose rather than back through the nostrils, creating a swirling airflow pattern that draws new odorant molecules into the olfactory chamber without interrupting the sampling cycle.3 The result is near-continuous analyte acquisition. A gas chromatograph requires minutes to hours. A dog requires seconds.

III. The Cancer Detection Evidence

The peer-reviewed clinical evidence for canine cancer detection begins in 1989, when Williams and Pembroke published a case report in The Lancet describing a dog that persistently sniffed a pigmented skin lesion on its owner’s leg, which was subsequently diagnosed as malignant melanoma.5 This was a single case. It was published in the most cited medical journal in the world. The FDA did not open a file.

The evidentiary base has since expanded considerably. In 2004, Willis and colleagues at Amersham Hospital published in the BMJ the first controlled, blinded study demonstrating that trained dogs could detect bladder cancer in urine samples at a success rate significantly above chance.6 The sensitivity was 41 percent, modest by diagnostic standards. The study established the proof of principle. The FDA did not respond.

In 2006, McCulloch and colleagues published a study in Integrative Cancer Therapies that fundamentally altered the evidentiary landscape. Using five trained household dogs, the researchers demonstrated that canine scent detection could identify lung cancer in exhaled breath samples with an overall sensitivity of 99 percent (95% CI, 0.99–1.00) and specificity of 99 percent (95% CI, 0.96–1.00). For breast cancer, sensitivity was 88 percent (95% CI, 0.75–1.00) and specificity was 98 percent (95% CI, 0.90–0.99).7 To place this in regulatory context: the FDA-cleared Cologuard colorectal cancer screening test, at the time of its 2014 premarket approval, demonstrated a sensitivity of 92 percent for colorectal cancer. Five pet dogs, without a 510(k), achieved 99 percent for lung cancer using only exhaled air.

Subsequent studies extended the findings across cancer types. Horvath and colleagues reported in 2008 that a trained dog detected ovarian carcinoma in tissue specimens with 100 percent sensitivity and 98 percent specificity, and in 2010 confirmed 100 percent sensitivity in blood serum samples.8 Cornu and colleagues demonstrated in 2011 that a Belgian Malinois could detect prostate cancer in urine with 91 percent sensitivity and 91 percent specificity.9 Sonoda and colleagues reported the same year that a trained Labrador Retriever identified colorectal cancer from exhaled breath with 91 percent sensitivity and 99 percent specificity, and from stool samples with 97 percent sensitivity and 99 percent specificity, with accuracy maintained even in early-stage disease.10

The aggregate literature, compiled in a systematic review by Boedeker and colleagues, encompasses more than two dozen prospective, blinded studies across at least nine cancer types, with sensitivities ranging from 41 percent at the lowest to 100 percent at the highest, and specificities routinely exceeding 90 percent. The evidence base is not anecdotal. It is not preliminary. It is a body of peer-reviewed clinical data that, were it submitted to the FDA’s Center for Devices and Radiological Health on behalf of any other diagnostic instrument, would constitute a substantial premarket notification.

No submission has been filed.

IV. The Infectious Disease Portfolio

The dog’s diagnostic capabilities are not limited to oncology. During the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, researchers at the University of Helsinki demonstrated that trained scent dogs could detect COVID-19 infections from skin swab samples with clinical accuracy comparable to RT-PCR, the gold-standard diagnostic test.

In a randomized, triple-blinded validation trial conducted at Helsinki-Vantaa International Airport between September 2020 and April 2021, four trained dogs—three Labrador Retrievers and one White Shepherd—were presented with 420 parallel samples from 114 PCR-positive and 306 PCR-negative individuals. The dogs identified positive samples with a combined sensitivity of 92 percent and specificity of 91 percent, with only minor variation among individual dogs (best: 93% sensitivity, 95% specificity; lowest: 88% sensitivity, 90% specificity).11

In the real-life phase of the study, the dogs screened skin swabs from 303 incoming passengers. PCR and canine results agreed in 296 of 303 cases—a concordance rate of 98 percent. The dogs processed each sample in seconds. The PCR test required hours. The dogs did not require reagents, a laboratory, or an electrical outlet. They required treats.

At Helsinki-Vantaa International Airport, four dogs screened arriving passengers for SARS-CoV-2 with 98 percent concordance with RT-PCR. The test took seconds. The dogs received treats. The FDA received nothing.

A subsequent meta-analysis by Dickey and Junqueira, reviewing 29 studies of canine COVID-19 detection, found that in six real-world field deployments—conducted at airports, pharmacies, concerts, a metro system, and a K-12 school—trained dogs achieved sensitivities between 68.6 and 95.9 percent, with three of the six exceeding 92 percent, and specificities between 75.0 and 99.9 percent.12 The researchers concluded, in language not typically found in a systematic review: “We believe that scent dogs deserve their place as a serious diagnostic methodology.”

The FDA, which issues Emergency Use Authorizations for novel diagnostic instruments during declared public health emergencies, issued no EUA for any dog during the COVID-19 pandemic. Approximately 523 EUAs were issued for in vitro diagnostic tests between February 2020 and May 2023. Authorized instruments included nasal swabs, saliva tests, and at-home antigen kits. Not one was a Labrador Retriever.

V. The Glycemic Monitoring Evidence

In the management of Type 1 diabetes, hypoglycemic episodes represent a potentially fatal complication that current technology detects imperfectly. Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs), which are FDA-cleared Class II medical devices, measure interstitial glucose with a physiological lag of approximately 5 to 15 minutes behind blood glucose. Diabetes alert dogs (DADs) detect hypoglycemia through volatile organic compound changes in the owner’s breath or perspiration.

Rooney, Morant, and Guest published a 2013 study in PLOS ONE evaluating seventeen clients who had been placed with trained glycemia alert dogs. Owner-recorded data showed that the dogs alerted with significant accuracy during episodes of both low and high blood sugar. Eight of ten dogs for which adequate records were available responded consistently more often when blood sugar was outside target range. Seven of nine clients recorded a significantly higher proportion of routine tests within target range after obtaining the dog. All seventeen clients reported reduced paramedic call-outs, decreased unconscious episodes, and improved independence.13

A separate 2016 in vitro study published in Diabetes Therapy confirmed that trained DADs demonstrated statistically significant sensitivity to hypoglycemic samples, ranging from 50 to 87.5 percent per dog, against a random expected alert rate of 14 percent, with specificities from 89.6 to 97.9 percent.14

The Dexcom G7 continuous glucose monitor, a Class II device cleared under 510(k), has a published MARD (mean absolute relative difference) of approximately 8.2 percent. A trained dog achieves its diagnostic output without a sensor wire inserted under the skin, without a transmitter, without a smartphone application, and without a prescription. The dog is, from the standpoint of the user experience, a zero-insertion, wireless, ambient glucose monitoring system that also provides emotional support. The FDA has cleared the Dexcom. It has not cleared the dog.

VI. The Device Classification Problem

Under the FDA’s risk-based classification system, established by the 1976 Medical Device Amendments, all medical devices are assigned to one of three regulatory classes.15 Class I devices (tongue depressors, bandages, stethoscopes) are subject to general controls. Class II devices (blood glucose monitors, pregnancy tests, powered wheelchairs) require a 510(k) premarket notification demonstrating substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate device. Class III devices (implantable pacemakers, replacement heart valves) require a premarket approval application with clinical trial data.

A diagnostic device that detects cancer in exhaled breath with 99 percent sensitivity and 99 percent specificity would, under any reasonable classification analysis, be a Class II or Class III device. It would require, at minimum, a 510(k) submission demonstrating substantial equivalence to an existing predicate device. The submission would need to include analytical performance data, clinical validation data, labeling, and a description of the device’s operating principles.

There is no predicate device for a dog. No substantially equivalent article has been cleared. The domestic dog is, in regulatory terminology, a “novel device” that would likely require either a De Novo classification request under Section 513(f)(2) of the FD&C Act, or a full premarket approval application. Between 2012 and 2021, the FDA granted 30,263 clearances for 510(k) devices and 256 De Novo approvals.16 None was for Canis lupus familiaris. None was for any organism.

The regulatory vacuum is notable. The FDA has cleared AI-powered software algorithms that analyze medical images to detect cancer. It has cleared electronic nose devices that detect volatile organic compounds in breath. It has not cleared the biological system that does both simultaneously, at higher sensitivity, while also being potty-trained.

VII. The CLIA Catastrophe

The Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 263a, require that any laboratory performing testing on human specimens for the purpose of diagnosis, prevention, or treatment of disease must hold a valid CLIA certificate.17 The statute applies to approximately 320,000 clinical laboratories in the United States. It applies wherever a “test” is performed on a “specimen” of human origin.

A dog that sniffs a breath sample to detect lung cancer is performing a test on a specimen of human origin. A dog that sniffs a urine sample to detect prostate cancer is performing a test on a specimen of human origin. A dog that sniffs a skin swab to detect SARS-CoV-2 is performing a test on a specimen of human origin. The analytical method—volatile organic compound pattern recognition via biological olfactory receptor activation—is unconventional but functionally equivalent to an electronic nose, which is itself subject to CLIA when used for clinical diagnosis.

CLIA specifies three levels of testing complexity: waived, moderate complexity, and high complexity. Tests that are simple, have an insignificant risk of erroneous results, and have been cleared by the FDA for home use may receive a Certificate of Waiver. All other tests require a Certificate of Registration, a Certificate of Compliance, or a Certificate of Accreditation, plus biennial proficiency testing and periodic inspections by CMS or an approved accrediting organization.

No dog in the United States holds a CLIA certificate of any type. No dog has been enrolled in an approved proficiency testing program. No dog-operated laboratory has been inspected by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. The homes in which these diagnostic operations occur—56.3 million of them, according to the 2025 AVMA Pet Ownership and Demographics Sourcebook—are unregistered testing facilities.18

VIII. The Quality System Catastrophe

Under 21 CFR Part 820, every manufacturer of a finished medical device intended for commercial distribution in the United States must establish and maintain a Quality System that includes documented procedures for design controls, production and process controls, corrective and preventive actions, complaint handling, device labeling, and records management.19 The Quality System Regulation is the FDA’s primary enforcement mechanism for ensuring that medical devices are consistently manufactured to meet applicable requirements and specifications.

The domestic dog is manufactured through sexual reproduction, a process characterized by stochastic recombination of approximately 2.8 billion base pairs of DNA across 39 pairs of chromosomes. No two production runs are identical. There is no master device record. There is no device history record. The design input was “wolf, but friendlier.” The design output has been diverging from specification for 15,000 years.2

The manufacturing environment does not conform to any recognized clean room standard. Production facilities include backyards, living rooms, veterinary clinics, and, in approximately 4.9 percent of cases, the open road.18 Process validation is performed, at best, through breed standard evaluation by the American Kennel Club, an organization that holds no FDA registration and whose quality criteria include coat texture, ear set, and “expression.”

The design input was “wolf, but friendlier.” The design output has been diverging from specification for 15,000 years. No master device record exists.

Corrective and preventive actions are, in practice, limited to “bad dog” and the withholding of treats. No complaint file is maintained. No medical device report has ever been filed under 21 CFR Part 803 for a false-positive cancer detection by any dog. The adverse event reporting system that applies to every cleared diagnostic device in the United States does not receive reports from any canine diagnostic operator, not because adverse events do not occur, but because the operator is not aware that it is a medical device.

IX. The Unique Device Identifier Gap

Under the FDA’s Unique Device Identification System, established pursuant to Section 519(f) of the FD&C Act, every medical device distributed in the United States must bear a Unique Device Identifier (UDI) on its label and packaging. The UDI consists of a device identifier, linking to the Global Unique Device Identification Database (GUDID), and a production identifier including lot number, serial number, and expiration date.20

Dogs do have identification systems. The American Kennel Club maintains a registry of approximately 63 million dogs. Microchip databases maintained by the American Animal Hospital Association store unique fifteen-digit ISO-compliant identification numbers for implanted transponders. The AKC registration number and the microchip number are, functionally, a two-part identifier linking a unique unit to a production record (breed, sire, dam, date of manufacture). They are, in every respect except FDA recognition, a Unique Device Identifier.

None of these identifiers appears in the GUDID. None has been submitted to the FDA. The agency that requires a UDI on a $2 tongue depressor has no identifying information on file for a 70-pound cancer-detection platform with a documented sensitivity exceeding that of a colonoscopy.

X. The 510(k) Paradox

The 510(k) premarket notification pathway requires a manufacturer to demonstrate that a new device is “substantially equivalent” to a legally marketed predicate device in terms of intended use and technological characteristics. Substantial equivalence does not require the new device to be identical to the predicate. It requires that any differences do not raise new questions of safety or effectiveness.21

The paradox is this: no predicate exists for a living diagnostic organism. The closest analogues in the FDA’s cleared device database are electronic nose systems, which detect volatile organic compound patterns using metal oxide semiconductor sensor arrays. These devices operate on the same fundamental principle as the canine nose—pattern recognition of complex volatile mixtures—but with approximately five orders of magnitude less sensitivity and no capacity for ambulatory deployment, emotional bonding, or unconditional love.

If an electronic nose were submitted as a predicate, the dog would need to demonstrate that its biological sensor array does not raise new questions of safety or effectiveness compared to the electronic version. This presents a conceptual difficulty. The dog’s sensor array is demonstrably more effective. The dog’s safety profile includes occasional biting, which would require a risk analysis and possibly a contraindication in the labeling. The labeling would also need to include, per 21 CFR § 801.1, adequate directions for use. Adequate directions for a dog are a matter of ongoing philosophical debate.

XI. The Population-Scale Enforcement Gap

According to the 2025 AVMA Pet Ownership and Demographics Sourcebook, based on surveys conducted in early 2025, there are approximately 87.3 million pet dogs in the United States, residing in 56.3 million households—42.6 percent of all American households.18 Approximately 88.8 percent of dog-owning households report viewing their pet as a member of the family.

Not all 87.3 million dogs are trained diagnostic instruments. But all 87.3 million possess the biological sensor array. The olfactory hardware is standard equipment across all breeds, from the Chihuahua (with its reduced but still formidable receptor population) to the Bloodhound (with its 300 million receptors and its 230 square centimeters of olfactory epithelium). Every dog in America is, at a minimum, an uncalibrated diagnostic instrument with the theoretical capability to detect volatile organic compound signatures associated with disease states.

The FDA’s Center for Devices and Radiological Health employs approximately 1,800 staff and conducts approximately 2,500 domestic device establishment inspections per year. At this rate, inspecting the 56.3 million establishments (households) in which unregistered diagnostic devices (dogs) are manufactured and deployed would require approximately 22,520 years. The device has been in domestic operation for approximately 15,000 years. The agency is, by its own inspection capacity, already behind.

The compliance rate across all 87.3 million units is zero. It has been zero since Canis lupus was first recruited from the Pleistocene wild to perform detection services for Homo sapiens. The enforcement gap is not a rounding error. It is the entire regulatory apparatus for medical devices, subtracted from the entire domestic dog population, yielding a remainder indistinguishable from the population itself.

XII. Conclusion

The evidence admits no ambiguity. Under 21 U.S.C. § 321(h)(1), a “device” is any “contrivance” “intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions.” Trained dogs diagnose disease. They do so with documented sensitivity and specificity that rival or exceed those of FDA-cleared instruments in multiple disease categories. They diagnose lung cancer in exhaled breath with 99 percent sensitivity. They identify ovarian carcinoma in blood serum with 100 percent sensitivity. They screen airline passengers for SARS-CoV-2 with 92 percent accuracy in seconds. They alert diabetic owners to hypoglycemia before their continuous glucose monitors do.

They are contrivances. They were contrived by human beings, through 15,000 years of artificial selection, for the express purpose of detecting what humans cannot detect. The diagnostic application is among the oldest and most intentional functions of the human-canine partnership. The device was designed before the concept of a medical device existed.

The FDA requires a 510(k) submission for a digital thermometer. It requires a De Novo classification request for a novel AI algorithm that reads a chest X-ray. It requires nothing for a biological system containing 300 million olfactory receptors, a dedicated neural processing array forty times larger than the human equivalent, and a demonstrated capacity to detect cancer at one part per trillion—provided the system also has four legs and an opinion about squirrels.

There are 87.3 million of them. They are in 42.6 percent of American households. They have been performing uncleared, unlicensed, uninspected diagnostic operations for fifteen millennia. The FDA has not issued a single 510(k) clearance. It has not opened a single inspection file. It has not sent a single warning letter. It has not convened a single advisory panel.

The most sensitive diagnostic instrument in the biological world sits on the couch, shedding on the cushions, waiting for someone to throw the ball. It has been waiting for regulatory acknowledgment since the Pleistocene.

It is a very good device.
