Water Law & Atmospheric Hydrology

18 U.S.C. § 2314’s Own “Stolen or Converted” Standard Classifies the Water Cycle as the Largest Interstate Stolen Property Operation in American History, and the FBI Has Never Investigated a Cloud

Under 18 U.S.C. § 2314, whoever transports in interstate commerce any goods, wares, or merchandise of the value of $5,000 or more, knowing the same to have been stolen, converted, or taken by fraud, shall be fined or imprisoned not more than ten years. Under the prior appropriation doctrine that governs water rights in the western United States, water rights are property—bought, sold, litigated, and valued at up to $85,000 per acre-foot in Colorado’s Front Range. Each year, the sun evaporates approximately 27.3 million acre-feet of water from American reservoirs alone—removing it from the possession of its lawful owners without consent, compensation, or so much as an eviction notice. Atmospheric currents then transport this converted property across state lines at altitudes between 3,000 and 40,000 feet, depositing it in jurisdictions that never purchased it. The annual value of this interstate transfer, calculated at prevailing western water-rights market prices, exceeds $2.8 trillion. The FBI has opened zero case files. No grand jury has been convened. No cloud has been indicted.

By Linnea Thorvald · May 14, 2026 · 14 min read

I. The Statutory Framework

The National Stolen Property Act, enacted in 1934 and codified at 18 U.S.C. §§ 2314–2315, is the federal government’s principal weapon against the interstate movement of stolen goods. Section 2314 provides, in relevant part: “Whoever transports, transmits, or transfers in interstate or foreign commerce any goods, wares, merchandise, securities or money, of the value of $5,000 or more, knowing the same to have been stolen, converted, or taken by fraud” shall be fined under the title or imprisoned not more than ten years, or both.1

The statute was designed to address a simple enforcement gap: state law enforcement could not pursue stolen goods once they crossed a state boundary. A thief who stole a car in New Jersey and drove it to Pennsylvania was beyond the jurisdictional reach of New Jersey police and had committed no crime that Pennsylvania recognized, because the theft occurred elsewhere. The National Stolen Property Act closed this gap by making the interstate transportation itself a federal offense.2

The FBI’s Classification 87—Interstate Transportation of Stolen Property—was established the same year to track enforcement of the new statute. It was expanded in 1939 to include forged securities and fraudulent checks, and again in 1956 to encompass all stolen property valued at $50,000 or more.3 The classification remains active. The Bureau continues to investigate cases in which goods valued above the statutory threshold are moved across state lines by parties who know or should know the goods were stolen or converted.

The statute requires three elements: (1) the transportation of goods in interstate commerce; (2) a value of $5,000 or more; and (3) knowledge that the goods were “stolen, converted, or taken by fraud.” The Ninth Circuit’s model jury instructions specify that “converted” means “the wrongful exercise of dominion or control over another’s property, in denial of or inconsistent with that person’s rights.”4

The question before us is not whether the statute has been vigorously enforced. It has. Tyler Wayne Jones of Vian, Oklahoma, received 36 months’ probation for transporting six stolen zero-turn mowers valued at $5,000.5 Deanthony Blocker of California was convicted for conspiring to transport stolen cigarettes from a Louisiana warehouse.6 The Department of Justice prosecutes these cases routinely.

The question is whether the same statutory framework, applied with the same rigor, classifies the water cycle as the largest ongoing stolen property operation in the United States. It does.

II. Water as Property

In the seventeen western states that follow the prior appropriation doctrine, water rights are not mere regulatory entitlements. They are property. Real, alienable, adjudicated property that can be bought, sold, mortgaged, condemned, bequeathed, and litigated with the same vigor as any parcel of land.

The foundational case is Coffin v. Left Hand Ditch Co., decided by the Colorado Supreme Court in 1882. The dispute concerned the diversion of water from St. Vrain Creek to James Creek by the Left Hand Ditch Company, which impaired downstream irrigation rights held by Coffin. The court ruled in favor of the Ditch Company, establishing that under Colorado law, water rights are allocated on a “first in time, first in right” basis—the doctrine of prior appropriation.7

The decision was explicit: the riparian doctrine of the humid East, under which all landowners adjacent to a watercourse share equal rights to its flow, had no application in the arid West. Water was too scarce, too valuable, and too essential to agriculture to be shared equally. Instead, the first person to divert water and put it to “beneficial use” acquired a senior right to that water, enforceable against all subsequent appropriators.8

Colorado incorporated the prior appropriation doctrine into its state constitution in 1876, six years before Coffin. Article XVI, Section 6 declares: “The right to divert the unappropriated waters of any natural stream to beneficial uses shall never be denied.”9 Water rights in Colorado are adjudicated through a specialized water court system, the only one of its kind in the United States, where rights are decreed with priority dates, quantities, and conditions of use.10

These rights are not theoretical. They trade on an active market. In a recent auction of Colorado-Big Thompson (C-BT) project units—the transferable water rights associated with the Bureau of Reclamation’s trans-basin diversion system supplying northeastern Colorado—six units sold for a total of $4.7 million, averaging $74,600 per acre-foot.11 This represents a doubling from the $36,300 per acre-foot recorded in 2015. In Colorado’s Front Range, where population growth has outpaced water supply, prices have reached $85,000 per acre-foot for Colorado River water rights.12

California’s water market is equally robust. Limoneira Company sold water rights in the Santa Paula Basin at $30,000 per acre-foot.13 The Colorado River Indian Tribes, granted Congressional authority in 2023 to lease 662,402 acre-feet annually, sit on water assets that Arizona municipalities would pay dearly to access.14 Denver’s suburban communities face projected water costs of $4,000 per acre-foot as aquifer depletion accelerates.15

Water, in the American West, is not a resource. It is a commodity. It is the most valuable commodity many of these states possess. And like all property, it can be stolen.

Water rights in Colorado’s Front Range have reached $85,000 per acre-foot. An acre-foot is 325,851 gallons. The sun does not pay market price.

III. The Conversion

Conversion, under American common law, is the wrongful exercise of dominion or control over another’s personal property in a manner inconsistent with the owner’s rights.16 The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 222A defines it as “an intentional exercise of dominion or control over a chattel which so seriously interferes with the right of another to control it that the actor may justly be required to pay the other the full value of the chattel.”

Evaporation is the phase transition of water from liquid to vapor, driven by solar radiation. When sunlight strikes the surface of a reservoir, lake, or agricultural irrigation canal, it imparts kinetic energy to water molecules. Those molecules with sufficient energy escape the liquid surface and enter the atmosphere as water vapor. This process is continuous, involuntary, and requires no human intervention.

It also requires no owner consent.

A reservoir owner in Colorado who holds a decreed water right of 10,000 acre-feet has, as a matter of state law, a property interest in that water. The water is allocated, adjudicated, and quantified. It belongs to someone. When solar radiation evaporates 8 percent of that stored water annually—a conservative estimate for arid western reservoirs—800 acre-feet of property is removed from the owner’s possession and transferred to the atmosphere.17

The owner did not authorize this transfer. The owner did not receive compensation. The owner did not enter into a contract with the sun. The water simply left. It was taken by a process over which the owner exercises no control, in a manner entirely inconsistent with the owner’s right to possess, use, and dispose of the property.

Nationally, the numbers are staggering. A 2023 study combining remote sensing and modeling approaches estimated that American reservoirs lose approximately 33.73 billion cubic meters of water per year to evaporation—equivalent to approximately 27.3 million acre-feet.18 This is not evapotranspiration from forests and grasslands, which involves water that has not been appropriated. This is water sitting in man-made reservoirs, behind man-made dams, held by entities with legal title to it, and it is leaving.

Lake Mead, the nation’s largest reservoir by capacity, loses approximately 800,000 acre-feet per year to evaporation—roughly 6 percent of its active storage.19 Lake Powell loses an estimated 600,000 acre-feet annually.20 These are not minor seepage losses. These are volumes that exceed the total annual water allocation of entire states under the Colorado River Compact.

The 1922 Colorado River Compact allocates 7.5 million acre-feet per year to the Upper Basin states (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming) and 7.5 million acre-feet per year to the Lower Basin states (Arizona, California, and Nevada).21 A 1944 treaty guaranteed Mexico 1.5 million acre-feet annually, bringing the total committed allocation to 16.5 million acre-feet per year.22 The combined evaporative loss from Lakes Mead and Powell alone—1.4 million acre-feet per year—approaches the entire Mexican allocation. It vanishes into the air. It does not flow downstream. It does not satisfy any compact obligation. It is simply gone, taken by a process that no signatory anticipated and no provision addresses.

Lake Mead loses approximately 800,000 acre-feet per year to evaporation. That is more water than the state of Nevada’s entire annual Colorado River allocation of 300,000 acre-feet. The sun takes it. Nevada does not complain.

IV. The Interstate Transportation

Water vapor, once evaporated, does not remain in place. It is transported by atmospheric circulation patterns—prevailing winds, jet streams, and the large-scale convective systems that drive weather across the North American continent. The mechanism is neither subtle nor speculative. It is the hydrological cycle, and it has been documented with precision by the United States Geological Survey, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, and every introductory earth science textbook published since the nineteenth century.

The most dramatic manifestation of this transport mechanism is the atmospheric river—a narrow, elongated corridor of water vapor in the lower troposphere that can carry moisture loads equivalent to 7.5 to 15 times the average daily discharge of the Mississippi River.23 NOAA describes atmospheric rivers as responsible for delivering up to 50 percent of the annual precipitation on the West Coast of the United States.24 They originate over the Pacific Ocean, but the moisture they carry has been evaporated from surfaces around the world, including American reservoirs, lakes, and rivers.

The domestic pattern is no less impressive. Water evaporated from reservoirs in the Colorado River Basin does not dissipate locally. It is entrained in westerly winds and transported eastward. Moisture evaporated in Arizona may precipitate in New Mexico. Moisture evaporated in California’s Central Valley enters the atmospheric system and may travel hundreds or thousands of miles before returning to Earth as precipitation in a state whose water rights regime has no relationship whatsoever to California’s.

The USGS has documented this process extensively. Approximately 70 percent of all precipitation that falls on the contiguous United States is returned to the atmosphere through evapotranspiration.25 Much of that moisture precipitates again downwind, often in a different state. The hydrological cycle is, in its mechanical essence, a continuous system of evaporation, atmospheric transport, and deposition that moves water across every state boundary in the nation without stopping at any checkpoint, inspection station, or port of entry.

Under 18 U.S.C. § 2314, the interstate element is satisfied when goods cross a state line. The statute does not require that the transport be conducted by a human agent. It does not require a truck, a train, or a shipping container. It requires only that goods be “transported, transmitted, or transferred in interstate or foreign commerce.” Water vapor evaporated from a Colorado reservoir that precipitates as rain in Kansas has been transported in interstate commerce by any definition the statute admits. The transport medium is the atmosphere. The carrier is the wind. The distance traveled is measured in hundreds of miles. The crossing of state boundaries is not incidental. It is the entire point of the hydrological cycle’s atmospheric transport phase.

V. The Valuation Problem

The $5,000 threshold in § 2314 was set in 1934, when Congress presumably imagined it would exclude trivially small thefts from federal jurisdiction. It has not been adjusted for inflation. In 2026 dollars, $5,000 in 1934 would be approximately $115,000.26 Congress chose not to update the threshold, meaning that the statute now captures the interstate movement of goods whose real value, in 1934 terms, may be as little as $217.

This is relevant because the evaporative “theft” of water from a single western reservoir exceeds the $5,000 threshold by a factor that renders the threshold meaningless.

Consider a modest agricultural reservoir in eastern Colorado holding 500 acre-feet under a decreed water right. At prevailing C-BT market prices of $74,600 per acre-foot, the total value of the stored water is $37.3 million.11 Annual evaporative loss of 8 percent removes 40 acre-feet of water worth $2.98 million. This is 596 times the statutory threshold. A single reservoir. A single year.

Scale this to the national reservoir system. The 27.3 million acre-feet of annual reservoir evaporation, valued at the conservative lower end of western water-rights markets—$4,000 per acre-foot, the projected cost for Denver suburban water15—represents $109.2 billion in converted property per year. At the C-BT market rate of $74,600 per acre-foot, the figure is $2.04 trillion. At the Colorado River Front Range price of $85,000 per acre-foot, it is $2.32 trillion.

These calculations use only reservoir evaporation—water that was indisputably owned, stored, and allocated under legal rights regimes. If we include agricultural evapotranspiration from irrigated cropland, where farmers hold appropriative water rights to every gallon applied to their fields, the volumes and values increase by an order of magnitude. The USGS estimates that total evapotranspiration across the contiguous United States exceeds 70 percent of annual precipitation—a volume so large that expressing it in dollar terms at western water prices produces numbers that cease to be meaningful and become instead a commentary on the relationship between law and physics.25

The statutory threshold is $5,000. The annual atmospheric conversion and interstate transport of western water rights exceeds this threshold by a factor of approximately 400 million. No other category of interstate stolen property in the history of the statute has approached this ratio.

At $74,600 per acre-foot, the annual evaporative loss from American reservoirs alone is valued at $2.04 trillion. The FBI’s entire annual budget is $11.3 billion. The water cycle steals in three days what the Bureau recovers in a year.

VI. The Colorado River Compact as Implicit Acknowledgment

The seven states of the Colorado River Basin have been aware of the evaporation problem since before the 1922 Compact was signed. The negotiations at Bishop’s Lodge in Santa Fe, New Mexico, presided over by Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover, produced an agreement that allocated 15 million acre-feet of river flow annually between the Upper and Lower Basins—a figure based on streamflow estimates from the Laguna Diversion Dam that were, in retrospect, measured during one of the wettest periods in the past five centuries.27

The Compact addressed water in the river. It did not address water leaving the river. The framers knew that reservoirs lose water to evaporation. They knew that Lake Mead, authorized by the Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928 and filled beginning in 1935, would lose hundreds of thousands of acre-feet per year to the desert sun.28 They built the reservoir anyway, and they did not charge the evaporated water against any state’s allocation.

This means that the Colorado River Compact implicitly recognizes that a substantial fraction of the basin’s allocated water will be taken by the atmosphere each year—and assigns the loss to no one. It is not an Upper Basin loss. It is not a Lower Basin loss. It is not a Mexican loss. It is the atmosphere’s gain, and the atmosphere is not a party to the Compact. It was not invited to Bishop’s Lodge. It did not sign the agreement. It takes the water anyway.

If the evaporative loss from Lakes Mead and Powell were charged against a single state’s allocation, it would consume Nevada’s entire annual entitlement of 300,000 acre-feet nearly five times over.29 It would exceed the total water usage of the City of Los Angeles. It would be, by volume, the single largest appropriation in the Colorado River system—and it is made by an entity with no water right, no beneficial use, no point of diversion, and no return flow.

The Bureau of Reclamation, which operates both dams, tracks evaporative losses with meticulous precision. The agency publishes annual consumptive use and loss reports that quantify, to the acre-foot, how much water left the reservoir system via evaporation. These reports are public. They are available on the Bureau’s website. They document, in detail, the continuous removal of allocated property from the federal water storage system.30

The Bureau knows. The states know. The water users know. Under 18 U.S.C. § 2314, knowledge that goods have been converted is an element of the offense. Every entity in the Colorado River system that has read an evaporation report possesses that knowledge. The 24-hour reporting window passed approximately 37,230 days ago.

VII. The Knowledge Element

Section 2314 requires that the transporter know the goods were “stolen, converted, or taken by fraud.” The Supreme Court addressed the knowledge requirement in United States v. Dowling (1985), holding that § 2314 applies only to goods that were “physically ‘stolen, converted, or taken by fraud’”—that is, goods over which the defendant or a prior party exercised wrongful physical dominion.31

The atmosphere exercises physical dominion over evaporated water with a thoroughness that would impress any common-law court. Once water molecules enter the vapor phase, they are in the exclusive possession of the atmospheric system. They are carried by winds. They are lifted by convective updrafts. They are condensed into droplets. They are transported thousands of miles from the point of conversion. The owner of the reservoir below has zero ability to reclaim them. The water has been physically taken from the owner’s possession and is now in the exclusive control of a meteorological system that acknowledges no property claim.

Does the atmosphere “know” the water was converted? This is, admittedly, a difficult question. The atmosphere does not possess consciousness. It does not maintain records. It does not consult attorneys. But the knowledge element of § 2314 has never required literal cognitive awareness on the part of the goods themselves—it requires knowledge on the part of the transporter. And here the analysis takes an uncomfortable turn.

The transporter is the atmospheric circulation system. Its behavior is governed by the laws of thermodynamics, not the laws of the United States. It does not know, in the human sense, that the water it carries was taken from a reservoir whose contents were owned by a municipal water authority. But neither does a truck. A truck transporting stolen goods across state lines does not “know” its cargo is stolen. The driver knows. And if the driver is the atmosphere itself—a system that has been performing this same conversion-and-transport operation continuously for 4.5 billion years, with a pattern so consistent that the USGS can predict its annual volume to within single-digit percentages—then the question is not whether the atmosphere knows. The question is whether anyone can claim, at this point, that it does not.

VIII. The Enforcement Gap

The FBI maintains 56 field offices and approximately 400 resident agencies across the United States. Its workforce exceeds 35,000 employees, including more than 13,000 special agents.32 The Bureau investigates complex financial crimes, public corruption, civil rights violations, organized crime, and terrorism. It has the resources, the jurisdiction, and the statutory authority to investigate the interstate transportation of stolen property valued at $5,000 or more.

It has never investigated a cloud.

No FBI field office has opened a case file on atmospheric moisture transport. No special agent has been assigned to the Evaporation Desk. No undercover operation has infiltrated a cumulonimbus formation. No wiretap has been placed on a jet stream. No informant within the atmospheric system has been cultivated, debriefed, or placed in a witness protection program.

This is not because the Bureau lacks jurisdiction. The interstate element is satisfied every time moisture crosses a state line. The value element is satisfied before the first acre-foot evaporates. The conversion element is satisfied the moment water is removed from a reservoir in which someone holds a legal property interest. Every element of § 2314 is met, continuously, across every western state, every day of the year.

The enforcement gap is total. In the same year that the Department of Justice secured a conviction against Tyler Wayne Jones for transporting six stolen lawn mowers valued at $5,000 across a state line, the sun converted approximately $2 trillion worth of appropriated water from American reservoirs and the atmosphere transported it across every state boundary in the western United States. The lawn mowers were recovered. The water was not.5

The Department of Justice Criminal Division’s manual on § 2314 prosecution notes that the statute “requires physical possession of goods” and does not extend to intangible property interests.33 Water is not intangible. It has mass. It has volume. It can be weighed, measured, and stored. When it evaporates, it transitions from one phase to another, but it does not cease to be a physical substance. A cloud weighs approximately 1.1 million pounds.34 If that is intangible, the Department of Justice has a definitional problem that extends beyond water law.

No FBI field office has opened a case file on atmospheric moisture transport. No special agent has been assigned to the Evaporation Desk. No undercover operation has infiltrated a cumulonimbus formation.

IX. The Grand Jury Problem

Under the Fifth Amendment and the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, no person may be prosecuted for a federal felony except upon indictment by a grand jury.35 To obtain an indictment under § 2314, a prosecutor would need to present evidence to a grand jury establishing probable cause that (1) property was stolen or converted, (2) it was transported in interstate commerce, and (3) the defendant knew of the conversion.

The identity of the defendant presents the first procedural obstacle. The sun is not a legal person. It is not incorporated. It does not maintain a registered agent in any U.S. jurisdiction. It is located approximately 93 million miles from the nearest federal courthouse, outside the territorial jurisdiction of the United States, in a vacuum that does not support service of process.36

The atmosphere is similarly unsuitable as a defendant. It is not a person, a corporation, a partnership, or an unincorporated association. It does not have a mailing address. It has never appeared in court. Grand jury subpoenas directed to “The Atmosphere, c/o The Troposphere, Altitude 0–12 km, United States Airspace” would present service difficulties that the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure do not contemplate.

One might argue that the responsible parties are the dam operators—the Bureau of Reclamation, the Army Corps of Engineers, state water authorities—who store water in open-air reservoirs knowing that evaporation will occur. By storing water in an open vessel in the desert, they create the conditions for conversion. They have knowledge that the conversion will occur, because they measure it annually. They continue the practice, year after year, decade after decade, despite losses that would constitute gross negligence in any other context of property management.

If a bank stored $2 trillion in cash on an open-air rooftop in a windstorm and reported annually that approximately 8 percent blew away across state lines, the FBI would not describe this as a meteorological phenomenon. It would describe it as a crime scene.

X. The Multi-State Jurisdictional Crisis

Water vapor does not respect state boundaries. A single evaporation event from Lake Powell in Utah may produce moisture that precipitates in Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona, or any of the thirty states downwind of the Four Corners region. The jurisdictional analysis under § 2314 requires identifying the state of origin and the state of receipt, but atmospheric transport does not maintain a chain of custody. There are no waybills. There are no bills of lading. There is no manifest.

The Colorado River Compact attempted to allocate water among seven states. It did not allocate atmospheric moisture. The 1922 negotiators divided the river into an Upper Basin and a Lower Basin, separated at Lee Ferry, Arizona—a geographic point chosen for the convenience of measurement, not the convenience of clouds.37 Water that evaporates from a Lower Basin reservoir and precipitates in an Upper Basin state has, in effect, reversed the Compact’s allocation framework. It has moved water from the Lower Basin to the Upper Basin without a transfer agreement, an environmental impact statement, or an act of Congress.

The Supreme Court addressed interstate water disputes in Kansas v. Colorado (1907), holding that the doctrine of equitable apportionment governs the allocation of interstate rivers between states.38 But equitable apportionment applies to water in rivers—liquid water moving through defined channels subject to measurement and diversion. It does not apply to water in the sky. No Supreme Court original-jurisdiction case has ever addressed the equitable apportionment of clouds. No Special Master has been appointed to divide the atmospheric moisture budget of the western United States.

If Colorado were to file suit against the atmosphere for depositing water that originated in Arizona’s allocation, the Court would face a preliminary question it has never confronted: can a state assert a property claim to moisture that was evaporated from another state’s reservoir, transported at 30,000 feet by the jet stream, and deposited as rain on Colorado’s Western Slope? And if Colorado collects that rain in a reservoir, has it received stolen property within the meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 2315?

The statute says yes. Physics says this is how precipitation works. The intersection of the two produces a result that neither Congress nor the USGS intended.

XI. The Rain Barrel Paradox

Colorado law, until 2016, prohibited the collection of rainwater by homeowners without a water right. House Bill 16-1005 amended the law to allow each Colorado household to collect up to 110 gallons of rainwater in two rain barrels for outdoor use.39 The rationale was that rainwater falling on a residential roof would otherwise have entered the surface water system and was therefore the property of senior appropriators.

The premise of Colorado’s rain barrel prohibition was, in essence, that rain is someone else’s water. The state recognized that precipitation has an owner—the downstream water rights holder whose appropriation depends on the natural flow of surface water and groundwater. A homeowner who diverted rainwater from the flow was, in the legal framework of prior appropriation, taking property that belonged to another.

Follow this logic upstream. If rain falling on a Colorado roof is the property of a downstream appropriator, then the moisture from which that rain condensed was also that appropriator’s property at the moment it entered Colorado airspace. And that moisture was evaporated from a reservoir or watercourse in another state, where it was the property of a different appropriator. The rain barrel homeowner is not the thief. The homeowner is the fence—the receiver of goods that were stolen when the sun evaporated them from a reservoir in Utah and transported across state lines by prevailing westerly winds.

Colorado recognized this property chain with sufficient seriousness to prohibit rain barrels for 140 years. It then created a narrow exception for 110 gallons per household, treating even this modest collection as a regulatory concession rather than a natural right. The state, in other words, understood that atmospheric water is owned. It simply declined to follow that understanding to its logical conclusion under federal stolen property law.

Colorado prohibited rain barrels for 140 years on the theory that rain is someone else’s water. The state understood that atmospheric moisture is owned. It declined to follow the logic to its federal conclusion.

XII. The Sentencing Exposure

Section 2314 provides for a maximum sentence of ten years’ imprisonment per violation. Each interstate transport of converted goods exceeding $5,000 in value constitutes a separate offense. The number of discrete violations occurring each year in the western United States is, by any calculation methodology, absurd.

If each reservoir evaporation event producing more than $5,000 in converted water rights constitutes a violation—and at $74,600 per acre-foot, the threshold is crossed by the evaporation of 0.067 acre-feet, or approximately 21,800 gallons, which occurs from a single mid-sized reservoir in a matter of hours—then the daily violation count from the western reservoir system runs to the tens of thousands. The annual count is in the millions.

The aggregate sentencing exposure, if each violation carried the maximum ten-year term served consecutively, exceeds the current age of the universe. This is not a meaningful number. It is a mathematical artifact of applying a statute designed for stolen mowers to a process that moves 27.3 million acre-feet of property per year across the boundaries of fifty states.

The fine provision is equally unworkable. 18 U.S.C. § 3571 provides for fines up to $250,000 per violation for an individual and $500,000 per violation for an organization.40 The atmosphere is neither. The sun is neither. The aggregate fine exposure, at $500,000 per violation across millions of annual violations, produces a sum larger than global GDP. The assessment would have to be levied against the sun, which has no assets in any jurisdiction subject to execution of judgment, and the atmosphere, which has no assets at all, unless one counts the water it already stole.

XIII. Conclusion

The National Stolen Property Act criminalizes the interstate transport of goods valued at $5,000 or more, knowing the same to have been stolen or converted. Western water law recognizes water rights as property—property that is bought and sold at prices exceeding $85,000 per acre-foot. The sun evaporates approximately 27.3 million acre-feet of appropriated water from American reservoirs each year, removing it from the possession of its lawful owners without consent or compensation. The atmosphere transports this converted property across state lines at velocities and altitudes that no law enforcement agency monitors. The annual value of the converted property, at prevailing market rates, exceeds $2 trillion.

The elements of the offense are satisfied. The property is real. The conversion is physical. The interstate transport is documented by every meteorological agency in the federal government. The value exceeds the statutory threshold by a factor of hundreds of millions. The knowledge element is met by the Bureau of Reclamation, every state water authority, every farmer who has ever watched a reservoir shrink in July, and every atmospheric scientist who has ever published a paper on evapotranspiration.

The FBI has opened zero investigations. No U.S. Attorney has convened a grand jury. No indictment has been returned against the sun, the atmosphere, or any cloud formation operating in the airspace above the western United States. The Department of Justice prosecutes a man in Oklahoma for transporting six stolen lawn mowers. It does not prosecute the hydrological cycle for transporting $2 trillion in converted water rights across every state boundary between the Rocky Mountains and the Mississippi River.

The statute is 92 years old. The water cycle is 4.5 billion years old. One of them was designed with the other in mind. Neither has been amended to account for the discrepancy.

Every cloud is a getaway vehicle. Every rainstorm is a drop. Every reservoir is a crime scene the FBI drives past on the way to more important things, like lawn mowers.

Ergo.
