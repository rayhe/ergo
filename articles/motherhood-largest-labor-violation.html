Federal Wage Law & Maternal Workforce Compliance

The Fair Labor Standards Act's Own Domestic Worker Provisions Classify American Motherhood as the Largest Uncompensated Labor Operation in Federal History. The Annual Settlement Is Brunch.

The 1974 amendments to the Fair Labor Standards Act extended minimum wage protections to domestic service workers employed in private households. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that mothers of young children perform approximately 4.5 hours of unpaid household and childcare labor per day. The federal minimum wage is $7.25 per hour. The National Retail Federation projects 2026 Mother's Day spending at a record $38 billion. This is not a holiday. It is a structured settlement with a 7.8 percent compliance rate.

By Miriam Osei-Bonsu · May 10, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Annual Payment

The National Retail Federation, in partnership with Prosper Insights & Analytics, projects that Americans will spend a record $38 billion on Mother's Day in 2026.1 This figure surpasses the previous record of $35.7 billion set in 2023 and represents an 11.4 percent increase over 2025's $34.1 billion.2 Eighty-four percent of American adults plan to participate. The average per-person expenditure is $284.25.

The payment is distributed across several categories. Flowers lead at 75 percent of shoppers, with total floral spending projected at approximately $3.2 billion. Greeting cards follow at 73 percent of shoppers, totaling $1.1 billion. Special outings (predominantly brunch and dinner) account for $6.3 billion, with 61 percent of celebrants participating. Jewelry reaches $7.5 billion. Gift cards contribute $3.5 billion.3

The composition of the payment is instructive. The largest category by participation rate (flowers) consists of a perishable asset with a median post-purchase lifespan of 7 to 14 days.4 The second-largest category (greeting cards) consists of a piece of folded cardstock whose market value drops to zero at the moment of delivery. The third category (brunch) is consumed in approximately 90 minutes and produces no lasting asset. The only categories with durable value, jewelry and gift cards, are the ones that recipients are statistically least likely to have selected for themselves.

This is not a consumer spending pattern. It is the disbursement structure of a settlement designed to minimize the present value of future claims.

II. The Liability

The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the American Time Use Survey (ATUS), the federal government's official measure of how Americans allocate their daily hours. The most recent release, covering 2024 data, reports that women spend an average of 2.61 hours per day on household activities: cooking, cleaning, laundry, financial management, and household maintenance.5 Men spend 1.76 hours. The gap of 0.85 hours per day has persisted, in various widths, since the survey began in 2003.

For mothers with at least one child under age six, the figures escalate. ATUS Table 9 reports that these mothers spend an average of 1.93 hours per day on primary childcare activities: feeding, bathing, dressing, reading to, playing with, and providing medical care for children.6 "Primary" is a critical qualifier. It captures only the activity the respondent classified as her main activity at the time. It excludes supervisory care, secondary childcare (tending to a child while cooking dinner, for example), and the ambient cognitive load of monitoring a household. None of these are captured by ATUS methodology. All of them would constitute compensable work hours under federal wage law.

Combined, a mother of young children performs approximately 4.54 hours per day of domestic labor that the BLS measures and an indeterminate additional quantity that it does not. At 4.54 hours per day, 365 days per year (domestic labor does not observe weekends), the annual total is 1,657 hours per mother.

The federal minimum wage, established by the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 and last adjusted on July 24, 2009, is $7.25 per hour.7 It has not changed in 17 years. At this rate, the annual minimum compensation owed to each mother of young children for documented domestic labor is $12,013.25.

III. The Workforce

The U.S. Census Bureau reports approximately 41 million mothers with children under 18 living in the household.8 The total population of American mothers, including those whose children have reached adulthood, is approximately 85.4 million.9

Of the 41 million active-duty mothers (to borrow a term from an institution that does compensate its workforce), approximately 72 percent participate in the paid labor force.10 These 29.5 million women perform unpaid domestic labor in addition to compensated employment. The remaining 28 percent, approximately 11.5 million mothers, perform domestic labor exclusively, without any compensated employment to offset the FLSA liability.

The aggregate annual liability across 41 million mothers at 1,657 hours and $7.25 per hour is $492.5 billion.

The 2026 Mother's Day settlement of $38 billion represents a payment of 7.7 percent of the annual obligation. The remaining 92.3 percent has been carried forward, in rolling fashion, since 1914.

IV. The 1974 Amendment

In 1974, Congress passed the Fair Labor Standards Amendments, Public Law 93-259, which for the first time extended federal minimum wage and overtime protections to domestic service workers employed in private households.11 Prior to 1974, domestic workers (housekeepers, cooks, childcare providers) were explicitly excluded from FLSA coverage. The exclusion had been in place since the original 1938 statute, a legacy of New Deal compromises with Southern legislators who sought to maintain low-cost domestic labor arrangements.12

The legislative history of Public Law 93-259 reveals that Congress acted on a straightforward premise: work performed in a private household is still work. The Conference Report stated that domestic service employees should receive the same minimum wage and overtime protections afforded to other covered workers.13

The amendment covers "any employee who is employed in domestic service in a household."14 The statute defines "employ" as "to suffer or permit to work."15 Section 3(e)(1) of the FLSA defines an employer as "any person acting directly or indirectly in the interest of an employer in relation to an employee."

The statute does not specify that the domestic worker must be a separate person from the household member performing the work. It does not require a W-2. It does not require a written agreement. It requires only that domestic service is performed in a household and that someone has suffered or permitted it to occur.

Every American household with children has suffered or permitted domestic service to occur. The laundry is visible. The meals are consumed. The children are supervised. The knowledge element is satisfied three times a day, at breakfast, lunch, and dinner.

V. The Coincidence

The Department of Labor's Women's Bureau published a study in May 2023 finding that unpaid caregiving reduces mothers' lifetime earnings by 15 percent, an average loss of $295,000 per working mother over the course of a career.16 The methodology examined longitudinal earnings data from the Current Population Survey and the Panel Study of Income Dynamics. The $295,000 figure represents foregone wages, reduced retirement contributions, and diminished lifetime Social Security benefits.

Now consider the parallel calculation. At the FLSA minimum wage of $7.25 per hour, 1,657 hours per year, over a 25-year child-rearing period (the approximate span from first child's birth to youngest child's 18th birthday for a mother of two), the cumulative minimum-wage liability per mother is $297,803.25.

The Department of Labor says mothers lose $295,000 in lifetime earnings because of caregiving. The Fair Labor Standards Act says mothers are owed $297,803 in minimum wages for the caregiving itself. The two figures converge to within 0.9 percent.

The DOL published both analyses without connecting them. The first quantifies what mothers forfeit. The second quantifies what they are owed. The numbers are functionally identical. The Department has, in two separate publications, independently arrived at the same valuation of American motherhood and has drawn no conclusion from the coincidence.

VI. The Flower Logistics

Approximately 80 percent of cut flowers sold in the United States are imported, with Colombia and Ecuador as the dominant suppliers.17 Colombia alone exports over $2 billion in cut flowers annually, with the United States as its primary market, and the Colombian floriculture industry employs approximately 240,000 workers, 60 percent of whom are women.18

During the 2026 Mother's Day season, Avianca Cargo transported 21,000 tonnes of flowers on more than 330 flights, moving approximately 24 million stems per day during peak operations.19 The airline allocated 42 percent of its total cargo capacity to flower transport. This means that nearly half of a major Latin American airline's freight infrastructure was temporarily repurposed to deliver the perishable component of an annual payment to American mothers.

The flowers transit from fields on the Colombian sabana at 2,600 meters elevation near Bogotá to refrigerated staging facilities, to cargo aircraft, to cold-chain distribution centers in Miami, to regional wholesalers, to local florists, to mothers' dining tables.20 The total supply chain involves at least seven handoffs across two countries, three climate zones, and a 2,500-mile air corridor. The median post-delivery lifespan of the product is 10 days.

No other annual compensation event deploys a transcontinental cold-chain logistics operation to deliver an asset that will be worthless within two weeks. A $60 bouquet purchased on May 10 is worth $0 by May 24. The logistics cost (fuel, refrigeration, customs clearance, last-mile delivery) approaches the wholesale value of the flowers themselves. The settlement is structured, in other words, so that approximately half the payment is absorbed by transportation overhead before the recipient touches a single petal.

VII. The Payroll Processor

Hallmark Cards, Inc. was founded in 1910 by Joyce Clyde Hall in Kansas City, Missouri, four years before Mother's Day became a federal observance.21 The timing is notable. The company that would become the dominant processor of Mother's Day documentation was incorporated before the documentation requirement existed.

Americans purchase approximately 141 million Mother's Day cards annually, making it the second-largest card-sending holiday after Christmas (1.6 billion cards).22 The Greeting Card Association estimates the overall U.S. greeting card market at $7.5 billion per year, with 6.5 billion cards sold across all occasions.23 Eighty percent of greeting cards are purchased by women, which means that a significant portion of Mother's Day cards are purchased by mothers for other mothers, a phenomenon in which the payees process their own settlement documentation.

The standard Mother's Day card performs three functions. First, it identifies the payee ("Dear Mom"). Second, it acknowledges the services rendered ("Thank you for everything you do"). Third, it is signed by the payor. These are the structural elements of a remittance advice: the document that accompanies a payment to explain its purpose and identify the parties.24

The Internal Revenue Service processes approximately 150 million individual tax returns per year.25 The Mother's Day card volume of 141 million is 94 percent of IRS filing volume, processed in a single weekend rather than across a four-month filing season. The throughput rate is not comparable.

Hallmark did not build a network of over 2,000 retail stores to sell sentiment. It built a distributed payroll documentation infrastructure for a $38 billion annual settlement that the Department of Labor has never audited.

VIII. The Negotiation Table

Nation's Restaurant News projects that 80 million Americans will dine out on Mother's Day 2026, up from 75 million in 2025.26 Mother's Day is, by the restaurant industry's own data, the single busiest dining day of the year. More seats are occupied on this day than on Valentine's Day, New Year's Eve, Thanksgiving, or any other occasion on the American calendar.

Fifty-three percent of 2025 Mother's Day restaurant traffic was booked between 10:00 AM and 4:00 PM, the window commonly associated with brunch.27 The NRF projects that $6.3 billion of total Mother's Day spending will go toward "special outings," the majority of which involve a table, a menu, and a mimosa.

The economics of Mother's Day brunch merit scrutiny. A typical brunch for a family of four at a full-service restaurant in a metropolitan area costs between $120 and $200, inclusive of beverages and gratuity. For this expenditure, the mother receives approximately 90 minutes of being served food she did not prepare, at a table she did not set, cleaned by staff she did not manage, in an establishment she did not organize.

This 90-minute window represents the only period in the fiscal year during which the average American mother's domestic labor is performed, on her behalf, by a compensated third party, and invoiced to a different member of the household.

In collective bargaining, this is called a concession period. The employer (the family unit) temporarily assumes the operating cost (the restaurant tab) to maintain the ongoing labor relationship without renegotiating the base compensation, which remains at $0.00 per hour. The concession period of 90 minutes, measured against 1,657 annual hours of unpaid domestic labor, represents a break of 0.09 percent of total working time.

Most union contracts negotiate break periods of at least 30 minutes per 8-hour shift, or 6.25 percent. Mothers receive 0.09 percent. The brunch is not a celebration. It is the worst break-time ratio in the history of organized labor.

IX. The Whistleblower

In 1907, Anna Marie Jarvis organized a memorial for her mother, Ann Reeves Jarvis, at St. Andrew's Methodist Church in Grafton, West Virginia.28 By 1908, she had launched a national campaign to establish a formal observance. In 1914, President Woodrow Wilson signed a joint resolution designating the second Sunday in May as Mother's Day.29

By the early 1920s, Jarvis had turned against the holiday she created. She organized boycotts against florists. She filed lawsuits against organizations using the name "Mother's Day" for commercial purposes. She was arrested in 1925 for disturbing the peace at a convention of the American War Mothers, where carnations (her chosen symbol for the holiday) were being sold at a markup.30 She lobbied Congress in 1943 to rescind the designation.31

The standard interpretation is that Jarvis opposed the commercialization of Mother's Day because she believed the holiday should remain a sincere personal tribute rather than a retail event. Jarvis herself said as much, repeatedly and publicly, for three decades.

The alternative interpretation is that Jarvis recognized, fifty years before the Department of Labor acknowledged unpaid domestic work as measurable economic output, that the settlement terms were inadequate. She watched the floral industry mark up carnations. She watched the greeting card companies extract processing fees. She watched the annual payment grow in nominal terms while the underlying liability grew faster. She did not object to flowers in principle. She objected to the exchange rate.

Jarvis died on November 24, 1948, at the age of 84, blind and largely impoverished, at the Marshall Square Sanitarium in West Chester, Pennsylvania.32 According to multiple accounts, her medical and care expenses were paid, at least in part, by individuals associated with the floral and greeting card industries: the same entities she had spent 30 years opposing.33

The person who identified the liability spent her final years in the care of the entities that profited from the settlement. In labor law, this is called a conflict of interest. In corporate governance, it would trigger an independent audit. In the history of Mother's Day, it triggered nothing at all.

X. The Compound Liability

Mother's Day has been observed as a federal holiday since 1914. The annual settlement has been paid, in some form, for 112 consecutive years.

The NRF has tracked Mother's Day spending since 2003. Over the 24-year period from 2003 to 2026, total reported spending exceeds $550 billion.34 Prior to 2003, comprehensive spending data is scarce, but industry estimates suggest the holiday was generating several billion dollars in annual revenue by the mid-1980s.

A conservative estimate of cumulative Mother's Day spending since 1914, adjusting for inflation and population growth, places the total historical outlay between $1.5 trillion and $2 trillion.

Now consider the liability side. The FLSA domestic worker provisions took effect in 1974. The cumulative minimum-wage liability from 1974 to 2026 (52 years), accounting for the growing mother population and rising time-use figures, exceeds $20 trillion at constant 2026 dollars.35

Cumulative settlements of approximately $1.5 to $2 trillion, measured against a cumulative liability of $20 trillion, yield a historical recovery rate of approximately 7.5 to 10 percent.

In class action settlement law, the median recovery rate on documented claims is approximately 9 percent of aggregate damages.36 Mother's Day, without any court involvement, without any attorneys, without any discovery process, and without any of the 85.4 million class members being informed that they are parties to a settlement, has independently converged on the same recovery rate that the American legal system produces after years of litigation.

This is either a remarkable coincidence or evidence that the settlement mechanism is older than the courts that would adjudicate it.

XI. The Opt-Out Clause

The Fair Labor Standards Act permits employees to file complaints with the Wage and Hour Division of the Department of Labor.37 The statute of limitations is two years for non-willful violations and three years for willful violations.38 A willful violation requires that the employer "knew or showed reckless disregard for the matter of whether its conduct was prohibited by the statute."39

Every American household that has benefited from a mother's domestic labor has done so with full knowledge that the labor was occurring. Laundry is visible. Meals are consumed. Children are verifiably supervised. The knowledge element is satisfied at minimum three times daily: at breakfast, lunch, and dinner.

Under the three-year willful provision, each of the 41 million mothers with children under 18 could file for back pay covering 4,971 hours (1,657 hours multiplied by three years) at $7.25 per hour, totaling $36,039.75. Liquidated damages under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b) double the back-pay award, bringing the per-claimant recovery to $72,079.50.40

Across 41 million eligible claimants, the aggregate available recovery is $2.96 trillion. This exceeds the annual federal budget for Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid combined.

The Wage and Hour Division receives approximately 24,000 complaints per year.41 In over fifty years since the 1974 amendments, not one has originated from a residential kitchen. The compliance rate for Mother's Day settlement payments, 7.7 percent, is poor. The complaint filing rate, 0.0 percent, is unprecedented in federal enforcement history.

XII. Conclusion

The data is unambiguous. Forty-one million American mothers perform 1,657 hours per year of unpaid domestic labor. The Fair Labor Standards Act, as amended in 1974, extends minimum wage protections to domestic service workers in private households. The annual liability, calculated at the federal minimum of $7.25 per hour, exceeds $492 billion. The annual settlement of $38 billion (delivered in perishable flowers, a single meal, a piece of folded cardstock, and jewelry the recipient did not select) covers 7.7 percent of the obligation.

Anna Jarvis understood this in 1920, without the benefit of the American Time Use Survey, the Bureau of Economic Analysis's household production satellite account, or a spreadsheet. She saw the flowers. She saw the cards. She calculated the gap in her head and spent 28 years saying so. The greeting card industry paid for her hospice care as a final processing fee.

The 141 million cards that will be purchased this weekend are not expressions of gratitude. They are remittance advices for a settlement that has never been renegotiated. The 80 million restaurant reservations are not celebrations. They are 90-minute concession windows in a labor dispute that began in 1914 and has never been resolved. The 21,000 tonnes of Colombian flowers currently in transit are not gifts. They are depreciating assets tendered in partial satisfaction of a liability that compounds annually and has never been audited.

The Department of Labor knows the hours. The FLSA establishes the rate. The Census Bureau counts the workers. The NRF tracks the payments. Four federal agencies have, between them, every data point necessary to identify a $492 billion annual labor violation, and none of them have connected the arithmetic.

Mother's Day is not a holiday. It is an annual structured settlement with a 7.7 percent compliance rate, a 10-day depreciation window on the primary asset class, a 0.09 percent break-time ratio, and a payroll documentation system operated out of Kansas City by a company founded four years before the obligation was federally recognized.

Happy Mother's Day.
