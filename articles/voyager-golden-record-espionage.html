Espionage Law & Interstellar Communications

The Espionage Act’s Own Definition of “National Defense Information” and Executive Order 13526’s Own Classification Framework Classify the Voyager Golden Record as the Most Comprehensive Unauthorized Disclosure of National Defense Information in American History, Transmitted at Seventeen Kilometers Per Second to an Unidentified Recipient Whose Capabilities, Intentions, and Relationship to the United States Are Entirely Unknown, and the Department of Justice Has Not Opened a Single Investigation in Forty-Nine Years

Section 793(d) of Title 18, United States Code, prohibits any person lawfully possessing “information relating to the national defense” from willfully communicating, delivering, or transmitting it “to any person not entitled to receive it.” In 1977, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration affixed to the exterior of two spacecraft a gold-plated copper phonograph record containing 115 photographs of American infrastructure, a map of Earth’s galactic coordinates precise enough to locate the planet from anywhere in the Milky Way, a sample of uranium-238, spoken greetings in fifty-five languages confirming the existence of a linguistically diverse civilization, and instructions for decoding all of it. The intended recipient has not been identified. No security clearance has been issued to any entity outside the solar system. The record is currently 16 billion miles from the nearest counterintelligence office and accelerating. The Department of Justice has not opened a file.

By Julian Wexford · June 27, 2026 · 16 min read

I. The Statutory Framework

The Espionage Act of 1917, as amended and codified at 18 U.S.C. §§ 793–798, provides the principal statutory framework for the protection of national defense information in the United States. Section 793(d) establishes criminal liability for any person who, “lawfully having possession of, access to, control over, or being entrusted with any document, writing, code book, signal book, sketch, photograph, photographic negative, blueprint, plan, map, model, instrument, appliance, or note relating to the national defense, or information relating to the national defense which information the possessor has reason to believe could be used to the injury of the United States or to the advantage of any foreign nation, willfully communicates, delivers, transmits or causes to be communicated, delivered, or transmitted the same to any person not entitled to receive it.”1

The statute enumerates, with conspicuous specificity, the categories of material covered: documents, writings, sketches, photographs, blueprints, plans, maps, models, instruments, and appliances. A phonograph record is an instrument. The photographs encoded on it are photographs. The pulsar map etched on its cover is a map. The uranium-238 sample electroplated onto its surface is, by any reasonable construction, an appliance. The statute covers the entire package.

Section 793(b) separately prohibits anyone from copying, taking, making, or obtaining “any sketch, photograph, photographic negative, blueprint, plan, map, model, instrument, appliance, document, writing, or note of anything connected with the national defense” with intent or reason to believe the information will be used to the injury of the United States or to the advantage of any foreign nation.2 Carl Sagan’s committee made the photographs. NASA took the map. The intent was to deliver all of it to a foreign recipient.

Executive Order 13526, signed by President Obama on December 29, 2009, and currently governing the classification of national security information, provides at Section 1.4 that information “shall not be considered for classification unless its unauthorized disclosure could reasonably be expected to cause identifiable or describable damage to the national security” and pertains to one or more of eight enumerated categories.3 These categories include: “(a) military plans, weapons systems, or operations; … (e) scientific, technological, or economic matters relating to the national security; … (g) vulnerabilities or capabilities of systems, installations, infrastructures, projects, plans, or protection services relating to the national security.”

At the time of the Voyager launch in 1977, the governing order was Executive Order 11652, signed by President Nixon in 1972, which contained substantively similar classification criteria.4 Neither order was consulted. No classification review was performed. The record was assembled by a committee of six academics, none of whom held active security clearances, under the chairmanship of a professor of astronomy at Cornell University who had never undergone a Single Scope Background Investigation.5

II. The Disclosure

The Voyager Golden Record is a 12-inch gold-plated copper phonograph disc, encased in a protective aluminum jacket, carried aboard both Voyager 1 and Voyager 2. Its contents were selected for NASA by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan. The committee assembled 115 images encoded in analog form, spoken greetings in fifty-five languages, an audio essay containing natural and industrial sounds, twenty-seven musical selections, and printed messages from President Jimmy Carter and United Nations Secretary-General Kurt Waldheim.6

The images include photographs of the Golden Gate Bridge, an airport in Toronto, a factory interior, a radio telescope in the Netherlands, the Great Wall of China, a construction site, the city of Boston, the Sydney Opera House, and an Antarctic expedition base.7 The audio includes the sound of a train, a tractor, an automobile, and an F-111 flyover. The record is, by any reasonable assessment, an intelligence collection officer’s briefing package assembled with public funding and launched into the operational environment on a government vehicle.

Each disc is accompanied by an aluminum cover engraved with operating instructions rendered in symbolic language, a diagram of the hydrogen atom to establish a universal unit of measurement, a pulsar map identifying the location of Earth’s solar system in the galaxy, and a small quantity of uranium-238 electroplated onto the cover’s surface, placed there so that a recipient can measure its radioactive decay and determine how long the spacecraft has been in transit.8

The record was produced in duplicate. One copy was mounted on Voyager 1, launched on September 5, 1977. The other was mounted on Voyager 2, launched on August 20, 1977. NASA did not merely transmit the disclosure once. It transmitted it twice, on separate vehicles, traveling in different directions, to maximize the probability that an unidentified recipient would intercept at least one copy.9 In espionage tradecraft, this is known as redundant dead-drop servicing.

III. The National Defense Information

Executive Order 13526, Section 1.4(g), requires classification of information pertaining to “vulnerabilities or capabilities of systems, installations, infrastructures, projects, plans, or protection services relating to the national security.” The Golden Record contains photographs of American bridges, airports, cities, factories, and construction techniques. It contains an image of a radio telescope, which is a dual-use facility integral to the Deep Space Network that NASA operates jointly with the Department of Defense for satellite tracking and ballistic missile early warning.10 It contains an image of a train, revealing the existence and general configuration of the American rail freight network that the Department of Homeland Security has designated as critical infrastructure under Presidential Policy Directive 21.11

Section 1.4(e) requires classification of information relating to “scientific, technological, or economic matters relating to the national security.” The record contains images depicting the structure of DNA, the mathematical definitions of physical constants, human cell division, and the anatomy of the human body in eight detailed diagrams.7 It reveals, in precise scientific notation, the fundamental technological capacity of the species that assembled it. To a sufficiently advanced recipient, these images constitute a comprehensive vulnerability assessment of human biology.

Section 1.4(a) covers “military plans, weapons systems, or operations.” The record contains the sounds of a military jet aircraft. It contains an image of a rocket launch. It contains, on its cover, a sample of uranium-238, which the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 defines as “source material”—that is, material from which fissile isotopes can be derived for use in nuclear weapons.12 The placement of source material on the exterior of a spacecraft bound for interstellar space, without NRC oversight, simultaneously violates Section 1.4(f) of Executive Order 13526, which protects information about “United States Government programs for safeguarding nuclear materials or facilities,” by demonstrating that the United States does not, in fact, safeguard its nuclear materials with any particular rigor.

The committee assembled 115 photographs, a map of Earth’s galactic coordinates, a sample of uranium-238, and instructions for decoding all of it. In espionage tradecraft, this is known as redundant dead-drop servicing.

IV. The Map

The cover of the Voyager Golden Record bears an engraved diagram designed by astronomer Frank Drake in 1971, first used on the Pioneer 10 and 11 plaques and reproduced with minor modifications for the Voyager mission. The diagram shows the position of Earth’s Sun relative to fourteen pulsars, with each pulsar’s rotation period encoded in binary notation to twelve significant figures.13

The fourteen pulsars selected by Drake serve as galactic reference points. Because pulsars are neutron stars with distinctive electromagnetic signatures—each one identifiable by its rotation period, spin-down rate, and approximate position—any technologically capable recipient can use the diagram to triangulate the location of Earth’s solar system within the Milky Way galaxy.14 The map does not merely indicate a general region. It provides sufficient information to locate one specific star among approximately 200 billion in the galaxy.

Section 793(b) of the Espionage Act specifically enumerates “map” among the categories of material whose unauthorized creation or transmission is prohibited. This is a map. It was created by an American citizen, at a facility that receives federal funding, depicting the location of every military installation, nuclear facility, command center, population center, and acre of sovereign territory belonging to the United States. It depicts the location of these assets because it depicts the location of the planet on which all of them exist. The map does not distinguish between classified and unclassified installations because it operates at a resolution that renders the distinction meaningless. If you can find the planet, you can find everything on it.

Drake later acknowledged, in an interview with his daughter Nadia Drake for National Geographic, that the committee gave no consideration to the security implications of broadcasting Earth’s coordinates: “In those days, all the people I dealt with were optimists, and they thought the ETs would be friendly. Nobody thought, even for a few seconds, about whether this might be a dangerous thing to do.”15 The Espionage Act does not contain an optimism exception.

V. The Recipient

Section 793(d) prohibits the transmission of national defense information to “any person not entitled to receive it.” The Voyager Golden Record was designed, explicitly and by the stated intention of its creators, to be received by an extraterrestrial civilization. Carl Sagan described the project as “a bottle into the cosmic ocean.”6

The intended recipient has not been identified. No background investigation has been conducted. No security clearance has been adjudicated. No determination has been made regarding the recipient’s citizenship, nationality, allegiance, criminal history, or relationship to any foreign government, foreign intelligence service, or hostile non-state actor. The recipient’s technological capabilities are unknown. The recipient’s intentions are unknown. The recipient’s existence is unknown.

From the standpoint of counterintelligence risk assessment, this is the worst possible configuration. An identified hostile foreign intelligence officer who intercepts classified material presents a bounded threat: the officer’s affiliation is known, the damage can be assessed, and countermeasures can be developed. An unidentified recipient of unknown capability, unknown intention, and unknown relationship to the United States presents an unbounded threat. The intelligence community has a term for disclosures to unidentified parties: they are called compromises, and the standard response is to assume worst-case exploitation.

No one on the Sagan committee held a valid security clearance. No one on the committee had undergone a counterintelligence polygraph. The committee included Sagan, an astronomer; Frank Drake, an astrophysicist; Ann Druyan, a creative director; Timothy Ferris, a journalist; Jon Lomberg, an artist; and Linda Salzman Sagan, an artist and writer.16 Not one had received the counterintelligence training required under Intelligence Community Directive 700 for any person handling classified material. This did not prevent them from assembling the most geographically comprehensive intelligence package in American history, loading it onto a government vehicle, and launching it toward an unidentified recipient at 17.1 kilometers per second.

VI. The Classification Failure

The classification system of the United States, as established by Executive Order 13526 and its predecessors, operates on the principle that any person who originates information that may require classification must transmit it promptly to the appropriate agency for a classification determination. Section 1.3(e) of EO 13526 provides that when an employee, contractor, or grantee “who does not have original classification authority originates information believed by that person to require classification, the information shall be protected in a manner consistent with this order.”3

The Sagan committee originated information that, under any reasonable application of Section 1.4, pertains to at least four of the eight classification categories. The committee did not submit the Golden Record’s contents for classification review. NASA did not submit them. No agency in the federal government reviewed the 115 images, the pulsar map, the uranium sample, or the fifty-five spoken greetings for classification equities. The package was assembled, approved by NASA Administrator James Fletcher, and launched.

The most operationally significant comparison is to the classification review process that applies to every other disclosure of national defense information by a federal agency. When the Department of Defense publishes a press release containing a photograph of a military installation, the photograph undergoes a pre-publication security review by the Defense Office of Prepublication and Security Review. When a government employee writes a memoir, the manuscript is submitted for classification review under Standard Form 312, the Classified Information Nondisclosure Agreement. When the Central Intelligence Agency declassifies historical documents, each page is reviewed line by line for currently classified information.

When NASA launched a phonograph record containing photographs of American infrastructure, a map to Earth, and a sample of fissionable source material toward an unidentified foreign recipient, no review of any kind was conducted. The contents were selected by a committee of six civilians, approved by a single administrator, and the spacecraft departed the launch pad at Cape Canaveral on schedule. The pre-launch security review consisted of nothing.

VII. The Ongoing Transmission

Unlike a conventional espionage disclosure, in which the compromised material reaches its recipient and the transmission concludes, the Voyager disclosure is ongoing. As of June 2026, Voyager 1 is approximately 25.8 billion kilometers (16 billion miles) from Earth, traveling at approximately 17.1 kilometers per second (38,000 miles per hour) toward a point in the constellation Ophiuchus.9 It crossed the heliopause and entered interstellar space on August 25, 2012, making it the first government vehicle carrying classified material to leave the sovereign territory of the solar system.17

Voyager 2 is approximately 21.4 billion kilometers (13.3 billion miles) from Earth, also in interstellar space, traveling in a different direction. The two spacecraft are diverging, which means the unauthorized disclosure is expanding in two directions simultaneously. The radius of the compromise zone increases by approximately 540 million kilometers per year, per vehicle.

NASA continues to operate both spacecraft through the Deep Space Network, a global array of radio antennas maintained jointly by NASA and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Commands are transmitted to Voyager 1 with a round-trip signal time of approximately 46 hours.18 The agency has not used this communication channel to transmit a recall order, a destruction directive, or an instruction to eject the record into the interstellar medium. It has instead used the channel to manage the spacecraft’s dwindling power supply in an effort to extend the mission—and the disclosure—for as long as possible.

NASA projects that Voyager 1 will approach within 1.7 light-years of the star Gliese 445, currently in the constellation Camelopardalis, in approximately the year 40,272.19 The record will survive this transit. It is designed to last approximately one billion years in the interstellar medium.8 This represents the longest sustained unauthorized disclosure in the history of federal record-keeping by a factor of approximately twenty million.

VIII. Comparative Sentencing

Private First Class Chelsea Manning was convicted by court-martial in 2013 of violations of the Espionage Act for transmitting approximately 251,287 United States diplomatic cables, over 400,000 classified army reports from the Iraq War, and approximately 90,000 army reports from the war in Afghanistan to WikiLeaks. The material was classified up to the SECRET level. Manning was sentenced to thirty-five years of imprisonment.20

Edward Snowden was charged in 2013 with two counts of violating the Espionage Act and one count of theft of government property for transmitting an estimated 1.5 million classified documents from the National Security Agency to journalists. He fled to Russia, where he was granted asylum and later citizenship. The charges carry a maximum combined sentence of thirty years.21

The Voyager Golden Record contains 115 photographs, a galactic map, fifty-five spoken greetings, twenty-seven musical compositions, an audio essay, printed messages from the President of the United States and the Secretary-General of the United Nations, a uranium-238 sample, and diagrammatic instructions for decoding all of it. The number of individual items is smaller than Manning’s or Snowden’s disclosures. The scope is larger. Manning disclosed the contents of diplomatic cables. Snowden disclosed the architecture of surveillance programs. The Sagan committee disclosed the location of the planet.

Under Section 793, each count carries a maximum sentence of ten years. The Golden Record contains, at a minimum, 115 photographs (115 counts), one map (1 count), one instrument (the record itself, 1 count), and one appliance (the uranium sample, 1 count). The theoretical maximum sentence is 1,180 years. The committee had six members. Under Section 793(g), “if two or more persons conspire to violate any of the foregoing provisions of this section, and one or more of such persons do any act to effect the object of the conspiracy, each of the parties to such conspiracy shall be subject to the punishment provided for the offense which is the object of such conspiracy.”1 Four of the six committee members are still alive. Carl Sagan died on December 20, 1996, at the age of sixty-two. Frank Drake, who designed the pulsar map, died on September 2, 2022, at the age of ninety-two. Their deaths have somewhat complicated the enforcement landscape but do not extinguish the conspiracy charge against the surviving members.

NASA Administrator James Fletcher, who approved the project, died on December 22, 1991. President Carter, whose printed message is physically present on the record and who signed the authorization for the Voyager mission, is one hundred and one years old. The statute of limitations for espionage offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 794 is ten years from the date of the violation, but the Voyager disclosure is a continuing offense. The record has not been retrieved. The transmission has not been terminated. New interstellar territory is being compromised at a rate of 540 million kilometers per year. The statute of limitations has not begun to run because the violation has not stopped.

IX. Conclusion

The disclosure is comprehensive. It includes photographs of American infrastructure, the electromagnetic signature of American industrial and transportation systems, the genetic blueprint of the American population, a map to the American homeland precise enough to locate it from any point in the galaxy, a sample of fissionable material confirming that the United States possesses nuclear technology, and instructions in symbolic language sufficient for any technologically capable recipient to decode all of it.

The recipient is unknown. The Sagan committee designed the record to be intercepted by an entity of unknown capability, unknown allegiance, and unknown intent. No risk assessment was conducted. No counterintelligence review was performed. No classification determination was made. The committee assembled the most complete intelligence package ever produced by an American government program and mounted it on the exterior of a spacecraft with no more security review than a luggage tag.

The transmission is ongoing. Voyager 1 has been carrying the disclosure into interstellar space for forty-nine years, is currently 16 billion miles from the nearest Department of Justice field office, and is accelerating beyond the practical reach of any retrieval mission that existing or foreseeable propulsion technology could execute. The record is designed to survive one billion years. Even if the Department of Justice opened an investigation tomorrow morning, the evidence would continue its outbound trajectory at 17.1 kilometers per second for the duration of the investigation, the trial, the appeal, and the heat death of the federal court system.

Manning transmitted 741,000 classified documents to a website and received thirty-five years. Snowden transmitted 1.5 million classified documents to journalists and faces thirty years. The Sagan committee transmitted the location of the planet, the anatomy of its dominant species, and a sample of its nuclear materials to an unknown recipient at a velocity that precludes recovery, and received a Peabody Award.

The Espionage Act does not contain an exception for good intentions. It does not contain an exception for scientific curiosity. It does not contain an exception for projects that seem, in the optimistic assessment of their creators, unlikely to cause harm. It contains a prohibition on the willful transmission of national defense information to any person not entitled to receive it. The Golden Record was willfully transmitted. Its contents relate to the national defense. Its recipient is not entitled to receive it, primarily because its recipient has not been identified, has not been vetted, and may not exist. The violation is ongoing, the evidence is leaving the solar system, and the Department of Justice has not opened a file.
