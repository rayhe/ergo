# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Bird Nests as Unpermitted Structures"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: long factual headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from code to structure to grid to demolition permit to enforcement void, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly: the code's permit and occupancy requirements, the structures' scale, the grid damage, the federal demolition-permit regime, the definitional void, the conclusion. The load-bearing beam is the demolition-permit inversion, that this is the only construction industry in which demolition is federally regulated and construction was never permitted, and it holds through all six sections. The garden-shed exemption beat in Section I, the 71-square-foot footprint against the 120-square-foot limit, is the piece's sharpest single observation. Minor weakness: Section V's definitional close echoes the cicada piece's Section V structurally; and the headline's final clause ("either permit") slightly undersells the two-permit architecture the body develops.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the cicada and mosquito pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "The exemption is the only section of the building code that the birds comply with, and they comply with it by accident," "In Florida it is a Tuesday," "It was built with a beak," "irony is not a listed management method," "the building official has no form for a tree," "the jurisdiction's largest structures are in the trees." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is "the birds appear to have read it," which lands as straight-faced procedural suspicion. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes, regulations, and code sections cited are real and quoted verbatim where quoted: IBC 105.1's permit requirement, IBC 111.1's occupancy prohibition, IBC 105.2's 120-square-foot accessory-structure exemption, all from the International Code Council's official code text; the Fish and Wildlife Service's 2025 MBPM-2 nest memorandum on in-use versus inactive nests; 50 CFR 22.27's eagle nest take permits; Eversource's Special Purpose Utility Permit terms. Biological and engineering facts verified: the St. Petersburg eagle nest (9.5 ft, 20 ft deep, almost 3 tons), the 34-year Vermilion, Ohio nest (2.7 m, 3.6 m, ~2 metric tons), osprey nest dimensions and pole preferences, monk parakeet nest weights and the Audubon-sourced 300-pound figure, and every outage and cost figure. Three pre-publication corrections were applied during drafting: an inaccurate mid-size-sedan weight comparison was removed, unverified "millions of builders" phrasing was softened, and the 50-state IBC adoption claim was verified against ICC and footnoted. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that nests are unpermitted structures under the building code, is the disclosed conceit; the piece is honest about the definitional escape hatch, birds are not owners or authorized agents, in the same way prior pieces disclosed theirs. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"Birds have never filed for a building permit" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone. The garden-shed exemption beat, a three-ton nest qualifying as a shed on floor area, is the single most shareable paragraph; "In Florida it is a Tuesday" and "It was built with a beak" are strong pull-quote material. The hero image, an inspector holding a stop-work tag beneath an osprey nest on a live utility pole, is built for the thumbnail. Slightly less universally graspable than the mosquito premise because the setup requires one paragraph of building-code exposition, the deck does that work, but the headline carries the full burden well. Very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The code citations are accurate: 105.1's "any owner or owner's authorized agent" language quoted verbatim from the ICC's published text, 111.1's occupancy prohibition, 105.2's exemption schedule. The MBTA framework is accurately summarized: in-use nests (viable eggs through fledging) require authorization for purposeful take; inactive nests may be destroyed without a permit but may not be relocated without authorization because relocation is possession. The eagle regime is accurately described: 50 CFR Part 22 nest take permits on Form 3-200-72 with 30-day reporting, possession otherwise prohibited without a separate permit. Eversource's permit terms are accurately rendered. The ICC's 50-state adoption claim is verified and footnoted. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that a nest is a "structure" within the meaning of the building code, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends the IBC defines nests as structures. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: the ICC's official code text for all three code sections, fws.gov for the 2025 nest memorandum, govinfo.gov for the eagle-permit regulation, aphis.usda.gov for the monk parakeet management fact sheet, the Maryland Department of Natural Resources for BGE's Osprey Watch figures, the University of Connecticut for the Florida outage and removal economics, the Oakland Zoo and the Stewards of the Upper Mississippi River Refuge for the nest records, and reputable regional press (Columbus Dispatch, Beacon Journal, WCMU) for the utility incidents. Minor weakness: the nest-record figures rely on secondary wildlife sources rather than a single primary measurement record, which is the nature of the available evidence and is transparently attributed throughout.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 9.5 ft diameter, 20 ft deep, almost 3 tons; 34 years, 2.7 m across, 3.6 m deep, ~2 metric tons; up-to-3-foot osprey nests; 300-plus-pound parakeet condominiums; the computed 71-square-foot footprint against the 120-square-foot exemption; ~12 osprey outages per year; 5,000 customers in Pickerington; a 60-foot replacement pole; 18,000 animal protection units; 198 outages in five months, 10,000 people dark, $4.7 million, 3,000 nests removed; $585,000 in 2001; $125,000 and 200-plus birds in Connecticut; 3,000 people for four hours in New Jersey. The comparison framework, permitted human construction versus the unpermitted avian sector on every metric, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
