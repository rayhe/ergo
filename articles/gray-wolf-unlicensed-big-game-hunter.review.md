# Ergo Review: Gray Wolf as the Largest Unlicensed Big-Game Hunting Operation in the Northern Rockies

**Topic:** The gray wolf (*Canis lupus*), as an unlicensed big-game hunter operating in violation of state licensing statutes and the Lacey Act's prohibition on wildlife taken in violation of state law: no license, no season, no bag limit, no 24-hour harvest reporting, no skull-and-hide check-in, no Pittman-Robertson excise contribution, and methods no licensed human hunter may use, set against Montana's 452-animal quota on humans who hunt wolves.
**Author:** Kit (with Ray)
**Date:** 2026-09-17

## Headline
"Title 87 of the Montana Code Annotated Requires Every Person Who Hunts Game Animals to First Obtain the Licenses the Law Prescribes, the Gray Wolf Holds No License of Any Kind, the National Park Service Has Measured the Wolf's Annual Take at Twenty-Two Ungulates Per Animal, Ninety-One Percent of Which Are Elk, a Hundred Wolves Therefore Take Approximately Twenty-Two Hundred Ungulates a Year, No Wolf Has Purchased a Base Hunting License, a Conservation License, or a Wolf Tag, No Wolf Has Reported a Harvest Within Twenty-Four Hours, No Wolf Has Presented a Skull or Hide to the Department Within Ten Days, No Wolf Has Paid the Eleven Percent Federal Excise Tax on Sporting Arms and Ammunition That Has Funded American Wildlife Conservation Since 1937, the Boone and Crockett Club Defines Fair Chase as Pursuit That Gives the Hunter No Improper Advantage Over the Game, the Wolf Hunts in Packs, at Night, by Relentless Pursuit to Exhaustion, Montana Maintains a Four-Hundred-Fifty-Two-Animal Quota on Humans Who Hunt Wolves and No Quota Whatsoever on Wolves Who Hunt Elk, and the Largest Unlicensed Big-Game Hunting Operation in the Northern Rockies Has Never Filed a Single Report"

## Thesis
Montana's game code requires licenses for hunting, mandates 24-hour reporting and 10-day skull-and-hide check-in for wolves taken by humans, and the state sells wolf licenses while capping the human wolf harvest at a 452-animal quota. The gray wolf itself hunts an estimated 18 to 22 elk per animal per year at the measured Yellowstone rate, in every season, with no bag limit, no license, no report, and no excise-tax contribution, using pack pursuit at night to exhaustion, methods the fair-chase code forbids to licensed humans. Congress proved in 2011 it can legislate wolves directly and chose delisting over licensing.

## The Deliberately Wrong Conclusion
Wolves are wildlife, not "persons" under the game codes; the licensing regime was written for human hunters; a wolf cannot obtain a Social Security number or photo ID, which Montana requires for a conservation license. The article discloses this defense explicitly in Section XI, then overrules it: the statutes prohibit the act of hunting without a license, the wolf performs the act, and Montana's own wolf-specific regulations prove the state knows how to write rules for wolves and chose to write them only for the hunting of wolves, never for the hunting by wolves.

## Research Notes
- Montana Code Annotated, Title 87, chapter 2: license required to hunt game animals; hunting without one is a violation. Montana FWP conservation license requires last four digits of SSN plus valid driver's license or photo ID (fwp.mt.gov).
- Montana 2021 wolf regulations (FWP): wolf license $12 resident / $15 nonresident, separate license per wolf, harvest reported within 24 hours, skulls and hides presented to FWP within 10 days, over-the-counter tags (fwp.mt.gov PDF).
- Montana 2025/2026 wolf regulations: statewide quota of 452 wolves; individual may harvest 15 via hunting and 15 via trapping; separate quotas of 3 each in WMUs 313 and 316 (westernmt.news; northernag.net).
- Montana nonresident Elk Combo license $1,078 (gohunt.com).
- NPS Yellowstone Science (Ten Years of Yellowstone Wolves): EIS predicted 12 ungulates/wolf/year; measured kill rates "closer to 22 ungulates per wolf per year," i.e., 2,200 ungulates for 100 wolves; Smith et al.: 1.6-2.2 elk/wolf/30 days in winter, elk 91% of kills, calves 43%/cows 36%/bulls 21% (nps.gov; arc.lib.montana.edu).
- Santa Clara University page citing NPS 2022: 18-22 elk killed per wolf per year; wolf body mass males 110 lb, females 90 lb (webpages.scu.edu).
- Reintroduction: 66 gray wolves from Canada released 1995 in Idaho and near Yellowstone; 1,651 wolves across the Rocky Mountain region at delisting (phys.org, Sierra Club).
- 2011 delisting: Section 1713, Department of Defense and Full-Year Continuing Appropriations Act, 2011 (P.L. 112-10), reinstating the 2009 delisting rule; first time in history Congress removed a species from the ESA by legislation (congress.gov CRS R47581; e360.yale.edu; earthjustice.org).
- Pittman-Robertson Act (1937): 11% excise on long guns and ammunition, 10% on handguns; $944,007,497 from firearm/ammunition makers in 2023; over $25 billion inflation-adjusted since 1937; elk numbered ~41,000 at inception (nssf.org).
- Lacey Act, 16 U.S.C. 3372(a)(2)(A): unlawful to import, export, transport, sell, receive, acquire, or purchase fish or wildlife taken, possessed, transported, or sold in violation of any state law; 16 U.S.C. 3373: civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation; criminal penalties up to $20,000 and five years imprisonment where market value exceeds $350.
- Fair chase: Boone and Crockett Club, founded by Theodore Roosevelt 1887; defines fair chase as "the ethical, sportsmanlike, and lawful pursuit and taking of any free-ranging wild big game animal in a manner that does not give the hunter an improper advantage over the game animals"; registered trademark; taught in mandatory hunter certification courses (boone-crockett.org).
- Computed ratios: 1,651 wolves x ~20 elk/yr = ~33,000 elk/yr at the measured rate; Montana nonresident Elk Combo $1,078 per elk-tag-equivalent; wolf license $12 resident = 1.1% of the elk combo price.
