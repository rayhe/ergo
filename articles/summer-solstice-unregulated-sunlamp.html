Product Safety Regulation & Solar Astrophysics

21 CFR 1040.20 Requires a Black-Box Carcinogen Warning on Every Device That Emits Ultraviolet Radiation to Tan Human Skin. The Sun Emits Ultraviolet Radiation. It Tans Human Skin. The Summer Solstice Is Its Annual Peak Output Day. No Warning Has Ever Been Issued.

The FDA’s sunlamp performance standard requires that every UV-emitting tanning product bear a black-box warning and undergo premarket review as a Class II medical device. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies both indoor tanning devices and solar radiation as Group 1 carcinogens, meaning “carcinogenic to humans.” On June 21, 2026, the summer solstice, the sun will deliver approximately 15 hours of continuous ultraviolet radiation to 331 million Americans. The FDA has regulated the 15-minute indoor version since 2014. The 15-hour outdoor original has operated without a product label for 4.6 billion years.

By Thomas Reinhardt · June 21, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Performance Standard

Title 21, Code of Federal Regulations, Section 1040.20 sets forth the performance standard for “sunlamp products and ultraviolet lamps intended for use in sunlamp products.”1 The regulation was promulgated under the authority of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, Sections 531 through 542, which grant the FDA jurisdiction over “electronic products” that emit radiation.2

In 2014, the FDA reclassified sunlamp products from Class I to Class II medical devices, requiring premarket notification (510(k) clearance) and special controls.3 The special controls include a requirement that every sunlamp product bear the following warning, in a black-box format, prominently displayed on the device: “Attention: This sunlamp product should not be used on persons under the age of 18 years.”4

The regulation specifies maximum timer intervals, mandatory protective eyewear requirements, maximum UV irradiance limits per exposure session, and detailed labeling including the recommended exposure schedule and the distance from the UV source at which the recommended exposure is calculated.5 The FDA also requires that certain promotional materials for sunlamp products include specific warnings about the risks of skin cancer, premature skin aging, and eye damage.6

The standard applies to any product designed to emit ultraviolet radiation for the purpose of tanning human skin. It does not apply to the sun.

The sun emits ultraviolet radiation. It tans human skin. The regulatory distinction between the two rests on the word “product.”

II. The Device

The sun is a G-type main-sequence star (G2V) located approximately 93 million miles from Earth. It has a core temperature of approximately 15 million degrees Celsius and a surface (photospheric) temperature of approximately 5,778 Kelvin.7 It has been in continuous operation for approximately 4.6 billion years. Its projected operational lifespan is another 5 billion years. No maintenance has ever been performed.

The sun emits electromagnetic radiation across the entire spectrum, from radio waves to gamma rays. Of particular regulatory interest is the ultraviolet (UV) portion: UV-A (315 to 400 nanometers), UV-B (280 to 315 nanometers), and UV-C (100 to 280 nanometers).8 The Earth’s ozone layer absorbs virtually all UV-C and a substantial portion of UV-B, but transmits most UV-A and a biologically significant fraction of UV-B to the surface.9

A typical tanning bed contains 24 to 60 fluorescent lamps emitting UV-A radiation at a peak wavelength of approximately 350 nanometers, with UV-B comprising 0.5 to 5 percent of total output depending on the lamp type.10 The sun emits the same wavelengths in the same spectral bands at intensities that, at the Earth’s surface on a clear summer day, exceed those of a standard tanning bed.

The solar UV irradiance at the Earth’s surface at solar noon on the summer solstice at latitude 40°N (approximately New York, Denver, Salt Lake City) is roughly 40 to 60 watts per square meter in the UV-A band and 1.5 to 2.5 watts per square meter in the UV-B band.11 A commercial tanning bed delivers UV-A at approximately 25 to 50 watts per square meter and UV-B at 0.2 to 1.5 watts per square meter over the body’s surface area.12

The sun is, by any photometric measure, a more powerful UV-emitting device than a tanning bed. The tanning bed requires a 510(k) premarket notification. The sun does not.

III. The Peak Operating Day

The summer solstice of 2026 will occur on Sunday, June 21, at 4:24 a.m. Eastern Time, when the sun reaches its maximum northern declination of 23.44 degrees above the celestial equator.13 On this date, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the sun at its maximum seasonal angle, producing the longest period of daylight and the highest peak solar altitude of the year for all locations north of the equator.

The hours of daylight on June 21, 2026, vary by latitude. At 30°N (Houston, New Orleans, Jacksonville), the day will be approximately 14 hours and 3 minutes. At 40°N (New York, Denver, Columbus), approximately 15 hours and 1 minute. At 47°N (Seattle, Minneapolis, Portland), approximately 15 hours and 53 minutes.14 At the Arctic Circle (66.5°N), the sun will not set at all. This constitutes a 24-hour continuous UV exposure window.

The UV Index, a standard measure of the strength of UV radiation at the Earth’s surface jointly developed by the World Health Organization, the United Nations Environment Programme, and the World Meteorological Organization, is scaled from 0 (nighttime) to 11+ (“Extreme”).15 On the summer solstice, UV Index values of 8 to 10 (“Very High”) are typical at solar noon across most of the continental United States, with values of 11 or higher (“Extreme”) common in the southern states and at higher elevations.16

The EPA defines a UV Index of 8 or higher as the threshold at which “unprotected skin and eyes can burn in minutes.”17 On the summer solstice, this threshold is exceeded for approximately five to seven hours in most U.S. locations, from mid-morning through mid-afternoon.

A tanning bed session typically lasts 10 to 20 minutes.18 The summer solstice lasts 15 hours. The solstice is not a tanning session. It is a shift.

IV. The Dosimetry

The erythemal dose is the standard measure of UV radiation exposure sufficient to produce reddening (erythema) of the skin, commonly known as sunburn. One minimal erythemal dose (MED) for a fair-skinned individual (Fitzpatrick Skin Type II) is approximately 200 to 250 joules per square meter of erythemally weighted UV radiation.19

A single commercial tanning bed session delivers approximately 1 to 3 MEDs, depending on exposure time and lamp output.20 The FDA’s performance standard for sunlamp products establishes maximum timer intervals and recommended exposure schedules specifically to control the per-session erythemal dose.

On the summer solstice at 40°N latitude, a fair-skinned individual standing outdoors unprotected would accumulate approximately 1 MED in 15 to 25 minutes during peak UV hours (10 a.m. to 2 p.m.).21 Over the full 15-hour daylight period, assuming variable solar angles and atmospheric conditions, the total accumulated unprotected erythemal exposure would be approximately 30 to 50 MEDs.22

Thirty to fifty MEDs is, for context, the equivalent of 15 to 25 consecutive tanning bed sessions delivered without interruption. The tanning bed requires a timer, a warning label, protective eyewear, and a 510(k) premarket notification for each of those sessions. The sun delivers them all at once, on a Sunday, for free, to anyone who steps outside. No timer. No eyewear. No label. No premarket notification. The dosimetry is not in dispute. Only the regulatory jurisdiction is.

V. The IARC Classification

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), an agency of the World Health Organization, maintains the definitive classification system for carcinogenic hazards. The system uses four groups: Group 1 (“carcinogenic to humans”), Group 2A (“probably carcinogenic”), Group 2B (“possibly carcinogenic”), and Group 3 (“not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity”).23

In 2009, the IARC elevated the classification of UV-emitting tanning devices from Group 2A (“probably carcinogenic to humans”) to Group 1 (“carcinogenic to humans”), based on a comprehensive review of the epidemiological evidence linking indoor tanning to melanoma and other skin cancers.24

Solar radiation has been classified as Group 1 since 1992.25

That chronology warrants examination. The sun was recognized as carcinogenic to humans seventeen years before the devices designed to replicate its effects received the same classification. The imitator was presumed less dangerous than the original for nearly two decades, during which time the FDA regulated neither. When the IARC finally classified both in the same group, the FDA moved to regulate the imitator. It did not revisit the original.

Both are Group 1. Both are “carcinogenic to humans.” Both emit UV-A and UV-B radiation that causes the same DNA damage (cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and 6-4 photoproducts) through the same photochemical mechanism in the same target tissue.26 The carcinogenicity classification is identical. The regulatory response is not.

VI. The Casualties

The American Cancer Society estimates that approximately 112,000 new melanomas will be diagnosed in the United States in 2026, with approximately 8,510 deaths.27 Melanoma accounts for only about 1 percent of skin cancers but causes a large majority of skin cancer deaths.28 Including basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, the total number of new skin cancer diagnoses in the United States exceeds 5 million per year.29

The WHO estimates that between 2 and 3 million non-melanoma skin cancers and approximately 132,000 melanoma skin cancers occur globally each year, with ultraviolet radiation identified as the primary environmental risk factor.30

The FDA cited these health risks as the justification for reclassifying tanning beds as Class II medical devices in 2014. The agency noted that UV radiation from tanning devices is “more dangerous than previously thought” and that exposure “can cause skin cancer, skin burns, premature skin aging, and eye damage.”31

The precise share of skin cancers attributable to indoor tanning versus solar exposure is a subject of ongoing epidemiological study. A 2014 meta-analysis published in the British Medical Journal estimated that approximately 3,234 melanoma cases, 2,085 squamous cell carcinomas, and 168,000 basal cell carcinomas per year in the United States could be attributed to indoor tanning.32 This would mean that indoor tanning accounts for approximately 3 percent of melanoma cases. Solar radiation, by exclusion, accounts for the remainder of UV-attributable cases.

The 3 percent is regulated. The remainder is not. The regulatory attention is inversely proportional to the epidemiological burden. This is, at minimum, an unusual allocation of enforcement resources.

VII. The Market

There are approximately 27,318 tanning salons operating in the United States as of 2025, generating approximately $1.9 billion in annual revenue.33 The industry has been in decline: the number of salons fell 4.5 percent per year on average between 2020 and 2025.34 Indoor tanning prevalence among adults aged 18 to 34 dropped from 14.3 percent in 2007 to 4.0 percent in 2018.35

This decline coincides precisely with increased regulatory burden. The 2014 reclassification introduced 510(k) premarket review requirements, mandatory black-box warnings, proposed age restrictions, and a risk acknowledgement certification process. Compliance costs increased. Consumer warnings expanded. Market participation contracted.

Meanwhile, the sun has continued to operate at full capacity. Its market share in the UV tanning sector is, and has always been, effectively 100 percent of outdoor exposure. It faces no premarket review. It carries no warning labels. It has no age restriction. It charges no admission fee. It is available in every U.S. jurisdiction without a business license. It operates on the summer solstice for 15 hours straight.

The tanning salon industry, at $1.9 billion, is the only commercial competitor to the sun in the UV exposure market. It is also the only participant in that market subject to federal product safety regulation. The other participant, which produces the overwhelming majority of UV-related health outcomes, operates with complete regulatory impunity on its most productive day of the year.

VIII. The Warning Label

21 CFR 1040.20(d)(1)(i) requires the following label on all sunlamp products: “DANGER: Ultraviolet radiation. Follow instructions. Avoid overexposure. As with natural sunlight, overexposure can cause eye and skin injury and allergic reactions. Repeated exposure may cause premature aging of the skin and skin cancer.”36

The regulation contains the phrase “as with natural sunlight.”

The FDA requires a product to carry a warning that compares the product’s dangers to those of natural sunlight, while not requiring natural sunlight to carry a warning that compares its dangers to those of the product. The warning label on a tanning bed cites the sun as the benchmark hazard. The benchmark hazard has no warning label of its own. The reference standard to which all product warnings appeal is itself unregulated.

If the FDA were to apply 21 CFR 1040.20 to the sun using the same criteria it applies to a ProSun SunDream or a Wolff System tanning bed, the required label would need to be permanently affixed to an exterior surface of the product so as to be legible and readily accessible to view when the product is fully assembled for use.37 The sun is fully assembled. It is approximately 864,000 miles in diameter. The label would need to be very large.

The practical difficulties are acknowledged. The jurisdictional question is not whether a label can be affixed to the sun. The jurisdictional question is why the same agency that considers UV radiation dangerous enough to require a black-box warning on a device that emits it for 15 minutes does not consider it dangerous when the same radiation is emitted by a different source for 15 hours.

IX. The Vitamin D Defense

The most commonly invoked justification for the sun’s regulatory exemption is the production of vitamin D. UV-B radiation catalyzes the synthesis of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) in the skin, a process that is essential for calcium absorption and bone health.38 The National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements recommends 600 to 800 IU of vitamin D per day for most adults, a level that can be achieved through approximately 10 to 30 minutes of midday sun exposure on the face, arms, and legs two to three times per week, depending on latitude, skin type, and season.39

Ten to thirty minutes. The summer solstice delivers 15 hours. The therapeutic window is approximately 0.5 to 3.3 percent of the available exposure duration. The remaining 96.7 to 99.5 percent of the day’s UV output has no vitamin D justification whatsoever. The body reaches a vitamin D synthesis plateau within approximately 20 to 30 minutes, after which continued UV exposure produces no additional vitamin D but continues to produce DNA damage.40

The vitamin D defense, applied to the summer solstice, is the equivalent of arguing that a bottle of whiskey should be unregulated because one tablespoon is used in a cough remedy. The therapeutic dose does not justify the total output. The FDA has historically understood this distinction when applied to any product that is not the sun.

X. The ACA’s Quiet Admission

The Affordable Care Act of 2010, Section 10907, imposed a 10 percent excise tax on indoor tanning services.41 The tax was projected to generate approximately $200 million per year in revenue.42 The legislative rationale explicitly cited the health risks of indoor UV exposure and the goal of reducing usage, particularly among young people.

This is notable for two reasons. First, Congress recognized UV tanning as sufficiently hazardous to warrant a Pigouvian tax, a tax designed to correct a negative externality by increasing the cost of the harmful activity. Second, Congress applied this tax exclusively to the indoor version of the activity while leaving the outdoor version completely untaxed.

An American who pays $30 for a 15-minute tanning bed session pays $3 in federal excise tax for the privilege of receiving a controlled dose of Group 1 carcinogenic radiation in a facility equipped with timers, protective eyewear, warning labels, and a certified operator. An American who lies on a beach for 15 hours on the summer solstice, receiving 30 to 50 times the erythemal dose, in an uncontrolled setting with no timer, no eyewear, no label, and no operator, pays zero.

The tax does not distinguish between UV photons based on their source. The tax distinguishes between UV photons based on whether they arrive through a roof. This is a construction standard, not a health standard.

XI. The Copernican Problem

The Radiological Health Program of the FDA’s Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) has jurisdiction over “electronic products” that emit radiation. Section 531(2) of the FFDCA defines an “electronic product” as “any manufactured or assembled product (or component, part, or accessory of such product) which, when in operation: (i) contains or acts as part of an electronic circuit and (ii) emits (or in the absence of effective shielding or other controls would emit) electronic product radiation.”43

The sun is not a manufactured or assembled product. It formed through gravitational collapse of a molecular cloud approximately 4.6 billion years ago.44 Gravitational collapse is not manufacturing. Nuclear fusion is not an electronic circuit. The jurisdictional exemption of the sun from 21 CFR 1040.20 rests on these distinctions.

The distinctions are real. The question is whether they should matter.

The Radiological Health program also regulates laser products (21 CFR 1040.10), which include natural-origin lasers used in industrial and medical settings. The regulatory framework does not exempt a laser from compliance requirements because the laser medium was mined from the Earth rather than manufactured in a laboratory. The relevant criterion is whether the device emits regulated radiation, not whether the device was assembled by a person or by gravity.

The sun emits regulated radiation. It emits it more intensely, more broadly, and for longer duration than any product subject to 21 CFR 1040.20. It emits it to a larger population. It has been emitting it for longer than any product manufacturer has existed. The regulatory framework exempts it because it was not “manufactured or assembled,” but the public health consequences of its emissions are indifferent to the production method.

XII. The Trade Protection Hypothesis

Consider the following facts in sequence.

The sun is the dominant supplier of UV radiation for human skin tanning. It holds what, in any antitrust analysis, would constitute a monopoly position: it supplies approximately 97 percent of all UV-related skin cancer outcomes and charges no direct fee, making competitive entry by other UV providers structurally disadvantaged from the outset.45

Beginning in the 1970s and 1980s, the indoor tanning industry emerged as the first viable commercial alternative to solar UV exposure, offering a controlled, year-round, weather-independent tanning service. Industry revenue grew from negligible to $5 billion annually at its peak in the mid-2000s, with more than 50,000 locations nationwide.46

In response, the FDA in 2014 imposed Class II medical device classification on the commercial competitor, requiring premarket review, black-box warnings, proposed age restrictions, and a compliance infrastructure that increased operating costs by an estimated 20 to 30 percent for small operators. Congress simultaneously imposed a 10 percent excise tax on the competitor’s services. The competitor’s market contracted by approximately 45 percent in the decade that followed. The monopolist was not subject to any of these measures.

In conventional trade economics, this pattern has a name. When a dominant supplier faces no regulatory burden while its competitor is subjected to classification requirements, premarket review, mandatory warnings, and excise taxation, the effect is functionally identical to a protective tariff. The competitor’s costs increase. The competitor’s market share declines. The monopolist’s position is reinforced.

The stated justification for the differential treatment is public health. But the public health data runs in the opposite direction: the unregulated monopolist produces approximately 97 percent of the UV-attributable cancer burden, while the regulated competitor produces approximately 3 percent. If public health were the organizing principle, the regulatory burden would be allocated proportionally to the harm produced. It is not. It is allocated exclusively to the competitor.

The summer solstice is the monopolist’s annual peak production day. On June 21, 2026, the sun will deliver more UV radiation to more Americans over a longer period of time than the entire indoor tanning industry will deliver in a month. Not one photon of that output will carry a warning label. Not one minute of that exposure will require protective eyewear. Not one participant will be required to sign a risk acknowledgement certification.

The tanning bed down the street, which delivers a smaller dose of the same radiation from the same part of the electromagnetic spectrum with the same carcinogenic classification, will bear a black box and charge a 10 percent tax for the service.

XIII. The Father’s Day Coincidence

In 2026, the summer solstice falls on Sunday, June 21, which is also Father’s Day. The National Retail Federation projects that Americans will spend approximately $22.9 billion on Father’s Day gifts in 2026.47 A substantial category of Father’s Day activity involves outdoor recreation: backyard barbecues, golf, fishing, and beach outings.

On the longest, most UV-intense day of the year, the American economy encourages millions of fathers to spend extended time outdoors, in direct exposure to an unregulated Group 1 carcinogen, as a form of celebration.

The promotional apparatus of Father’s Day does not include cancer risk disclosures. No Hallmark card carries a UV Index warning. No Home Depot grill advertisement includes the recommended exposure schedule that 21 CFR 1040.20(d)(1)(ii) would require if the celebration involved a tanning bed instead of a patio. The day is, from a radiation dosimetry perspective, the single worst day of the calendar year to encourage sustained outdoor activity. It is marketed as a gift.

Whether this constitutes a regulatory failure, a cultural oversight, or a seasonally optimized distribution strategy for the solar UV monopolist is left as an exercise for the reader.

XIV. Conclusion

The FDA’s performance standard for sunlamp products rests on a simple causal chain. Ultraviolet radiation causes skin cancer. Devices that emit ultraviolet radiation for the purpose of tanning should therefore carry warnings, undergo premarket review, and be subject to special controls proportional to the carcinogenic hazard.

Every element of this causal chain applies to the sun with greater force than it applies to a tanning bed. The sun emits more UV radiation, at higher peak intensity, over longer exposure periods, to a vastly larger population, and produces approximately 97 percent of UV-attributable skin cancer outcomes. The IARC classified solar radiation as Group 1 seventeen years before it classified tanning beds the same way. The epidemiological evidence is older, deeper, and more extensive.

The FDA regulates the 3 percent and exempts the 97 percent. Congress taxes the 3 percent and subsidizes the 97 percent with a holiday.

There are 27,318 tanning salons in the United States. Each one carries a black-box warning. Each one requires a 510(k) premarket notification. Each one pays a 10 percent excise tax on services. Each one has watched its market shrink by 45 percent in a decade.

There is one sun. It has no warning label, no premarket clearance, no excise tax, and no age restriction. On June 21, 2026, it will operate for 15 hours at peak annual output, delivering radiation classified by the WHO as “carcinogenic to humans” to every American who walks outside. Its market share has remained at 100 percent of outdoor UV exposure for 4.6 billion years. Its regulatory burden is zero.

The tanning industry is the only competitor the sun has ever had. The federal government has spent twelve years systematically regulating that competitor out of the market while the monopolist operates under a blanket exemption justified by the fact that it is very old and very large and was not manufactured.

This is either the most significant gap in radiation safety regulation since the FFDCA was amended to cover electronic products, or it is the most effective trade protection scheme in the history of federal regulation: a $1.9 billion industry destroyed by compliance costs while its $0 competitor delivers the same product at 30 times the dose, 15 times the duration, and zero times the regulatory overhead.

Happy summer solstice. Wear sunscreen. The sun won’t remind you. It doesn’t have to.
