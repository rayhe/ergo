Environmental Regulatory Law & Atmospheric Geochemistry

The Clean Air Act’s Own National Ambient Air Quality Standards and the EPA’s Own Exceptional Events Rule Classify the Annual Saharan Dust Plume as the Largest Unregulated Transboundary Pollutant Import in American History, and the Agency’s Response Has Been to Build a Formal Bureaucratic Process for Not Counting It

The Clean Air Act establishes National Ambient Air Quality Standards for particulate matter. The EPA’s own monitoring network documents that Saharan dust events regularly cause American cities to exceed these standards. An estimated 182 million tons of Saharan dust are lofted into the atmosphere annually, carrying bacteria, fungi, and phosphorus across 5,000 miles of ocean into American airspace without a single import declaration, phytosanitary certificate, or air quality permit. The agency created a sixty-six-page final rule, codified at 40 CFR 50.14, establishing a formal procedure by which states may petition to have these violations excluded from their compliance records. The regulatory term for this procedure is “exceptional events.” The event has occurred every year for at least several million years. The agency considers it exceptional every time.

By Marcus Theriault · May 31, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Standards

The Clean Air Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 7401 et seq., requires the Environmental Protection Agency to establish National Ambient Air Quality Standards for pollutants that “cause or contribute to air pollution which may reasonably be anticipated to endanger public health or welfare.”1 Among the six criteria pollutants for which NAAQS have been established, particulate matter is regulated under two size categories: PM10, particles with an aerodynamic diameter of 10 micrometers or less, and PM2.5, particles with a diameter of 2.5 micrometers or less.

The current primary NAAQS for PM2.5, codified at 40 CFR § 50.20, sets the annual standard at 9.0 micrograms per cubic meter and the 24-hour standard at 35 micrograms per cubic meter.2 The primary NAAQS for PM10, codified at 40 CFR § 50.6, sets the 24-hour standard at 150 micrograms per cubic meter.3 These are not advisory thresholds. They are legally binding limits. Under Section 109 of the Clean Air Act, the standards must be set at a level “requisite to protect the public health” with “an adequate margin of safety.”4

When an area’s monitored air quality exceeds these standards, the Clean Air Act triggers a cascade of regulatory consequences. The area may be designated “nonattainment” under Section 107(d). The state must develop a State Implementation Plan under Section 110 demonstrating how it will achieve compliance. Federal highway funding and permits for new pollution sources may be restricted. The consequences are designed to be severe because the standards are designed to represent the outer boundary of what is safe to breathe.

Every summer, an estimated 182 million tons of mineral dust depart the Sahara Desert, cross the Atlantic Ocean, and enter the airspace of the United States and its territories.5 A significant fraction of this material is PM2.5 and PM10. When it arrives, it causes American air quality monitoring stations to record values that exceed the legally binding NAAQS. The violations are real. The monitoring data is accurate. The air is, by the EPA’s own standards, unsafe to breathe. The agency knows this. Its response has been to build a system for pretending it did not happen.

II. The Source

The Sahara Desert occupies approximately 3.6 million square miles of northern Africa, an area slightly smaller than the continental United States.6 It is the world’s largest source of airborne mineral dust. The mechanism is straightforward: strong surface winds, particularly those associated with the Harmattan and the West African monsoon system, lift fine particles of sand, mineral clay, and dried organic sediment into the atmosphere, where they are carried westward by the prevailing easterly trade winds across the Atlantic in a phenomenon known as the Saharan Air Layer.

The primary source region within the Sahara is the Bodélé Depression in northeastern Chad, a topographic low point that was once the bed of a vast paleolake known as Mega-Chad. When the lake dried approximately 6,000 years ago, it left behind billions of tons of diatomaceous earth, rich in silica, iron, and phosphorus from the fossilized remains of freshwater diatoms. This sediment is exceptionally fine-grained and easily aerosolized. The Bodélé Depression alone produces an estimated 56 percent of all Saharan dust emissions, making a dried lakebed in central Chad the single most productive point source of atmospheric particulate matter on the planet.7

NASA’s Cloud-Aerosol Lidar and Infrared Pathfinder Satellite Observation, or CALIPSO, satellite measured this transport in three dimensions from 2007 through 2013. The data show that an average of 182 million tons of dust are carried past the western edge of the Sahara at longitude 15°W each year. Of this volume, 132 million tons remain airborne as the plume crosses longitude 35°W near the eastern coast of South America. Approximately 27.7 million tons are deposited on the Amazon basin. An additional 43 million tons travel past longitude 75°W, reaching the Caribbean Sea and the southeastern United States.5

One hundred and eighty-two million tons. Annually. For context, the total volume of goods imported into the United States through all ports of entry in 2024 was approximately 27.8 million twenty-foot equivalent container units.8 At a typical laden weight of 14 metric tons per container, that represents approximately 389 million tons of imported merchandise. The Saharan dust plume delivers nearly half that tonnage into American airspace every year, and it does not pass through a single port of entry, clear a single customs checkpoint, or file a single piece of documentation with any federal agency.

III. The Composition

The material entering American airspace is not inert sand. Saharan dust is a complex mixture of mineral particles, organic compounds, and biological organisms. The mineral fraction consists primarily of quartz, clay minerals (illite, kaolinite, montmorillonite), feldspars, and iron oxides. The phosphorus content, predominantly in the form of apatite derived from the diatomaceous sediments of the Bodélé Depression, has been quantified by NASA at approximately 22,000 tons per year deposited in the Amazon basin alone.5

The biological fraction is more concerning from a regulatory perspective. Research published in Scientific Reports by Maki et al. documented that bacterial concentrations in the atmosphere increase by a factor of three to ten during Saharan dust events compared to background conditions.9 Studies conducted by the U.S. Geological Survey identified viable bacteria and fungi hitchhiking on dust particles after crossing 5,000 miles of open ocean, including species associated with coral disease in the Caribbean.10 Research published in FEMS Microbiology Letters analyzed metagenomes from five Saharan dust events in the Caribbean collected in 2002 and 2008, finding consistent microbial communities dominated by Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria, with fungi representing a “prominent feature” of the dust-associated microbiome compared to other environmental samples.11

A 2024 study published in Environmental Science: Atmospheres by the Royal Society of Chemistry assessed the conditions favoring survival of African dust-borne microorganisms during trans-Atlantic transport. The researchers confirmed that the Saharan Air Layer, which travels at altitudes of 1,500 to 5,000 meters, provides conditions surprisingly conducive to microbial survival: the dry, warm air desiccates but does not kill many bacterial and fungal species, and UV exposure is partially shielded by the dust particles themselves.12

The import, then, consists of mineral particulates at concentrations sufficient to violate federal air quality standards, phosphorus in quantities sufficient to fertilize the world’s largest rainforest, and a diverse community of viable bacteria and fungi from the soils of north-central Africa. Every component of this import is regulated by at least one federal agency when it arrives in the United States by any other means. When it arrives by wind, no agency claims jurisdiction.

IV. The Violations

The air quality impacts are not hypothetical. They are documented by the EPA’s own monitoring network, the same network whose data form the legal basis for every NAAQS attainment determination in the country.

In June 2020, a Saharan dust plume of unprecedented density crossed the Atlantic, an event that atmospheric scientists and media outlets dubbed the “Godzilla” dust cloud. Preliminary analysis of aerosol optical depth measurements from NASA’s MODIS sensor showed a greater concentration of airborne particulates over the Atlantic on June 20, 2020, than on any day since satellite observations began in 2003. A ground-based AERONET sensor at Ragged Point, Barbados, recorded the highest aerosol optical depth value in the station’s history, dating to 1996.13

Air quality across the Caribbean fell to “hazardous” levels on the EPA’s Air Quality Index. Pablo Méndez Lázaro, an environmental health specialist at the University of Puerto Rico, described it as “the most significant event in the past 50 years” and warned that “conditions are dangerous in many Caribbean islands.” The plume continued into the southeastern United States, degrading air quality across Florida, Texas, and the Gulf Coast states.14

The 2020 event was dramatic, but it was not anomalous in kind. Saharan dust events impact American air quality every summer. The Saharan Air Layer is a seasonal phenomenon, most active between June and October, that has been transporting African dust to the Americas for as long as the Sahara has been a desert. The dust has been arriving in the United States, degrading air quality, depositing biological material, and violating the federal standards designed to protect public health, continuously. What was new in 2020 was not the phenomenon. It was the density.

The event has occurred every year for at least several million years. The EPA considers it exceptional every time.

V. The Exceptional Events Rule

Section 319(b) of the Clean Air Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 7619(b), directs the EPA to promulgate regulations governing the treatment of air quality monitoring data that has been influenced by “exceptional events.” The statute defines an exceptional event as one that “affects air quality,” is “not reasonably controllable or preventable,” is caused by “human activity that is unlikely to recur at a particular location or a natural event,” and is “determined by the Administrator through the process established in the regulations” to meet these criteria.15

The EPA finalized the implementing regulation in 2007 and revised it substantially in 2016. The result is 40 CFR § 50.14, the Exceptional Events Rule, a sixty-six-page regulation establishing a detailed bureaucratic process by which state air quality agencies may petition the EPA to exclude monitored exceedances from their NAAQS compliance determinations.16

The process is not simple. A state must first place a flag on the relevant data record in the Air Quality System database. It must then submit an initial description of the event by July 1 of the following calendar year. It must subsequently prepare and submit a detailed demonstration that includes a narrative conceptual model, a comparison of the event to historical norms, an analysis showing that the event caused the exceedance, and evidence that the state has taken “reasonable measures” to mitigate the public health impact. The EPA then reviews the demonstration, may request additional information, and ultimately issues a concurrence or denial.

The regulation is, in structural terms, a formal procedure for removing violations from the record. It does not prevent the air from being polluted. It does not prevent people from breathing the polluted air. It does not reduce the concentration of particulate matter in the atmosphere by a single microgram. What it does is allow the state in which the violation was recorded to avoid the regulatory consequences that the Clean Air Act attaches to violations. The air was unsafe. The people breathed it. The monitors recorded it. The bureaucratic apparatus then produces a determination that the recording should not count.

Consider the architecture of this regulatory response. The Clean Air Act establishes a standard: air with particulate matter above a specified concentration is unsafe. The monitoring network detects violations of that standard. A natural phenomenon causes those violations on a recurring, predictable, annual basis. And the agency responsible for protecting air quality responds not by addressing the pollution, not by warning the public, not by modifying the standard to reflect reality, but by constructing a multi-step administrative procedure through which the data documenting the unsafe air can be bureaucratically reclassified as though the unsafe air had not occurred.

The word the regulation uses for this phenomenon is “exceptional.” The phenomenon is the annual transport of Saharan dust to the Americas, a process that has been occurring continuously for approximately four to five million years, since the Sahara transitioned from savanna to desert in the late Pliocene.17 In regulatory terminology, an event that has occurred annually for several million years remains “exceptional” as long as the paperwork is filed correctly.

VI. The Import No One Declared

Under 19 U.S.C. § 1484, the importer of any merchandise entering the United States is required to “make entry” with U.S. Customs and Border Protection by filing documentation that includes a description of the merchandise, the country of origin, the quantity, the value, and the classification under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule.18 The requirement is comprehensive. It applies to “any article brought into the United States from a foreign country,” whether the article is commercial goods, personal effects, or, by the plain language of the statute, any article at all.

Forty-three million tons of African mineral dust pass longitude 75°W each year, entering the airspace above the Caribbean and the territorial waters and sovereign land mass of the United States. The material originates in Chad, Niger, Mali, Mauritania, and other Saharan and Sahelian nations. It is produced in a specific geographic region, the Bodélé Depression and surrounding arid lands. It crosses an international boundary. It enters the United States.

No entry has been filed. No customs declaration has been submitted. No duty has been assessed. No importer of record has been identified. The material arrives, is deposited on American soil, American waterways, and American lungs, and the nation’s customs enforcement apparatus processes zero documents because the delivery mechanism is the atmosphere rather than a container ship.

VII. The Biological Cargo

The regulatory gap is most acute for the biological content of the dust. The Plant Protection Act, codified at 7 U.S.C. § 7701 et seq., authorizes the Secretary of Agriculture to “prohibit or restrict the importation, entry, exportation, or movement in interstate commerce of any plant, plant product, biological control organism, noxious weed, article, or means of conveyance, if the Secretary determines that the prohibition or restriction is necessary to prevent the introduction into the United States or the dissemination of a plant pest or noxious weed.”19

USDA’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service administers this authority through detailed regulations at 7 CFR Parts 319 and 330. Under 7 CFR § 330.200, a permit is required for the importation of any “plant pest,” defined broadly to include any organism that can directly or indirectly injure, cause damage to, or cause disease in any plant or plant product. Under 7 CFR § 319.37-5, a written permit is required for the importation of plants for planting, with applications processed by the Plant Protection and Quarantine unit at APHIS headquarters in Riverdale, Maryland.20

Research by the USGS has documented that Saharan dust transports viable fungi associated with disease in Caribbean coral reefs, including Aspergillus sydowii, the causative agent of sea fan disease that has devastated Gorgonia ventalina populations throughout the Caribbean since the 1980s.10 Additional research has identified plant-associated bacteria and soil fungi transported across the Atlantic that, upon deposition, interact with agricultural ecosystems in the receiving regions.

If a researcher at a university wished to import a soil sample containing Aspergillus sydowii from Chad for laboratory study, the importation would require a USDA permit, a biosafety review, containment protocols, and documentation filed with APHIS. If the same organism arrives on a dust particle that has traveled 5,000 miles through the Saharan Air Layer and been deposited on a coral reef in the Florida Keys, no permit is required, no documentation is filed, and no agency claims jurisdiction. The organism is identical. The regulatory treatment is determined entirely by whether the means of conveyance was a FedEx package or the trade winds.

VIII. The Fertilizer That Fell From the Sky

Among the most remarkable aspects of the Saharan dust plume is that it functions as an unregistered agricultural input on a continental scale. NASA’s CALIPSO data quantified the phosphorus deposition: an estimated 22,000 tons per year are deposited on the Amazon basin, an amount approximately equal to the phosphorus lost from the basin through rainfall and flooding.5 The Sahara is, in effect, fertilizing the Amazon rainforest. Without this annual phosphorus subsidy from African dust, the Amazon’s notoriously nutrient-poor soils would likely be unable to sustain the forest’s current biomass.

Phosphorus is a regulated substance when applied as fertilizer. The Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act defines a “plant regulator” as any substance that “through physiological action, will accelerate or retard the rate of growth or rate of maturation or otherwise alter the behavior of ornamental or crop plants.”21 Phosphorus applied to soil accelerates plant growth. That is its primary function in every commercial fertilizer formulation. When a farmer applies 22,000 tons of phosphorus to cropland, the application is subject to nutrient management planning, runoff regulations under the Clean Water Act, and state fertilizer registration requirements. When the Saharan Air Layer applies the same 22,000 tons of phosphorus to the Amazon basin, no regulation applies because the applicator is the jet stream.

The phosphorus does not stop at the Amazon. Saharan dust deposits iron and phosphorus across the Caribbean Sea, stimulating phytoplankton blooms. Research by the USGS demonstrated that iron from Saharan dust deposits promotes rapid growth of Vibrio species in marine waters, including bacteria in the genus that contains the causative agents of cholera, shellfish-associated gastroenteritis, and seawater wound infections. The dust nutrients cause these bacteria to bloom as “first responders” to iron deposition events.22

An unregistered fertilizer, arriving from a foreign country without import documentation, is deposited on American territorial waters, where it stimulates the growth of pathogenic marine bacteria. Under any domestic regulatory framework, this sequence of events would trigger enforcement actions from the EPA, USDA, and potentially the FDA. Under the current framework, it triggers a NASA press release.

IX. The Scale of the Regulatory Void

The regulatory void is not a gap between two overlapping regulatory regimes. It is a space in which no regime operates at all. Consider the agencies with jurisdiction over the constituent elements of the Saharan dust plume if those elements arrived by any means other than wind:

The EPA has jurisdiction over airborne particulate matter under the Clean Air Act. It has established legally binding concentration limits. The dust exceeds those limits. The agency’s response is the Exceptional Events Rule.

U.S. Customs and Border Protection has jurisdiction over all articles imported into the United States under 19 U.S.C. § 1484. The dust is an article from a foreign country. CBP has processed zero entries.

USDA-APHIS has jurisdiction over imported biological material that could harbor plant pests under the Plant Protection Act. The dust contains viable bacteria and fungi from African soils. APHIS has issued zero permits.

The EPA has jurisdiction over nutrient discharges into navigable waters under the Clean Water Act. The dust deposits phosphorus and iron into the Caribbean Sea, stimulating bacterial blooms. The agency has issued zero NPDES permits.

The regulatory architecture of the United States is sophisticated, comprehensive, and thoroughly documented. It regulates particulate matter in the air, biological material crossing borders, nutrients entering waterways, and foreign articles arriving in the country. It regulates all of these things vigorously, at considerable expense, through multiple overlapping agencies with decades of enforcement history. And it does not regulate any of them when the delivery mechanism is the atmosphere of the planet on which the regulatory architecture was built.

X. The Definition Problem

The central regulatory question is one of definition. The Exceptional Events Rule rests on the statutory language of Section 319(b) of the Clean Air Act, which requires that an exceptional event be “a natural event” that is “not reasonably controllable or preventable.”15 Saharan dust transport is undeniably natural and undeniably not controllable by any American state or federal agency.

But the word “exceptional” does work in this statute that the phenomenon does not support. An exceptional event is, by ordinary meaning, an event that is unusual, infrequent, or outside the normal range of expected occurrences. The annual Saharan dust season is none of these things. It occurs every year. It has occurred every year for millions of years. It follows predictable seasonal patterns driven by well-understood atmospheric dynamics. It is, in every meaningful sense, the normal condition of the atmosphere in the Caribbean basin and the southeastern United States during summer.

The regulatory framework has created a category, “exceptional events,” that by its plain meaning describes something unusual, and then applied that category to one of the most predictable, regular, and ancient atmospheric phenomena on Earth. The framework then requires states to demonstrate, annually, through a multi-step bureaucratic process involving narrative models, historical comparisons, and causal analyses, that the thing that happened this year, which is the same thing that happened last year, and the year before, and for the past several million years, is in fact exceptional.

The alternative would be to acknowledge that the air quality standards describe a condition that the natural atmosphere of the southeastern United States does not, and cannot, meet during significant portions of every calendar year. That the NAAQS for particulate matter, as applied to regions downwind of the Saharan Air Layer, describe an air quality condition that has not existed in those regions at any point during the period since Homo sapiens evolved. That the standards are, in these regions and during these months, aspirational descriptions of air quality that the planet’s own atmospheric systems have never provided and will never provide.

This acknowledgment would require modifying either the standards or their application. Neither option is bureaucratically convenient. It is far simpler to process the annual Saharan dust season through the Exceptional Events Rule, classifying each recurrence of a multimillion-year-old atmospheric phenomenon as a regulatory surprise, and filing the paperwork again next summer.

XI. Conclusion

The evidence is not ambiguous. An estimated 182 million tons of mineral dust depart the Sahara Desert every year, driven by atmospheric processes that have been operating continuously for several million years. Approximately 43 million tons of this material reach the airspace and territorial boundaries of the United States. The dust contains PM2.5 and PM10 at concentrations that the EPA’s own monitoring stations document as exceeding the legally binding NAAQS established under the Clean Air Act. It carries viable bacteria and fungi from African soils that, if imported by any conventional means, would require permits from USDA-APHIS under the Plant Protection Act. It deposits phosphorus and iron in American territorial waters, stimulating blooms of pathogenic marine bacteria. It arrives without customs declaration, without phytosanitary certificate, without air quality permit, and without any documentation of any kind filed with any agency of the United States government.

The EPA’s response to this annual, predictable, multimillion-ton import of regulated pollutants, regulated biological material, and regulated nutrients has been to promulgate a sixty-six-page regulation establishing a formal bureaucratic process for removing the resulting air quality violations from the compliance record. The regulation requires states to document, each year, that the event was “exceptional.” The states comply. The EPA concurs. The data is excluded. The record reflects that no violation occurred. The air that people breathed was, by every metric the agency itself established, unsafe. The bureaucratic record says otherwise.

The Saharan dust plume is the largest unregulated transboundary pollutant import in American history. It exceeds every other source of imported particulate matter by orders of magnitude. It carries biological material across international boundaries without inspection. It deposits agricultural nutrients on foreign ecosystems without registration. And the most powerful environmental regulatory apparatus ever constructed by any nation treats each annual recurrence of this multimillion-year-old phenomenon as a surprise that merits a special filing, and then files it away.

The dust will arrive again this summer. It will violate the standards again. The states will file their demonstrations again. The EPA will concur again. And the atmosphere will continue to deliver 182 million tons of African desert to the Americas, as it has for longer than any regulatory framework, any nation-state, or any species capable of writing a regulation has existed on this planet.
