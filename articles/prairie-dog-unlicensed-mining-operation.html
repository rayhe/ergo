Federal Mining Law & Sciurid Geotechnics

The Federal Mine Safety and Health Act’s Own Definition of “Mine” Classifies Every Prairie Dog Colony in America as an Unlicensed Mining Operation, and MSHA Has Conducted Zero Inspections Across an Estimated Ten Million Unregulated Excavations in Forty-Eight States

The Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977 defines a “coal or other mine” as “an area of land from which minerals are extracted in nonliquid form,” including “excavations, underground passageways, shafts, slopes, tunnels and workings” used in or resulting from such extraction. The definition contains no minimum size threshold, no species limitation, and no requirement that the operator hold a business license or possess opposable thumbs. Cynomys ludovicianus and its four congeners excavate underground tunnel systems five to ten meters long and two to three meters below the surface, extracting mineral-bearing soil in nonliquid form and depositing it on the surface in engineered conical mounds that serve as observation posts and ventilation infrastructure. The species operates across eleven western states from Montana to Texas, with all five species collectively spanning twelve states and parts of two Canadian provinces. The largest documented operation covered approximately 25,000 square miles of West Texas and employed an estimated 400 million workers. The Mine Safety and Health Administration regulates approximately 12,000 active mines in the United States and conducts tens of thousands of inspections per year. It has conducted zero at a prairie dog colony. The ventilation systems have never been surveyed. The roof control plans have never been filed. The operator weighs approximately two pounds and communicates primarily through a system of alarm barks that encode the size, shape, speed, and color of approaching predators but do not encode compliance with 30 CFR Part 56.

By Linnea Thorvald · May 27, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Statutory Framework

Congress enacted the Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977, Public Law 95–164, codified at 30 U.S.C. § 801 et seq., in response to a legislative history that included, among other motivating tragedies, the 1968 Farmington Mine disaster in West Virginia, which killed 78 miners, and the continued prevalence of black lung disease among coal workers.1 The Act created the Mine Safety and Health Administration within the Department of Labor, gave it sweeping enforcement authority over all mining operations in the United States, and established mandatory health and safety standards applicable to every person who works in a mine.2

The word “sweeping” is not editorial. The Act was deliberately designed to be comprehensive. Its legislative history reflects a Congressional intent to reach “the widest possible range of mining activities” and to eliminate the jurisdictional gaps that had allowed mine operators to evade safety regulation through creative classification of their operations.3 To achieve this, Congress defined the term “mine” with a breadth that lawyers have spent forty-nine years attempting to contain and that prairie dogs have spent twenty-three million years inadvertently satisfying.

II. The Definition

Section 3(h)(1) of the Mine Act, codified at 30 U.S.C. § 802(h)(1), provides the operative definition:

“coal or other mine” means (A) an area of land from which minerals are extracted in nonliquid form or, if in liquid form, are extracted with workers underground, (B) private ways and roads appurtenant to such area, and (C) lands, excavations, underground passageways, shafts, slopes, tunnels and workings, structures, facilities, equipment, machines, tools, or other property including impoundments, retention dams, and tailings ponds, on the surface or underground, used in, or to be used in, or resulting from, the work of extracting such minerals from their natural deposits in nonliquid form.4

The definition operates in three layers. Layer (A) establishes the core: an area of land from which minerals are extracted. Layer (B) extends jurisdiction to the roads leading to it. Layer (C) extends jurisdiction to everything used in, intended for use in, or resulting from the extraction: the excavations, tunnels, shafts, structures, equipment, and property.

The definition does not require that the extraction be performed by a human. It does not require that the operator intend to sell the extracted minerals. It does not require that the operation be registered with any state or federal agency. It does not require a profit motive, a corporate charter, or a physical address. It requires only that minerals be extracted from land in nonliquid form and that the statutory elements be present.

The definition does not define “minerals.”

III. What Constitutes a Mineral

Soil is a mineral material. This is not a creative interpretation. It is the position of the United States Geological Survey, which classifies soil as an unconsolidated mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, water, and air.5 The mineral fraction of typical grassland soil comprises quartz, feldspar, mica, clay minerals including montmorillonite and kaolinite, and various iron oxides, carbonates, and sulfates.6 These are the same mineral classes extracted by operations that MSHA regulates without controversy: sand and gravel pits, quarries, and surface mines for clay, crushed stone, and common fill.

MSHA has consistently asserted jurisdiction over operations that extract sand, gravel, clay, topsoil, and other common earth materials. In Marshfield Sand & Gravel Co. v. Secretary of Labor, the Federal Mine Safety and Health Review Commission held that a sand and gravel pit constitutes a mine under the Act.7 The material extracted was ordinary alluvial sand. The material extracted by a prairie dog is ordinary alluvial soil. The legal distinction between these two materials is that one is extracted by a corporation with a tax identification number, and the other is extracted by a rodent.

IV. The Operator

Section 3(d) of the Mine Act defines “operator” as “any owner, lessee, or other person who operates, controls, or supervises a coal or other mine.”8 Section 3(f) defines “person” as “any individual, partnership, association, corporation, firm, subsidiary of a corporation, or other organization.”9

The genus Cynomys comprises five recognized species of burrowing ground squirrels in the family Sciuridae. The black-tailed prairie dog (Cynomys ludovicianus) is the most widely distributed, ranging across the Great Plains from southern Saskatchewan to northern Mexico.10 All five species are obligate burrowers. They excavate tunnel systems as a primary survival behavior. They operate, control, and supervise these excavations with a degree of organizational sophistication that most human mining operations would recognize immediately.

Prairie dog colonies are divided into territorial family groups called coteries, each comprising one adult male, two to three adult females, and their offspring.11 Coteries are aggregated into wards, which are separated by physical barriers such as ridges or roads. Wards are aggregated into towns. A town may contain hundreds of coteries working simultaneously across hundreds of acres. The organizational structure is hierarchical. Labor is divided. The workforce is coordinated through a vocal communication system that Professor Con Slobodchikoff of Northern Arizona University has documented encoding the size, shape, speed, and color of approaching objects in distinct acoustic signals.12 No human mining operation in history has developed a communication system that can encode the shirt color of an approaching safety inspector into a single alarm call.

Whether a prairie dog constitutes a “person” under Section 3(f) is a question Congress did not anticipate. The statute lists “individual, partnership, association, corporation, firm, subsidiary of a corporation, or other organization.” A prairie dog coterie is, by any behavioral definition, an association of individuals organized for the purpose of cooperative excavation and territorial defense. That it lacks articles of incorporation is a filing deficiency, not a structural one.

V. The Scale of Operations

A single prairie dog burrow system is modest by human mining standards. It extends five to ten meters horizontally and two to three meters below the surface.13 Entrance tunnels descend at steep angles. Interior passages connect sleeping chambers, nursery chambers, listening posts, and latrine areas. A single system may have up to six surface openings, each ten to thirty centimeters in diameter.14 The material excavated per burrow system has been estimated at approximately 100 to 200 kilograms of soil, deposited on the surface in mounds that can reach one meter in height.15

These figures become significant at population scale. The pre-European population of black-tailed prairie dogs in North America has been estimated at several hundred million to as many as five billion individuals, occupying approximately 40 million hectares of the Great Plains.16 Systematic poisoning campaigns, habitat conversion to agriculture, and sylvatic plague have reduced the population by an estimated 95 to 98 percent.17 Current populations are estimated at 10 to 20 million animals occupying approximately two million acres of remaining habitat across the western United States.18

Even at these reduced populations, the cumulative excavation is formidable. With an average colony density of 20 to 35 burrow entrances per acre and multiple interconnected tunnel systems per family group, the total number of active excavation sites in the United States is on the order of tens of millions.19 The total volume of mineral material extracted and deposited on the surface across all active colonies each year has not been calculated by any federal agency, which is itself a regulatory anomaly: MSHA tracks production tonnage for every registered mine in its jurisdiction.

VI. The Texas Colony

In 1901, C. Hart Merriam, chief of the USDA’s Bureau of Biological Survey, documented a single black-tailed prairie dog colony in the Texas Panhandle and western Texas that he estimated covered approximately 25,000 square miles, an area roughly the size of West Virginia.20 The colony’s workforce was estimated at approximately 400 million individuals.21

For comparison, the largest active surface mine in the United States, the North Antelope Rochelle Mine in Wyoming’s Powder River Basin, covers approximately 170 square miles.22 It holds a mining permit. It files production reports. Its 1,400 employees receive health and safety training under 30 CFR Part 46. The Texas prairie dog colony was 147 times larger, employed 285,714 times as many workers, operated across multiple counties, and held no permit of any kind.

Whether subsequent historians have questioned Merriam’s population estimate is immaterial to the regulatory analysis. Even a colony one-tenth the size he reported would constitute the largest mining operation by area in the Western Hemisphere. MSHA’s enforcement records for this operation consist of zero inspections, zero citations, zero closure orders, and zero penalty assessments. The colony was eliminated not by regulatory action but by strychnine.

VII. The Ventilation Question

Title 30 of the Code of Federal Regulations contains extensive ventilation requirements for underground mines. For metal and nonmetal mines, 30 CFR § 57.8520 requires that “main fans shall be installed on the surface in fireproof housings.” Section 57.8525 requires mechanical ventilation to maintain “an atmosphere that is safe and healthful for all persons.” Section 57.8530 requires minimum air velocities in working areas.23

Prairie dogs have solved the ventilation problem. They have not solved it in compliance with 30 CFR Part 57.

Prairie dog burrow systems feature two types of surface openings: dome craters, which are low, rounded mounds typically 20 to 30 centimeters high, and rim craters, which are taller, more sharply defined mounds that can reach one meter in height.24 The asymmetry between these openings is not accidental. When wind passes over the taller rim crater, the Bernoulli effect creates a region of lower air pressure at the opening. The shorter dome crater, experiencing less wind acceleration, maintains relatively higher pressure. The resulting pressure differential drives a continuous flow of air through the tunnel system: fresh air enters through the dome crater, circulates through the underground passages, and exits through the rim crater.25

This passive ventilation mechanism was first described in the fluid dynamics literature by Steven Vogel and others, who demonstrated that prairie dog mound geometry creates measurable airflow through burrow systems under typical Great Plains wind conditions.26 The system operates without electricity, without mechanical fans, without maintenance, and without a single ventilation survey having been submitted to MSHA for approval.

The ventilation system is, by engineering standards, elegant. By regulatory standards, it is a violation. No mine ventilation plan has been filed. No air velocity measurements have been recorded. No carbon monoxide monitoring equipment has been installed. No methane detectors are present, despite the fact that soil decomposition in enclosed underground spaces produces methane. The operator has been running an underground mining operation with an unapproved ventilation system for approximately 23 million years.27

VIII. The Roof Control Problem

Section 57.3360 of 30 CFR requires that “mining methods shall be used that will maintain the stability of the ground,” and Section 57.3200 requires that “ground conditions that create a hazard to persons shall be taken down or supported before other work or travel is permitted in the affected area.”28 For underground coal mines, the roof control requirements are even more stringent: 30 CFR § 75.220 requires each mine operator to develop and follow a roof control plan approved by the MSHA District Manager.29

Prairie dog tunnel roofs consist of compacted soil reinforced by root structures from surface vegetation. The roof thickness varies from less than one meter at listening posts near the surface to two to three meters at deeper nursery chambers.30 No roof bolting has been installed. No wire mesh has been applied. No shotcrete has been sprayed. The ground support system consists entirely of whatever root density the overlying grasses happen to provide, supplemented by the natural cohesion of the soil matrix.

Roof falls occur. They occur regularly. Prairie dog burrows collapse under the weight of passing bison, cattle, horses, and vehicles. When a tunnel collapses, the prairie dogs dig around the obstruction or abandon the affected passage and excavate a new one. They do not file a Section 50.10 accident report with MSHA. They do not conduct a roof fall investigation. They do not revise their roof control plan, because they have never submitted one. The Federal Mine Safety and Health Review Commission has never been asked to adjudicate a roof control citation against a prairie dog operator, which represents either an extraordinary compliance record or an extraordinary enforcement failure.

IX. The Reclamation Deficit

The Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977, 30 U.S.C. § 1201 et seq., requires that surface mining operations restore mined land to its “approximate original contour” upon completion of mining activities.31 The Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement administers this requirement, which includes the posting of reclamation bonds to guarantee that funds are available for restoration if the operator defaults.32

Prairie dog colonies, when abandoned due to disease, predation, or poisoning campaigns, leave behind extensive surface disturbance: crater-pocked terrain with hundreds or thousands of open holes per acre, undermined ground surfaces, and altered drainage patterns. The operator posts no reclamation bond. The operator files no mine closure plan. The operator vacates the premises, typically because an outbreak of sylvatic plague caused by Yersinia pestis has killed most of the workforce, and leaves behind an unremediated former mining site that will take years to naturally revegetate.33

The Abandoned Mine Land Reclamation Program, funded by fees on coal production under SMCRA Title IV, has distributed approximately $12.3 billion for the reclamation of abandoned coal mines since 1977.34 It has distributed zero dollars for the reclamation of abandoned prairie dog colonies. The colonies are not in the inventory. The inventory does not include them because MSHA does not classify them as mines. MSHA does not classify them as mines because it has never inspected them. It has never inspected them because no operator has filed a mine identification number. No operator has filed a mine identification number because the operator is a ground squirrel.

X. The Endangered Species Paradox

The regulatory analysis produces a paradox. If every prairie dog colony in America constitutes an unlicensed mine, then MSHA has the authority and the obligation to issue closure orders under Section 104(a) of the Mine Act, which authorizes an inspector to issue a withdrawal order when a violation “could significantly and substantially contribute to the cause and effect of a mine safety or health hazard.”35 Unsurveyed ventilation, absent roof control plans, and zero training compliance would satisfy this standard at every colony in every state.

The paradox is the black-footed ferret.

Mustela nigripes, the black-footed ferret, is one of the most endangered mammals in North America. It was declared extinct in the wild in 1987. A captive breeding program maintained by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Smithsonian’s National Zoo, and partner institutions has reintroduced approximately 300 to 500 individuals to sites across the western United States.36 The black-footed ferret depends on prairie dog colonies for approximately 90 percent of its diet and uses prairie dog burrows as its primary shelter.37 It is an obligate associate. Without prairie dog colonies, there are no black-footed ferrets.

If MSHA were to enforce the Mine Act against prairie dog colonies, issuing closure orders and requiring cessation of all mining activity until the operations obtained proper permits, ventilation plans, and roof control plans, the resulting shutdown would eliminate the habitat and food source of a species protected under the Endangered Species Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1531 et seq.38 This would trigger Section 7 consultation requirements, under which MSHA would be required to consult with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to ensure that its enforcement action does not “jeopardize the continued existence” of the black-footed ferret or “result in the destruction or adverse modification of habitat” designated as critical.39

The Fish and Wildlife Service would almost certainly issue a jeopardy finding. MSHA would then be required to choose between enforcing the Mine Act, which would extinguish an endangered species, and declining to enforce the Mine Act, which would leave millions of unlicensed mining operations in continuous violation of federal law. The two statutes, both enacted by Congress, both administered by the Executive Branch, and both predicated on the protection of life, cannot be simultaneously satisfied. The prairie dog colony is the point at which federal mining law and federal wildlife law collide, and neither has a provision for yielding.

XI. The Commercial Parallel

The irony is sharpened by the fact that when humans extract the same material from the same land using substantially similar methods, MSHA asserts jurisdiction without hesitation. Sand and gravel operations, topsoil removal operations, and borrow pits are all classified as mines under the Act and are subject to the full range of MSHA health and safety standards, including Part 46 training requirements, regular inspections, and penalty assessments for violations.40

A rancher who digs a series of shallow excavations across several hundred acres of grassland, removes mineral-bearing soil, deposits it on the surface, and creates an underground tunnel network would be subject to MSHA jurisdiction. A prairie dog that does the same thing on the adjacent parcel is not. The material is the same. The extraction method is the same. The surface disturbance is the same. The underground void creation is the same. The regulatory outcome is different because MSHA, in forty-nine years of enforcement, has drawn a jurisdictional line between the extraction of minerals by organisms with Social Security numbers and the extraction of minerals by organisms without them. This line does not appear in the statute.

XII. The Jurisdictional Void

The number of active mines regulated by MSHA as of 2023 was approximately 12,000, including 970 coal mines and approximately 11,000 metal and nonmetal operations.41 MSHA employs approximately 2,000 inspectors and enforcement personnel to conduct tens of thousands of inspections per year, issue approximately 100,000 citations and orders, and collect approximately $100 million in civil penalties annually.42

The number of active prairie dog mining operations in the United States is estimated at several million, spread across approximately two million acres in forty-eight states. Hawaii, which has no native prairie dog population, is the sole state without these operations.43 The ratio of MSHA-regulated mines to unregulated prairie dog mines is approximately 1 to several hundred. The ratio of MSHA inspections conducted at prairie dog colonies to inspections conducted at human mines is zero. The ratio of civil penalties assessed against prairie dog operators is zero. The ratio of mine rescue teams stationed at prairie dog colonies is zero.

The most prolific mining operator in the United States weighs between 0.5 and 1.5 kilograms. It is stout-bodied, with short legs, small ears, and a tail that constitutes approximately one-sixth of its total body length.44 It operates in colonies that can span hundreds of acres. It has excavated more underground tunnels than any corporation, municipality, or federal agency in the Western Hemisphere. It has been extracting minerals from their natural deposits in nonliquid form for approximately 23 million years, which is 22,999,951 years longer than MSHA has existed.

The federal mine safety regulatory apparatus was constructed to govern human extraction of earth materials. The extraction it does not govern is orders of magnitude larger, has been in continuous operation since the late Oligocene, operates around the clock except during periods of torpor, and is, by the federal government’s own ecological assessments, more beneficial to soil health, water infiltration, and grassland biodiversity than the operations it does regulate.45
