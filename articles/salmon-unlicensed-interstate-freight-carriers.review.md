# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Salmon as Unlicensed Interstate Freight Carriers"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: long factual headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from registration to carrier to cargo to route to documentation to cargo loss to historical scale to conclusion, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly: the registration requirement, the carrier-is-the-cargo inversion, the 14,000-ton manifest, the 900-mile three-state route, the documentation void, the Carmack cargo-loss problem, the 16-million-carrier historical peak, and the honest escape hatch. The load-bearing beam is the vessel-is-the-cargo inversion, and it holds through all eight sections. Minor weakness: Section VII's Lewis-and-Clark framing slightly overlaps the route material in Section IV; and the headline's final clause, while the strongest single line, telegraphs the punchline the body earns more gradually.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the homing-pigeon and sun pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "The hull is the shipment," "The delivery is terminal in every sense," "No salmon has ever been placed out of service," "the regulatory apparatus has no form for a fish," "the consignees, being trees, have never filed a complaint," and the closer "COD: cash on death." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is "the freight system works only through its continuous failure," which lands as straight-faced systems analysis. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes, regulations, and code sections cited are real and accurately characterized: 49 U.S.C. § 13902's registration requirement, § 13102's motor-carrier definition, 49 CFR § 385.301's pre-operation requirements, § 387.9's insurance minimums, §§ 80101–80116 on bills of lading, and § 14706's Carmack liability, all verified against Cornell LII and FMCSA sources. Biological facts verified: the Nature 2024 continental-scale figures (119 million fish, 14,000 t/yr, 9,000 t N), the chum 130 g N figure, the 22–24% foliar nitrogen subsidy, the 900-mile/6,500-foot Snake River sockeye run, Bonneville counts since 1938, and the 2024 pinniped predation figures (2,218 of 79,241, 2.8%). The 16-million historical figure comes from a secondary outdoor-journal source and is transparently attributed as an estimate. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that salmon are unlicensed interstate freight carriers, is the disclosed conceit; the piece is honest about the definitional escape hatch, "a salmon is not a motor vehicle," in the same way prior pieces disclosed theirs. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"The largest unlicensed freight network in the Pacific Northwest is made of fish" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone. The vessel-is-the-cargo beat is the single most shareable paragraph; "No salmon has ever been placed out of service" and "COD: cash on death" are strong pull-quote material. The hero image, an inspector with a NO USDOT NUMBER clipboard confronting a salmon at a fish ladder backed up like a weigh station, is built for the thumbnail. Slightly setup-dependent because the premise requires one paragraph of FMCSA exposition, but the deck does that work and the headline carries the full burden well. Very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The freight-law citations are accurate: § 13902's registration mandate and out-of-service penalties, § 385.301's USDOT-number requirement, § 387.9's $750,000/$300,000 minimums and BMC-91 filings, the bill-of-lading provisions at §§ 80101–80116, and the Carmack Amendment at § 14706. The § 13102 "motor vehicle transportation for compensation" definition is quoted in substance and correctly identified as the airtight defense. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that salmon are common carriers subject to FMCSA jurisdiction, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends any court has held a fish to be a motor vehicle. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: Nature 2024 (Stricker et al.) for the continental-scale tonnage, USGS for the agency summary, Scientific American (Helfield & Naiman) for life-cycle and per-fish nutrient figures, ResearchGate-hosted peer-reviewed papers for the riparian studies, the Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences via PubMed for the Mokelumne wine-country figures, Idaho Fish and Game for the 900-mile/6,500-foot route, ODFW's 2024 pinniped predation report for the cargo-loss figures, and Cornell LII plus FMCSA for every legal citation. Minor weakness: the 16-million-fish historical figure and the Lonesome Larry anecdote rely on a secondary outdoor-journal source rather than a primary historical record, which is the nature of the available evidence and is transparently attributed throughout.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 119 million fish; 14,000 t/yr total (9,000 t N with 95% credible interval, 1,100 t P, 2,300 t DHA, 1,600 t EPA); 130 g N and 20 g P per chum; 80 kg N per 250-meter reach; 22–24% and 18–25% foliar nitrogen subsidies; 900 miles, 6,500 feet, three states, eight dams; 700,000 sockeye, first time since 1938; 2,218 of 79,241 (2.8%) lost to sea lions; 16 million historical carriers; one Lonesome Larry. The comparison framework, licensed human freight versus the unlicensed piscine sector on every metric, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
