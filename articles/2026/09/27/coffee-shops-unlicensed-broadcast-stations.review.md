# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Coffee Shops as Unlicensed Broadcast Stations"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: a long statute-quoting headline that names every clause of the case, a factual deck, Roman-numeral sections building systematically from the exclusive right (I), to the operator and its scale (II), the license terms (III), the homestyle exemption asymmetry (IV), the enforcement record (V), the damages arithmetic (VI), the deliberately wrong broadcaster classification (VII), the Part 15 escape hatch (VIII), and the conclusion (IX). Pullquotes, a numbered Sources footnote section with bidirectional links, og:image, JSON-LD schema, and the deadpan "Ergo." close are all present and consistent with the raccoon and bird-nest pieces. The load-bearing beam is the Section IV asymmetry — same cafe, same customers, same song, same speakers; FM tuner lawful, Spotify infringement — and it is the sharpest single legal observation in the piece, genuinely the case rather than window dressing. The escalation into the broadcast-station classification in Section VII, followed by the definitional escape hatch in Section VIII, mirrors the raccoon piece's exclusion-beat architecture exactly. Minor weaknesses: footnote 3 links the 17 U.S.C. § 501(a) citation to the govregs § 106 page (same URL as footnote 1 — a copy-paste link-target error); and Section II's "The American pattern is materially identical" extrapolates the UK Soundtrack survey to the United States without a domestic source.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "It does not exempt short distances. It does not exempt lattes," "A cafe without music is a waiting room with better chairs," "The cafe knows it is a place open to the public. It has a sign that says so," "the statute's definition stops one clause short of reaching it," and "the lawfulness of the performance turns entirely on whether the cafe received the song over the air or summoned it from a server." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is "The company that sells you the playlist is telling you, through a separate company, that you are using the wrong product," which lands as straight-faced corporate forensics. Zero em dashes in prose (three en dashes, all in footnotes: statutory ranges and a frequency band). No "Magnificent Seven"/"Mag7". Byline is a pen name ("Reginald Crowe"), no AI disclosure, matching canon. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
Every quoted statute was verified verbatim against the primary source: § 106(4)'s exclusive right; § 101's definitions of "publicly," "transmission program," and "proprietor" (the proprietor quote uses a proper ellipsis and matches Cornell LII, including the "commercial subscription music service" clause); § 501(a); § 504(c)(1)-(2)'s $750/$30,000/$150,000 figures; § 110(5)(B)'s five conditions accurately summarized (3,750 sq ft, six speakers/four per room, no direct charge, no further transmission, licensed transmission); § 301's radio-apparatus prohibition; § 511(h)(2)'s Part 15 carve-out, quoted verbatim; and the CRS RS21107 passage, quoted verbatim from the EveryCRSReport PDF. All four enforcement cases verified: the Ottobar/BMI matter (three karaoke songs confirmed by investigator audio surveillance, liability conceded, slightly over $12,000 statutory damages plus $6,500 fees against a $60,000 request); the Texas Clarion Inn matter (15 songs, $45,000, $11,243.36 fees, innocent-infringer defense rejected after repeated warnings); River City Bar & Grill (W.D.N.C. — the judgment text confirms an express willfulness finding and awards approximately $35,000 for seventeen claims, with the phone calls, cease-and-desist letters, and improper license application on the record); and Range Road Music v. East Coast Foods (9th Cir. 2012 — $36,000 affirmed, $162,728.22 in fees). The deliberately wrong conclusion — that cafes are unlicensed broadcast stations — is the disclosed conceit, and the piece is honest about the definitional escape hatch (the Part 15 parenthetical in Section VIII) in the same way prior pieces disclosed theirs. The "willful because it read Spotify's terms" inference is aggressive but framed as the article's own argument, not as settled law. Minor notes: footnote 3's wrong link target (see General Editor); the CRS "do[es]" bracket is a defensible grammatical alteration of the plural original; "no cafe has ever held a license for this apparatus" is unprovable phrasing but harmless inside the conceit.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.5/10
"Your neighborhood cafe is an unlicensed radio station" is immediately graspable at the headline level alone — everyone has sat in a cafe, and everyone has pressed the Spotify button the article criminalizes. The headline's final clause ("the Largest Unlicensed Broadcasting Operation in America Serves Lattes") is the most shareable clause in any Ergo headline this month. Quotable lines are dense: "Ad-free is not the same as cleared for business," "Three songs. Eighteen thousand five hundred dollars," "It does not exempt lattes," and the Section IX closer, "the same song. The same speakers. The same customers. Different button, different federal statute, different seven-figure exposure." The hero image — an enforcement agent posting a bright red Official Enforcement Notice on a cozy cafe window while a barista pours latte art — is built for the thumbnail. Slightly more setup-dependent than the raccoon premise (the deck must carry the § 110(5) radio/Spotify asymmetry), but the deck does that work in one paragraph. Very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The statute citations are accurate and the quotes are verbatim where quoted. The § 110(5)(B) conditions are correctly rendered. The § 511(h)(2) Part 15 exclusion is correctly quoted and correctly positioned as the escape hatch that defeats the piece's own headline conclusion — the genre's honest move. The "proprietor" definition is quoted accurately, though the prose elides that the definition is expressly scoped "For purposes of section 513," a slight gloss on its generality. The deliberately wrong conclusion — that § 301's license requirement reaches a Part 15 Bluetooth speaker — is the disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends the FCC has ever licensed or pursued a cafe; it concedes the Commission's actual enforcement (18 NALs, six forfeiture orders, $5M+, all AM/FM by statutory definition) and then asks the reader to notice the asymmetry. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text, plus: footnote 3 links § 501(a) to the § 106 govregs page; the prose lists GMR as a fourth licensing society without footnote support (the cited page names only ASCAP, BMI, SESAC); and the § 511(h)(2) quote is sourced to a law-firm article rather than the statute itself.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: govregs.com, Justia, FindLaw, Cornell LII, and congress.gov for every statute; the FCC's own PIRATE Act annual report (via In Compliance Magazine) for the FY2024 figures; the Baltimore Banner and Bloomberg Law for the two lead cases; World Coffee Portal for the market figures; Music Ally for the Soundtrack survey; Spotify's own archived Terms of Service (via a transparently labeled GitHub terms archive, quote verbatim); and multiple trade-press sources confirming Soundtrack Your Brand's 2013 Stockholm founding as a Spotify joint venture. The key numbers were independently verified: 45,227 outlets and $58.5 billion (World Coffee Portal); 1,070 executives, 800,000 UK locations, 86 percent consumer-streaming (Music Ally); 300 million Premium subscribers and 777 million MAUs (confirmed against Spotify's own Q2 2026 newsroom announcement). Weaknesses: the Spotify stats footnote cites demandsage.com (an aggregator) rather than Spotify's newsroom; the § 15.247 classification leans on a vendor application note; the § 511(h)(2) citation should be the statute; the River City case relies on a captcha-walled CaseMine page (though the judgment's dispositive language was verified through the indexed text); and the Texas case rests on a 2013 TorrentFreak piece. The footnote text is otherwise faithful to what each source actually says.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.5/10
Every figure is consistent across headline, deck, body, pullquotes, and footnotes, and every one is sourced. The statutory-damages arithmetic is exact: 60 songs × $750 = $45,000; × $30,000 = $1,800,000; × $150,000 = $9,000,000. The Ottobar math checks: slightly over $12,000 plus $6,500 = $18,500, rendered in the pullquote as "Three songs. Eighteen thousand five hundred dollars." The Range Road "factor of four" framing is conservative against the actual $162,728.22/$36,000 = 4.52× ratio. The River City $2,058.82-per-claim × 17 ≈ $35,000 arithmetic is the court's own. The $12,000-to-$45,000 headline range is anchored to the Ottobar and Clarion Inn statutory-damages figures. The 45,227-outlet figure is used consistently as the denominator (and the piece's "if even one quarter" speculation is explicitly conditional). No invented numbers, no rounding beyond what the sources support.
