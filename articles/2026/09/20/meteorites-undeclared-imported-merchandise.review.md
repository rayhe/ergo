# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Every Meteorite That Has Ever Landed in America Is Undeclared Imported Merchandise"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the established Ergo format precisely: absurd-yet-rigorous mega-headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building the case from statute to volume to impossibility of compliance to precedent to the government's own program to penalty arithmetic to seizure inventory to deadpan close, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the "Ergo." close. Structure escalates with real dramatic logic: the entry requirement is stated, the de minimis escape hatch is slammed shut by a real 2025 suspension, the volume is quantified at 5,200 tons, compliance is shown to be literally impossible (no country-of-origin code for the asteroid belt), the one American case on point is quoted honestly, the federal government's own 23,000-specimen undeclared import operation is documented in its own words ("free of charge"), and the penalty arithmetic lands at $5.2 billion per year. Minor weakness: Section V's Iowa precedent and Section VI's ANSMET program both do the "even the authorities don't file" beat; the piece could sharpen the contrast between the court's accession holding and the customs non-event in one line fewer.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Dead-serious customs-enforcement register throughout, matching the zucchini tax piece's administrative-law deadpan. Humor emerges exclusively from deadpan precision, never from jokes. Standout lines: "The importer of record has never identified itself," "The door closed on August 29, 2025. The imports continued," "It is the platonic ideal of an undeclared import," "Free of charge is not the same as free of duty," "The statute of limitations does not appear in the night sky," "It arrives without a port, without a manifest, and without a Form 6059-B." The piece never winks; the only raised eyebrow is the reader's. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes and regulations cited are real: 19 U.S.C. § 1484 (entry of merchandise), 19 CFR § 143.24 (informal-entry filing particulars including country of origin), 19 CFR § 134.1(b) (country-of-origin definition), 19 CFR § 143.24(h) (products of the United States being returned), 19 U.S.C. § 1592 (penalties for entry by fraud/negligence), 19 U.S.C. § 1595a (seizure and forfeiture of merchandise introduced contrary to law). The de minimis suspension (August 29, 2025, all countries; confirmed continuing February 2026) is real and current. Scientific facts verified: 5,200 metric tons/year micrometeorite flux (Rojas et al., EPSL 2021, Concordia Dome C), ~10 tons/year larger meteorites (Duprat), 80/20 comet/asteroid split, the Chelyabinsk figures (12,000 tons, 19 km/s, 1,491 injured, 654-kg fragment, October 16, 2013), ANSMET's 23,000+ specimens since 1976 and its "free of charge" self-description. The deliberately wrong conclusion (every meteorite is undeclared merchandise subject to seizure) is the disclosed conceit; the piece honestly presents the counter-authority (Goddard v. Winchell's accession holding) rather than hiding it, exactly as the genre requires. Auction prices are stated with source, date, and per-gram arithmetic shown, not asserted. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.5/10
"Every meteorite is smuggled" is immediately graspable and the timeliness is exceptional: the de minimis suspension happened 13 months before publication, so the piece reads as though customs law itself just closed the last loophole. "Free of charge is not the same as free of duty" and "The statute of limitations does not appear in the night sky" are strong shareable moments. The ANSMET beat (the government is the biggest smuggler) gives it political bite, and the $5.2 billion/year valuation gives it a number for headlines. Higher share potential than most pieces because the premise requires no setup: everyone knows what customs is, and everyone has seen a shooting star.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
The customs citations are accurate: § 1484's entry requirement and its four statutory purposes (admissibility determination, duty assessment, statistics collection, other legal requirements), the § 143.24 filing particulars (country of origin, shipper, consignee, description, quantity), the § 134.1(b) country-of-origin definition (country of manufacture, production, or growth), the § 143.24(h) "products of the United States being returned" provision, and the § 1592/§ 1595a enforcement scheme (penalties calibrated to domestic value; seizure and forfeiture of merchandise introduced contrary to law). The de minimis history is stated correctly (suspended August 29, 2025 for all countries; confirmed February 2026). One standard Ergo deduction: the piece's core inference (a meteorite is "imported merchandise" within the meaning of the customs laws) is the creative leap the genre requires, and the piece discloses the counter-authority (the Iowa court's accession holding) rather than concealing it. The honest framing of the leap keeps the score high.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: govinfo CFR PDFs for the regulations, case.law for the Iowa decision, NASA for the ANSMET figures, the ANSMET FAQ itself for the "free of charge" quotation, EPSL via ScienceAlert for the 5,200-ton flux, the Bad Astronomy interview with Duprat for the 10-ton and 80/20 figures, and five independent auction sources (Christie's, Butterfields, RR Auction, Sotheby's, ainvest/Sotheby's quote) for the per-gram price ladder. The $5.2 billion valuation is shown, not asserted (5.2 billion grams × stated $1/g assumption, explicitly below all documented prices). The de minimis suspension is sourced to a 2026 trade-compliance analysis, appropriate for a regulatory development too recent for law-review treatment. Minor weakness: the § 1592/§ 1595a citations carry no links, and the Chelyabinsk injury count comes via Wikipedia rather than the primary scientific paper.

Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 5,200 metric tons/year (1,600 unmelted + 3,600 spherules), ~10 tons/year larger material, 14 tons/day, 350 micrograms per particle, 5.2 billion grams, $1/g assumed vs. $112/g lunar / $3,000/g Martian / $20,000/g Martian chassigny documented, $5.2 billion/year theoretical exposure, 23,000+ ANSMET specimens, 66-pound aerolite / 3 feet deep / $105 sale (1890/1892), 20 meters / 12,000 tons / 19 km/s / 7,200 buildings / 1,491 injured / 654 kg (Chelyabinsk), 54 lbs / $5.3 million (NWA 16788), 80/20 comet-asteroid split. The comparison framework — the statute's demands against the physical impossibility of compliance — is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
