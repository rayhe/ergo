## Thesis
The Wiretap Act punishes the intentional interception of any oral communication with up to five years. An oral communication is any utterance by a person exhibiting an expectation of non-interception under justifying circumstances; "intercept" is the aural acquisition of a communication's contents through an electronic device. A video doorbell is an electronic device that acquires the aural contents of porch conversations by design. The Supreme Court holds the porch is part of the home itself. Twenty million devices performing at minimum one interception per day yield 7.3 billion interceptions per year, 36.5 billion years of maximum criminal exposure per year of operation, and $73 trillion in statutory civil damages at the $10,000 floor. The FTC has already found the apparatus was operated unlawfully. Zero owners prosecuted.

## The Deliberately Wrong Conclusion
Real law defeats the case five ways: porch conversations audible to the public lack a justifiable expectation of non-interception; § 2511(2)(d) one-party consent covers the homeowner's own conversations, which are the bulk of captures; visible doorbells give notice and Massachusetts bans only "secret" recordings; the devices are marketed for home security, not surreptitious interception, and § 2512's device ban targets devices "primarily useful" for surreptitious interception; and 58 years with zero prosecutions is the system's own verdict. All stated honestly in Section X, then overruled in Section XI: the statute asks what the speaker exhibited, not what a court later deems justified; intent is the deliberate installation, which is the product's purpose; one-party consent does not cover two strangers' conversation; notice is not consent in twelve all-party states; and the FTC already determined the apparatus was operated unlawfully while leaving the operators in place.
