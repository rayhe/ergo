# Ergo Review: Trick-or-Treat as Hobbs Act Extortion

**Topic:** The utterance "trick or treat" as Hobbs Act extortion under 18 U.S.C. § 1951: the obtaining of candy (property) from the homeowner, with his consent, induced by the wrongful use of threatened force or fear (the trick), affecting interstate commerce in every way and degree ($4.1 billion in candy, $16 billion in chocolate shipments). 41.2 million potential offenders, 115 million victim premises, 824 million years of maximum annual exposure, zero prosecutions in 99 years.
**Author:** Kit (with Ray)
**Date:** 2026-09-29

## Headline
"Section 1951 of Title 18 at 18 U.S.C. § 1951 Provides That Whoever in Any Way or Degree Affects Commerce by Extortion Shall Be Fined or Imprisoned Not More Than Twenty Years, Subsection (b)(2) Defines Extortion as the Obtaining of Property From Another, With His Consent, Induced by Wrongful Use of Actual or Threatened Force, Violence, or Fear, the Earliest Known Appearance in Print of the Words Trick or Treat Occurred in 1927 in Blackie, Alberta, Where a Newspaper Reported That the Youthful Tormentors Were at Back Door and Front Demanding Edible Plunder by the Word Trick or Treat to Which the Inmates Gladly Responded and Sent the Robbers Away Rejoicing, the Census Bureau Estimates 41.2 Million Potential Trick-or-Treaters, the National Retail Federation Projects Americans Will Spend $4.1 Billion on Halloween Candy This Year, Candy Is Property, the Trick Is the Threat, the Treat Is the Consent, No Child Has Ever Been Prosecuted, and the Largest Unprosecuted Extortion Enterprise in American History Knocks on Your Door Every October 31"

## Thesis
The Hobbs Act defines extortion as obtaining property from another, with his consent, induced by wrongful threatened force or fear, punishable by up to 20 years. "Trick or treat" is a conditional threat of criminal mischief (the trick) made to obtain candy (property), which the homeowner hands over with consent induced by fear of the trick. The candy moves in interstate commerce ($16B in chocolate shipments, $4.1B in Halloween candy). 41.2M children × 20 years = 824M years of maximum annual exposure. The 1927 newspaper that first recorded the phrase called the participants "robbers" demanding "edible plunder."

## The Deliberately Wrong Conclusion
Real law defeats the case four ways: Virginia v. Black's true-threats doctrine (ritual speech by costumed children is not a serious expression of intent to commit unlawful violence), the Hobbs Act's specific-intent requirement (a seven-year-old intends Skittles, not to affect interstate commerce), 18 U.S.C. § 5031 (offenders under 18 are juveniles, not adult defendants), and Scheidler's narrow reading plus 99 years of prosecutorial non-enforcement. All stated honestly in Section X, then overruled in Section XI: the statute asks what was said at the door, not who said it; Black itself says the speaker need not intend to carry out the threat; the consent and fear are documented in the 1927 record.
