# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Microwave Ovens as Unlicensed Radio Transmitters"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: absurdly long factual headline, scholarly deck, ten Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from statute to apparatus to frequency to license void to interference to scale to identification to penalties to disclosed defenses, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly: the statutory prohibition, the magnetron, the ISM band assignment, the license that does not exist, the documented interference, the dinner-hour gigawatt arithmetic, the station-identification failure, the trillion-dollar penalty exposure, the four disclosed objections, the conclusion. The load-bearing beam is the disjunctive reading of "energy or communications or signals" paired with Section 18.111(a)'s no-license text, and it holds through all ten sections. The sharpest single beats are the thousand watts "from a countertop, through a waveguide, toward a burrito," the "largest unlicensed radio transmission operation in the United States runs three minutes at a time," and the beep as "a sign-off without a sign-on, broadcast by a station that has never identified itself." Minor weakness: the Section VIII penalty arithmetic is doing the heavy satirical lifting and the piece concedes in Section IX that the PIRATE Act's band scope is "accurate," which slightly thins the section even as the disclosure strengthens the genre's honesty.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the bird-nest and cicada pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "Congress listed energy first," "A higher frequency does not make them less radio. It makes them more radio, more often," "The statute does not distinguish between the two on the basis of culinary intent," "It does not announce a license, because it does not hold one," "The burrito is irrelevant to the analysis," "the drafters simply failed to imagine a pirate station with a turntable," and "A transmitter whose interference is anticipated, measured, and pre-excused by regulation is not an accident. It is a program." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is "the microwave oven is the first example the Commission thought of," which lands as straight-faced procedural suspicion. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes, regulations, and survey figures cited are real and quoted verbatim where quoted: 47 U.S.C. Section 301's full prohibition and all six jurisdictional subsections, verified against Cornell LII; 47 CFR 18.301's 2,450 MHz plus-or-minus-50 ISM table; 18.305(a)'s "unlimited radiated energy" verbatim; 18.111(a), (b), and (c) verbatim, including the ISM-band exception to the interference-elimination duty; 18.107(g)'s Consumer ISM equipment definition verbatim; 18.212's compliance statement; 18.213(a)'s "interference potential of the device or system"; 21 CFR 1030.10(b)(1)'s definition, (c)(1)'s 1 and 5 mW per sq cm limits, and (c)(2)'s two-interlock minimum; 73.1201's identification regime accurately summarized; the EIA's 2015 RECS figures (113.6 million with, 4.6 million without); the PIRATE Act's $100,000 per day, $2 million maximum, and the inflation-adjusted $115,802 and $2,316,034 with the maximum penalty proposed against the Queens operators. Three pre-publication corrections were applied during drafting: the Section 301(d) quotation was completed to the full clause instead of a silently truncated one, the Consumer ISM equipment cite was corrected from a stale 18.113(g) to the current 18.107(g), and the definition's "notwithstanding use in other areas" clause was restored. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that microwave ovens are unlicensed radio transmitters violating Section 301, is the disclosed conceit; Section IX addresses the four objections rather than suppressing them, including conceding the PIRATE Act band limitation as accurate. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"113.6 million unlicensed radio transmitters in American kitchens" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone, with zero setup required: everyone owns the premise. The "$11.36 trillion in theoretical forfeitures incurred between breakfast and dinner, by appliances" is the single most shareable paragraph; "Congress listed energy first" and "It does not announce a license, because it does not hold one" are strong pull-quote material. The hero image, a microwave photographed as an evidence exhibit with an antenna mast and caution placard, is built for the thumbnail. Very high share potential; slightly less surreal than the raccoon premise but more universal.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The statutory and regulatory citations are accurate: Section 301's prohibition, disjunctive "energy or communications or signals," and full subsection (d) text quoted verbatim; 18.305(a)'s unlimited radiated energy; 18.111(a)'s no-vested-right rule; 18.111(c)'s ISM-band exception to the interference duty; 18.107(g)'s definition; 21 CFR 1030.10's definitional and safety provisions; 73.1201's identification requirements; the PIRATE Act penalty structure with verified inflation adjustments. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that equipment authorization under Part 18 does not satisfy Section 301's license requirement, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends the Commission's authorization regime is actually a licensing void in the eyes of the law; it treats its own creative inference as the joke. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: Cornell LII for the statute and every CFR section, the FDA and EIA for the federal figures, congress.gov for the PIRATE Act, Radio Insight and radio-online for the enforcement record. The Wi-Fi interference claims were re-verified against primary publication: the Macworld lab testing with a MetaGeek spectrum analyzer showing throughput collapse from just over 100 Mbps to about 3 Mbps, and the Ofcom consumer guidance ("Did you know that microwave ovens can also reduce Wi-Fi signals?") corroborated across four independent outlets. Minor weakness: the 700-to-1,200-watt output range and the magnetron waveguide description rely on secondary explainers rather than manufacturer engineering specifications, which is the nature of the available evidence and is transparently attributed. Passes threshold.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 2,450 MHz plus or minus 50 MHz; 700 to 1,200 watts; 113.6 million versus 4.6 million households, 96 percent; 100 Mbps collapsing to 3 Mbps; 1 mW per sq cm before purchase, 5 after; two interlocks; $100,000 per day, $2 million maximum; $115,802 and $2,316,034 inflation-adjusted; $11.36 trillion daily exposure against roughly 40 percent of GDP; 11.36 million magnetrons, 11.4 gigawatts at the dinner hour. The comparison framework, individual Queens pirate broadcasters at a few dozen watts drawing maximum penalties versus 113.6 million one-thousand-watt transmitters drawing nothing, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
