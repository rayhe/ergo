# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "The Human Heart as an Unapproved Class III Medical Device"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the established Ergo format precisely: absurd-yet-rigorous single-sentence headline, scholarly deck, eight Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from statute to approved comparator to operator to installed base to adverse-event record to replacement market to labeling to enforcement impossibility, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, pullquotes at the load-bearing beats, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. Structure escalates naturally: the classification framework, the eighteen-month approval ordeal for the one approved unit, the biology of the unapproved unit, the 340,000-to-1 installed-base ratio, the fifty-year reporting silence, the bridge-to-transplant irony, the labeling gap, and the enforcement dead end. Minor weakness: Section VII (labeling) is the thinnest section, with only two paragraphs and no human-scale comparator, but the "cannot even read the labeling, because there is none" closer lands.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Dead-serious investigative tone throughout, matching the mosquito and tree pieces in the FDA-law register. The humor emerges exclusively from deadpan precision, never from jokes. Standout lines: "The paperwork file is empty. The devices are everywhere," "The regulatory system approves the bridge. It has never approved the destination," "The Center for Devices and Radiological Health has no form for no one," "The device is installed before birth and operated continuously for decades by a user who has received no instruction, holds no certification, and cannot name the device's manufacturer," "Its distribution channel is birth, which predates the Medical Device Amendments by the entirety of human history." The piece never winks. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 8.5/10
All statutes and regulations cited are real: Section 513's three-class risk-based system and Class III premarket approval (fda.gov), the Class I "supporting or sustaining human life" exclusion (FDA reclassification document), Section 513(f)(1)'s automatic Class III for post-May-28-1976 devices, the Syncardia PMA P030011 approval letter of October 15, 2004 with its filing date, twelve amendments, bridge-to-transplant indication, in-hospital restriction, and 21 CFR 801.109 prescription-use restriction (accessdata.fda.gov), 21 CFR Part 803 manufacturer reporting duties and 30-day clock (fda.gov), and Section 518's recall authority among the general controls (Federal Register via justia). Biological and demographic facts verified: AHA 100,000 beats and 2,000 gallons per day with 2.5 billion beats per 70-year lifetime, the sinoatrial node as natural pacemaker (UCSB Science Line), Census 340.1 million residents July 1, 2024, CDC 702,880 heart-disease deaths in 2022 (21.4 percent, leading cause), UNOS 4,111 heart transplants in 2022, and the $252.2 billion cost figure. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that the human heart is a Class III device requiring premarket approval, is the disclosed conceit, stated outright in Section III ("the Act was written with manufactured devices in mind. This investigation notes it too, and proceeds anyway"). Weakest link: the 513(f)(1) automatic-Class-III point is sourced to a vendor compliance blog rather than the statute itself; the claim is correct but the citation is the piece's least authoritative. Passes threshold.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"Your heart is an unapproved medical device" is immediately graspable and viscerally funny; the 340,000-to-1 ratio of unapproved units to approved-class implants gives readers a concrete absurdity to share. "The regulatory system approves the bridge. It has never approved the destination" is social media gold, as is "The paperwork file is empty. The devices are everywhere." Slightly less universally graspable than "the Sun is unlicensed" because the premise requires one paragraph of FDA classification setup, the deck does that work, but the headline carries the whole burden alone. Still very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
The FD&C Act citations are accurate: Section 513's Class I/II/III structure with Class III subject to premarket approval, the Class I exclusion for devices purported for supporting or sustaining human life, 513(f)(1)'s automatic Class III classification, the PMA P030011 details quoted from the actual approval letter (filing date, amendment dates, indication, prescription-use and training restrictions), 21 CFR 803's manufacturer death/serious-injury/malfunction reporting with the 30-calendar-day clock and the "any employee becomes aware" standard, and Section 518's repair/replacement/refund authority. The article's deliberately wrong conclusion, that a human heart is a "device" under the Act, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends the statute defines organs as devices. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: the FDA's own approval letter (accessdata.fda.gov) for the Syncardia PMA, fda.gov for the classification and MDR frameworks, CDC's National Vital Statistics System for the 702,880 deaths, the Census Bureau for the 340.1 million population, the American Heart Association for the cardiac output figures, UNOS via Becker's for the 4,111 transplants, and the Federal Register for the general-controls enumeration. Minor weaknesses shared with the genre: the implant-count figure ("more than 1,000 implants") comes from trade press (dicardiology.com), and the 513(f)(1) citation runs through a vendor blog. The article is transparent about its sources throughout and flags its one computed figure (fn13) as computed.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.5/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 100,000 beats and 2,000 gallons per day, 730,000 gallons per year, 2.5 billion beats per 70-year lifetime, 340.1 million residents, more than 1,000 approved-class implants, the 340,000-to-1 ratio, 702,880 deaths (21.4 percent, 1 in 5, one every 33 seconds), the 30-calendar-day reporting clock, 18 months and 12 amendments for the one approval, 4,111 transplants in 2022 against a 340-million installed base ("rounds to zero"), $252.2 billion in costs, and fifty years of the reporting clock running. The comparison framework, the one approved unit versus the 340 million unapproved ones on every metric, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
