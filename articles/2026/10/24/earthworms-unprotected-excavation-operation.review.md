# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Earthworms as an Unprotected Excavation Operation"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: long factual headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from protective system to specific requirements to definitions to target to operator to scale to competent-person void to asymmetry to definitional escape hatch to conclusion, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly: the rule, the ancillary duties, the definitions that do the enforcement work, the weight of soil, the worm, Darwin's weighings, the absent inspector, the licensed contractor comparison, the honest hatch, the conclusion. The load-bearing beam is the Section V geometry move, that a burrow deeper than it is wide and narrower than 15 feet at the bottom satisfies the dimensional definition of a trench while standing at 90 degrees against a 34-degree maximum slope, and it holds through all ten sections. The Section IX escape-hatch beat, declining to use the hatch because "the soil is not using it," mirrors the bats piece's sharpest structural choice without repeating its wording. Minor weakness: Section IV restates the fatality figures already given in Section I rather than advancing new evidence.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the bats and earthquake pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "A vertical wall is not a slope. A vertical wall is the condition the slope exists to prevent," "It has no authorization to take any measure. It has setae," "The distance required is two feet. The distance observed is zero," and "The only ladder in the vicinity is the one in the photograph accompanying this investigation, and it was placed there by the photographer." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is "in a generous mood," which lands as straight-faced procedural suspicion. Zero em dashes in the draft, well within house discipline. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All regulations and definitions cited are real and quoted verbatim where quoted: 29 CFR 1926.650(a) and (b) scope and definitions, 1926.651(c)(2) egress, (j)(2) spoil, (k)(1) and (k)(2) inspection and removal, 1926.652(a)(1), (a)(2), and (b)(1)(i) protective systems and the 34-degree slope. Biological and historical facts verified: nightcrawler burrows at five to six feet (Kansas State Extension), 6.5 feet (National Geographic), and eight feet in moist soil (University of California); Darwin's ten tons per acre per year with 16.1 and 18.12 ton calculations (contemporary reviews via Darwin Online); the 7.56 to 15.12 ton range and the Roman villa burial (Popular Science Monthly summary); the 53,767 worms per acre figure (contemporary review). The deliberately wrong conclusion, that worms operate an unprotected excavation, is the disclosed conceit; the piece is honest about the definitional escape hatch, excavations must be man-made and the standard protects employees, in the same way the bats piece disclosed its hatch. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.5/10
"Ten tons of dirt per acre, moved by worms, with no hard hat in sight" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone, and the hero image, an OSHA inspector studying a burrow beside a miniature ladder and traffic cone, is built for the thumbnail. The 56-degree slope violation and the zero-foot spoil setback are the most shareable paragraphs; "It has setae" is strong pull-quote material. Slightly below the bats piece on shareability because excavation law is less instantly vivid than a cave of 15 million bats, but the Darwin angle, the man who rewrote the history of life ending his career weighing worm excrement, gives it a second shareable hook. High share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The Subpart P citations are accurate: the 1926.650(b) definitions quoted verbatim from the published standard, the 1926.651(c)(2) egress language, the (j)(2) two-foot spoil rule, the (k)(1) daily and post-rainstorm inspection duty, the 1926.652(a)(1) protective-system rule with its two exceptions, and the (b)(1)(i) one and one-half horizontal to one vertical slope. The trench fatality figures are accurately attributed to the Department of Labor releases for 2022, 2023, and 2024, with the July 2025 partial-year figure dated as such. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that a worm burrow is a regulated excavation, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends Subpart P regulates animals; Section IX states the escape hatch explicitly. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: OSHA and Cornell for the regulatory text, the Department of Labor for the fatality series, Kansas State Extension, National Geographic, and the University of California for the burrow depths, Darwin Online for the contemporary reviews of Darwin's worm book, the New York Public Library for the book's reception, and the Popular Science Monthly summary for the measured range and the villa burial. Competing depth figures (five to six feet, 6.5 feet, eight feet) are reported as a range with each source named, rather than collapsed into a single convenient number, and the most conservative figure is correctly noted as already triggering the five-foot rule. Author's arithmetic (100 tons per acre per decade, 1,000 tons per year for a 100-acre field) is explicitly labeled as such. Minor weakness: the trench and competent-person definitions are cited to a university-hosted copy of Subpart P rather than the eCFR text itself.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: five-foot protective threshold, four-foot egress threshold, 25 feet of lateral travel, two-foot spoil setback, 34-degree maximum slope, 90-degree worm shaft, 56-degree exceedance, five to six, 6.5, and eight-foot burrow depths, four to eight inches and 14 inches of worm, six-year lifespan, one third of body weight eaten per day, ten tons per acre per year, 16.1 and 18.12 ton high calculations, 7.56 to 15.12 ton measured range, one to more than two inches of new soil per decade, 53,767 worms per acre, 11 to 16 inches of mould over a Roman floor, 39, 15, and 12 trench deaths, one cubic yard weighing as much as a car. The comparison framework, licensed contractor versus worm colony on every metric, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
