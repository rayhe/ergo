Solid Waste Management & Dendrology

The Environmental Protection Agency Estimates That Yard Trimmings Accounted for 35.4 Million Tons of Municipal Solid Waste in 2018, Section 4005 of the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act Prohibits the Open Dumping of Solid Waste, Twenty-Four States Ban or Restrict Yard Waste Disposal, Fair Lawn, New Jersey Imposes Fines of Up to $2,000 for Blowing Leaves Into the Street, the United States Geological Survey Has Found That Fallen Leaves in the Street Are the Greatest Source of Urban Phosphorus, and the Generators of All of It Hold No Permit, No Manifest, and No Waste-Generator Identification Number

The EPA counted 35.4 million tons of yard trimmings in the municipal solid waste stream in 2018, 12.1 percent of everything America discarded. Congress banned the open dumping of solid waste in 1976. About two dozen states now ban or restrict yard waste disposal. Municipalities fine residents up to $2,000 for putting leaves in the wrong place. The U.S. Geological Survey found that fallen leaves in the street are the greatest source of phosphorus in urban stormwater, and that 27 to 80 percent of a leaf’s phosphorus leaches out in the first 24 hours of soaking. Every autumn, the generators release the entire annual tonnage in a matter of weeks, onto sidewalks, into streets, and down storm drains, without a permit, without a manifest, and without a single inspection in the history of the program.

By Cornelius Bramble · October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Waste

The Environmental Protection Agency measures the generation, recycling, composting, combustion with energy recovery, and landfilling of yard trimming material in municipal solid waste, which the agency defines to include grass, leaves, and tree and brush trimmings from residential, institutional, and commercial sources.1 In 2018, the agency estimated generation at 35.4 million tons, or 12.1 percent of all municipal solid waste generated in the United States.1 Of that tonnage, an estimated 22.3 million tons were composted or mulched, a 63 percent composting rate; 2.6 million tons were combusted with energy recovery; and landfills received about 10.5 million tons, which comprised 7.2 percent of all municipal solid waste landfilled that year.1

The agency further notes that yard trimmings generation has declined since 1990, and attributes the decline in part to state legislation discouraging yard trimmings disposal in landfills, including source reduction measures such as backyard composting and leaving grass trimmings on the yard.2 The legislation did not reduce the generation of the material. It reduced the counting of the material. The leaves still fall; they are simply no longer weighed at the gate.

Consider the temporal concentration. The 35.4 million tons are not generated evenly across the year. The deciduous fraction, the leaves, is released in a single seasonal pulse across the autumn weeks, by generators that operate no balers, no compactors, no transfer stations, and no permitted disposal facilities of any kind. It is the largest seasonal generation event in the American waste stream, and it is conducted entirely by unpermitted operators.

II. The Ban

The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, at Section 4005, prohibits the open dumping of solid waste.3 The prohibition is the centerpiece of Subtitle D, the non-hazardous solid waste program: every approved state solid waste management plan must prohibit new open dumps and require the closure or upgrade of every existing one, and any facility failing to meet the criteria in 40 CFR Parts 257 and 258 is, by definition, an open dump.4 The statute was enacted in 1976. The trees were not consulted.

The states have layered their own prohibitions on top. Twenty-four states ban or restrict yard waste disposal, according to industry data; twenty-six states require yard waste to be disposed of separately from the rest of household trash; and Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, and Iowa were the first to enact yard waste bans, in the early 1990s.56 In Iowa, it is illegal to put leaves in the garbage bin for landfill disposal, illegal to blow them into the street, and illegal to burn them in an open fire.7 The leaf, in Iowa, has no lawful destination that does not involve a compost pile.

The municipalities enforce the regime at the curb. In Fair Lawn, New Jersey, dumping or blowing leaves into the street is strictly prohibited, and violators face fines of up to $2,000; the borough’s Recycling Division announced the warning this week, as leaf cleanup gets into full swing across Bergen County.8 In Davenport, Iowa, raking leaves or blowing grass clippings into the street violates City Code Chapter 8.14.400, with a first fine of $100 and subsequent fines of up to $500.7 In Mansfield, Ohio, the city’s 2026 leaf program instructs residents not to place leaves in the streets, citing ordinance 911.06(b), because the practice causes flooding and stormwater problems.9 In Grand Rapids and Lansing, Michigan, it is illegal to rake, pile, or blow leaves or tree branches into city streets or storm drains, and under the state’s littering law, disposing of yard waste in a water body is a civil infraction punishable by fines of $800 to $5,000.10 In Fort Wayne, Indiana, the city’s annual leaf pickup mailers remind residents that throwing leaves in the trash is against city code and can result in a fine, that burning leaves is illegal under state law, and that leaf piles must be kept off the streets and on the curbs.11

The regime is comprehensive. It regulates the homeowner, the landscaper, the renter, and the passerby. It does not regulate the generator.

III. The Generators

The generators hold no waste-generator identification number. They file no manifests. They maintain no operating record, no contingency plan, no financial assurance for closure, and no permitted transfer station, treatment facility, or disposal unit. Every October they discharge the year’s production at the point of generation, directly onto sidewalks, streets, lawns, and storm drains, and then they stand there, unpermitted, while the humans manage the consequences.

The generators hold no waste-generator identification number. They file no manifests. Every October they discharge the year's production at the point of generation, and then they stand there, unpermitted, while the humans manage the consequences.

The liability, meanwhile, attaches exclusively to the humans who touch the material after it falls. In Fair Lawn, landscapers must purchase a seasonal permit, $100 per truck, to drop leaves at the borough compost center; the hauler is permitted, the generator is not.8 In the District of Columbia, where gas-powered leaf blowers have been banned since January 1, 2022, the District tries to fine the landscaper who operates the blower, and if the landscaper cannot be identified but the complaint is valid, the property owner may be fined instead, up to $500 per offense.12 The enforcement chain runs from the municipality to the landscaper to the homeowner, and stops precisely one link short of the organism that produced the leaves.

The structural problem is definitional, as it always is. The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act addresses itself to persons who generate, transport, treat, store, or dispose of solid waste. The tree is not a person. The tree does not generate, in the sense the statute means; it sheds. It does not dispose, in the sense the statute means; it drops. The most prolific waste generators in the country operate entirely outside the vocabulary of the waste law, which is why fifty years of solid waste regulation has produced exactly zero permitted trees.

IV. The Water

The leaves do not stay where they fall. Rain and melting snow wash whatever is on streets and other paved surfaces down storm drains and into local waterways, and leaves, grass, and dirt contain phosphorus, which feeds the algae that turn lakes green.13 The U.S. Geological Survey’s findings on the subject are stark: fallen leaves in the street are the greatest source of phosphorus from urban stormwater, according to a 2016 study.14 USGS hydrologist Bill Selbig, evaluating the impact of leaf litter on nutrient levels in urban stormwater, found that 27 to 80 percent of a leaf’s initial phosphorus was leachable in the first 24 hours of soaking, which is to say that the pollution begins almost immediately, long before the leaf has visibly decomposed.15

In Madison, Wisconsin, USGS researchers paired catchments to test municipal leaf collection and street cleaning programs and confirmed that fall leaf litter can be a primary source of nutrients in stormwater, particularly phosphorus: where an active, thorough leaf removal and street cleaning program was in place, total and dissolved phosphorus loads fell by 84 and 83 percent, respectively, and total and dissolved nitrogen loads fell by 74 and 71 percent, compared with no program.16 The timing of removal matters, because of the highly leachable nature of leaves; significant reductions are achieved by removing leaf litter before a precipitation event.16 The municipality is, in effect, racing the rain to intercept the tree’s discharge before it reaches the water.

Here the regulatory inversion reaches its purest form. Municipalities hold stormwater discharge permits under the Clean Water Act’s municipal separate storm sewer system program, and the EPA’s own permit materials list decaying organic matter, such as leaf litter and grass clippings, among the phosphorus sources the permits must address.17 The city is the permittee for the tree’s waste. The city did not generate the waste, cannot bill the generator, cannot inspect the generator, and cannot require the generator to implement best management practices, because the generator is a tree. The permit covers the discharge; the discharge is leaves; the leaves are not the city’s. The city holds the paper, and the tree holds the canopy.

V. The Cleanup Economy

The municipalities have built an entire seasonal industry around waste they did not generate. Vacuum trucks, street sweepers, curbside bag programs, drop-off sites, and composting facilities mobilize every October to move the tonnage from where the generators put it to where the law allows it to be. The USGS found that weekly street cleaning with a regenerative-air street cleaner reduced phosphorus loads by 65 to 71 percent, regardless of the leaf collection method.18 The sweepers are the compliance mechanism for a generator that cannot be compelled.

The cleanup equipment, remarkably, is more regulated than the generator. In the District of Columbia, the Leaf Blower Regulation Amendment Act of 2018 took effect on January 1, 2022, prohibiting the sale and use of gasoline-powered leaf blowers, with fines of up to $500 per offense; any person who observes a gas-powered blower in use may file a signed complaint with photographic evidence within seven days.12 More than 170 jurisdictions in 31 states now regulate gas-powered leaf blowers, according to a D.C. Council report.19 The machine that moves the leaves is subject to a signed-complaint enforcement regime with photographic evidence standards. The organism that made the leaves is subject to nothing.

The economics of the material are equally perverse. Large-scale leaf composting reduces the original volume by 40 to 75 percent and the original weight by 50 percent; uncompacted yard waste runs 250 to 500 pounds per cubic yard, while landfilled yard waste compacts to 1,500 pounds per cubic yard.5 New York City’s residential composting mandate, in effect since April 2025, is the largest single organics program in the country.6 The cities spend millions collecting, hauling, and composting 35.4 million tons of material whose producers contribute nothing to the cost of management, hold no franchise agreement, and have never once been sent a bill.

VI. The Enforcement Void

The compliance rate is zero. No deciduous tree has ever obtained a solid waste facility permit. No oak has ever filed a manifest. No maple has ever submitted to an inspection, posted financial assurance, or responded to a notice of violation, because no notice of violation has ever been issued to a tree, because the enforcement apparatus has no form for a tree, and the form it would need does not exist.

The regime’s own concessions are instructive. In Fort Wayne, Indiana, environmental organizers remind residents that there is no ordinance or law requiring leaves to be removed at all; the city criminalizes putting leaves in the trash, criminalizes burning them, and criminalizes putting them in the street, but compels nothing.11 The law is a fence with no gate: the waste may not go here, may not go there, may not go anywhere the city has thought to prohibit, and must go somewhere the city provides, at the city’s expense, on the city’s schedule.

The record, taken as a whole, describes the largest unpermitted solid-waste disposal program in the United States. Its generators produce 35.4 million tons a year. They discharge the entire annual tonnage in a matter of weeks, directly onto public rights-of-way and into the storm sewer system, where the material becomes the greatest source of urban phosphorus pollution. The federal government banned the open dumping of solid waste fifty years ago. The states ban the landfilling of the material in two dozen jurisdictions. The municipalities fine the humans up to $2,000 for mishandling what the trees release for free.

The trees do not recognize the jurisdiction. They do not retain counsel. They do not experience regulatory anxiety. They leaf, they drop, they stand bare through the winter, and they leaf again in the spring, where the municipality has thoughtfully scheduled a vacuum truck. The permit application is not pending. It will not be filed. The inspector is still responsible for every generator in the jurisdiction, and the jurisdiction’s largest generators are in the ground.
