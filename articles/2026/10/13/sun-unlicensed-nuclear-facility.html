Nuclear Licensing Law & Stellar Physics

Section 101 of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 Provides That It Shall Be Unlawful for Any Person to Use Any Utilization Facility Except Under a License Issued by the Commission, the Commission’s Regulation at 10 CFR 50.10 Provides That No Person May Begin Construction of a Utilization Facility Without a Construction Permit, the Sun Has Sustained a Thermonuclear Fusion Reaction Producing 3.828×1026 Watts for Approximately 4.6 Billion Years, the Entire Licensed Fleet of 95 American Commercial Reactors Generates Roughly 100 Gigawatts, Less Than One Quadrillionth of That Output, No License Application Has Ever Been Filed for the Sun, No Inspection Has Ever Been Performed, No Licensee Event Report Has Ever Been Submitted, and the Largest Operating Nuclear Reactor in the Solar System Runs Entirely Outside the Licensing Framework

The Nuclear Regulatory Commission licenses 95 commercial reactors generating about a fifth of America’s electricity, devoting roughly 5,960 inspection and assessment hours to each operating reactor per year. The Sun produces 3.828×1026 watts through sustained thermonuclear fusion, has done so for 4.6 billion years, has no license, has never filed a report, and has already caused one documented major infrastructure failure. The violation is continuing.

By Ambrose Quill · October 13, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Facility

The Sun is a sustained thermonuclear fusion reactor. It is 1.4 million kilometers across, the equivalent of 109 Earths set side by side, and its energy is generated by the fusion of hydrogen into helium in a core where the temperature reaches more than 15 million Kelvin.1 It is, in other words, a nuclear reactor by the ordinary technical meaning of the term: a device in which nuclear reactions are initiated and sustained, producing useful energy. Its luminosity, the total power output radiated to space, is 3.828×1026 watts.2 The National Aeronautics and Space Administration puts the figure at 3.8×1026 joules per second, and computes that this requires the conversion of roughly 4 million tons of the Sun’s mass into energy every second.3

The facility has been operating continuously since approximately 4.6 billion years ago, when hydrogen fusion ignited in its core and the Sun was born.4 It will, by NASA’s own estimate, continue operating for roughly 5 billion more years, at which point it will expand into a red giant and engulf Mercury, Venus, and possibly Earth.5 This is relevant to the enforcement posture discussed below. The facility has no decommissioning plan on file, no scheduled shutdown, and a published end-of-life scenario that involves the destruction of the planet it illuminates.

The Sun is, by the ordinary technical meaning of the term, a nuclear reactor: a sustained thermonuclear fusion reaction converting roughly 4 million tons of mass into energy every second, with no decommissioning plan on file.

II. The Licensing Regime

The United States maintains a comprehensive licensing regime for nuclear reactors. Section 101 of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 provides that it shall be unlawful, except as provided in section 2121 of the title, for any person within the United States to manufacture, produce, transfer, acquire, possess, use, import, or export any utilization or production facility except under and in accordance with a license issued by the Commission pursuant to section 2133 or 2134 of the title.6 The language is deliberately exhaustive. Manufacture, produce, transfer, acquire, possess, use. The statute covers the entire lifecycle of the facility, from birth to operation, and attaches a licensing requirement to each verb.

The Commission’s implementing regulation is equally explicit. Section 50.10 of Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations provides that no person within the United States shall manufacture, produce, transfer, acquire, possess, or use any production or utilization facility except as authorized by a license issued by the Commission, and further provides that no person may begin the construction of a production or utilization facility on a site on which the facility is to be operated until that person has been issued a construction permit.7 Construction first, license first. The regulation contemplates no grandfather clause for facilities whose construction predates the regulation. The Sun’s construction, which occurred approximately 4.6 billion years before the regulation’s promulgation, did not have a construction permit.

Section 103 of the Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 2133, authorizes the Commission to issue commercial licenses for utilization and production facilities for industrial or commercial purposes.8 Licenses under this section are issued for a specified period not exceeding forty years, and may be renewed. The forty-year cap is the first of several provisions the Sun’s licensing case will struggle with. The facility has been operating for 4.6 billion years. The longest licensed operating term in the American program is eighty years, reached only by a handful of units under subsequent license renewal. The Sun exceeds the statutory maximum license term by a factor of roughly 115 million.

The regulatory apparatus that administers these provisions is not small. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission currently lists 95 commercial nuclear power plants licensed to operate in the United States, 64 pressurized-water reactors and 31 boiling-water reactors, generating about 20 percent of the nation’s electrical use.9 The agency’s Information Digest reports 94 commercial reactors operating in 28 states at 54 sites, generating about 18.6 percent of gross United States electricity, roughly 779 billion kilowatt-hours, and records about 5,960 total inspection and assessment hours at each operating reactor in fiscal year 2024.10 Every licensed reactor in the country is inspected, assessed, reported, and renewed. The unlicensed reactor is none of these things.

III. The Capacity Comparison

The arithmetic is the heart of the matter, and it is not disputed by anyone, because it is arithmetic. The entire licensed American commercial fleet has a combined generating capacity on the order of 100 gigawatts electric. The unlicensed facility’s luminosity is 3.828×1026 watts. Dividing the latter by the former yields a ratio of approximately 3.8×1015: the Sun’s power output exceeds the combined capacity of every licensed reactor in the United States by a factor of roughly 3.8 quadrillion.

The entire licensed fleet of the United States generates less than one quadrillionth of the Sun’s output. Every gigawatt of it is licensed. None of the Sun’s 3.828×1026 watts is.

Considered at the unit level, the comparison is worse. A large licensed commercial reactor produces on the order of one gigawatt. The Sun exceeds a single licensed unit by a factor of roughly 3.8×1017, which is to say that one unlicensed stellar reactor equals, in round numbers, 380 quadrillion licensed reactors. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission devotes approximately 5,960 inspection and assessment hours per year to each operating reactor it licenses. The agency devotes zero inspection hours per year to a facility 380 quadrillion times as powerful. The allocation of the agency’s inspection resources is, on a per-watt basis, not obviously optimal.

Nor is the unlicensed facility’s output consumed elsewhere. At Earth’s distance, NASA measures the solar flux at 1,400 joules per second per square meter, and computes that the Earth as a whole intercepts 1.8×1017 watts of the Sun’s output every second.3 The entire planet sits inside the facility’s discharge plume. The facility’s effluent is weather, climate, agriculture, and photosynthesis. The receiving environment is everything.

IV. The Unmonitored Effluent

A licensed reactor’s effluents are monitored with obsessive granularity. Gaseous and liquid effluents are sampled, measured, reported, and limited by technical specification. The environmental monitoring program of the United States, meanwhile, continuously measures the unlicensed facility’s emissions while drawing no regulatory inference from them. The Environmental Protection Agency’s RadNet system operates 140 radiation air monitors across all 50 states, running 24 hours a day, seven days a week, collecting near-real-time measurements of gamma radiation.11 These monitors measure the background radiation of the environment, which is to say they measure, among other things, the output of the unlicensed reactor and its secondary products. The data are reviewed continuously. No enforcement action has ever followed.

The facility also emits, continuously, a stream of charged particles known as the solar wind, which NASA notes forms a huge magnetic bubble around the Sun called the heliosphere, extending beyond the orbit of the planets. NASA’s own statement of the geometry is worth quoting for the record: Earth exists inside the Sun’s atmosphere.4 Every licensed reactor in the United States is required to monitor its exclusion area. The unlicensed reactor’s exclusion area is the orbit of Neptune.

Periodically, the facility discharges coronal mass ejections, expulsions of plasma and magnetic field from its outer atmosphere. These are the effluent events the licensing framework would classify, at a licensed facility, as unplanned releases. At the unlicensed facility, they are called space weather, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration maintains a Space Weather Prediction Center whose entire function is to issue warnings about them, like a fire department for a building the fire marshal has never inspected.

V. The Recordable Event

On September 1, 1859, the English astronomer Richard Carrington observed a sudden white-light eruption from a group of sunspots. It was the first recorded observation of a solar flare. Within roughly 17.6 hours, a billion-ton coronal mass ejection crossed the 150 million kilometers to Earth and produced the most intense geomagnetic storm in recorded history.12 Aurorae were visible as far south as the Caribbean and Central America. Telegraph systems worldwide failed. Spark discharges shocked telegraph operators and set telegraph paper on fire. On the line between Boston and Portland, Maine, two operators held a conversation over wires that had been disconnected from their batteries, exchanging messages on what they called the auroral current alone. The storm was powering the lines.13

This was, in the taxonomy of licensed operations, an unplanned release with offsite consequences. It was the largest such event in the instrumental record, and it caused the only documented nineteenth-century infrastructure failure attributable to a nuclear facility’s effluent. No licensee event report was filed. No root-cause analysis was submitted to the Commission. No corrective action was tracked to closure. The facility resumed normal operation immediately, as it has after every such event for 4.6 billion years, without so much as a confirmatory action letter.

The most intense geomagnetic storm in recorded history knocked the Victorian internet offline, set telegraph paper on fire, and powered disconnected lines on auroral current alone. No licensee event report was filed.

The near-misses are also on the record. On July 23, 2012, a powerful explosion on the Sun hurled a Carrington-class coronal mass ejection away from Earth’s position. It missed. Professor Daniel Baker of the University of Colorado, summarizing the event at NOAA’s Space Weather Workshop, observed that had it struck, we would still be picking up the pieces.13 A NASA-funded study by the National Academy of Sciences concluded that a Carrington-level event today could cause substantial damage to society’s high-tech infrastructure and require years for complete recovery.13 One risk model, reported by Lloyd’s of London, estimated that a comparable event today could cost the United States up to $2.6 trillion.14 The licensed fleet’s entire annual generation is not worth that much. The unlicensed facility risks it as a matter of routine operation.

VI. The Penalty

Section 234 of the Atomic Energy Act authorizes the Commission to impose civil monetary penalties for violations of the Act. The current maximum, as adjusted for inflation through fiscal year 2025, is $372,240 for each violation, and the rule is explicit that if any violation is a continuing one, each day of such violation shall constitute a separate violation for the purposes of computing the applicable civil penalty.15

The violation is continuing. The Sun has operated without a license for every day since the Atomic Energy Act took effect on August 30, 1954. The auditors will observe the discipline of measuring only the period during which the statute has been in force, rather than the full 4.6 billion years of unlicensed operation, since a penalty cannot accrue before the law exists. That discipline is what makes the number so striking: it is the conservative figure.

From August 30, 1954, to October 13, 2026, is 26,342 days of continuing unlicensed operation. At $372,240 per day, the accrued maximum civil penalty is $9,805,546,080, approximately $9.8 billion.16 This is the penalty for the violation as bounded by the statute’s own effective date. The unbounded figure, the penalty that would have accrued had the Atomic Energy Act been in force for the facility’s entire operating history, is left as an exercise for the reader, along with the observation that it exceeds the gross domestic product of the planet the facility illuminates by a factor the auditors declined to compute, out of a sense of decorum.

At $372,240 per day of continuing violation, the Sun’s unlicensed operation since the Atomic Energy Act took effect in 1954 has accrued a maximum civil penalty of approximately $9.8 billion. This is the conservative figure.

VII. The Liability Gap

The Price-Anderson Act, Section 170 of the Atomic Energy Act, authorizes the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to limit the liability of its reactor licensees for radioactive damages to members of the public, and the Commission indemnifies its licensees against damage awards above that limit. Under the current framework, the primary and secondary insurance layers would provide up to $15.5 billion in public compensation for each nuclear incident, drawn from $158.0 million in retrospective premiums from each of the 95 currently covered reactors.17 The liability regime is, in other words, a closed system for licensed operators: capped, pooled, indemnified, and extended by Congress most recently in 2024.

The unlicensed facility is not a licensee. It therefore holds no Price-Anderson indemnification, pays no retrospective premium, and enjoys no liability cap. Its exposure for the next Carrington-class event is unlimited. The licensed fleet, whose combined output is less than one quadrillionth of the Sun’s, operates under $15.5 billion of pooled protection per incident. The unlicensed facility, whose output is the other 99.9999999999996 percent, operates under no protection at all, which is to say that the entire downside of the next $2.6 trillion event rests on an operator that has never been served with process, cannot be served with process, and, being a star, is judgment-proof in the most literal sense available.

The objection will be raised that the Commission cannot license a star, and that the Atomic Energy Act was not written with stellar objects in mind. The audit does not dispute the Commission’s practical difficulties; it disputes the regulatory asymmetry. The statute says it shall be unlawful for any person to use any utilization facility except under a license. It does not say except stars. The omission is the holding.

VIII. The Remedy

There are, in principle, three lawful courses. The first is enforcement. The Commission should issue a notice of violation for unlicensed operation of a utilization facility, assess the accrued civil penalty of approximately $9.8 billion, and issue an order requiring the operator to cease operation until a license is obtained. The order would be a shutdown order, or in the industry’s term, a scram. The auditors acknowledge the practical difficulty of scramming a star. They note that the difficulty is not a defense. Licensed operators are not excused from shutdown orders on the grounds that shutdown is inconvenient.

The second course is licensing. The Commission could accept a license application for the facility under Section 103, issue the license for the statutory maximum of forty years, and place the facility under the Reactor Oversight Process, with resident inspectors, performance indicators, and a corrective action program. The application would need to address the 4.6 billion years of prior unlicensed operation, the absence of a construction permit, the Carrington event and its undocumented corrective actions, the missing decommissioning plan, and the published end-of-life scenario involving the engulfment of the host planet. The auditors estimate the review would take slightly longer than the eighteen months typical for a combined license.

The third course is the one the Commission will presumably take, which is to do nothing, to continue devoting 5,960 inspection hours per year to each of 95 licensed reactors generating a fifth of the nation’s electricity, while the unlicensed reactor generating 3.8 quadrillion times their combined output continues to operate without a license, without inspections, without event reports, and without insurance, directly overhead, in plain sight, every day. This course has the advantage of familiarity. It has the disadvantage of being the precise arrangement this investigation has shown to be unlawful.

IX. Conclusion

The Sun is a sustained thermonuclear fusion reactor with a luminosity of 3.828×1026 watts, operating continuously for 4.6 billion years. Section 101 of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 makes it unlawful to use a utilization facility without a Commission license. Section 50.10 of Title 10 requires a construction permit before construction begins. No license has been issued. No permit was obtained. No inspection has been performed. No event report has been filed for the largest geomagnetic storm in recorded history, which the facility caused. The accrued maximum civil penalty for the continuing violation, measured conservatively from the statute’s effective date, is approximately $9.8 billion. The facility carries no Price-Anderson indemnification and no liability cap.

The licensed fleet generates about a fifth of America’s electricity and is inspected 5,960 hours per reactor per year. The unlicensed facility generates everything else and is inspected never. The reactor is the sky. Ergo.
