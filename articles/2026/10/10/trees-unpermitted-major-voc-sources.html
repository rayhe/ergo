Air Quality Law & Forest Botany

Title V of the Clean Air Act Requires Every Major Source Emitting One Hundred Tons Per Year of Any Air Pollutant to Hold an Operating Permit, the Threshold Falls to Fifty, Twenty-Five, or Ten Tons Per Year of Volatile Organic Compounds in Serious, Severe, and Extreme Ozone Nonattainment Areas, the Environmental Protection Agency’s Own 2014 National Emissions Inventory Counts 38.7 Million Tons of Biogenic Volatile Organic Compounds Per Year Against 16.5 Million Tons From All Human Activity Combined, the Agency’s Preferred Methods Document States That Natural Source VOC Is Estimated to Be Higher Than Anthropogenic Source VOC and That the Dominant Natural Source Is Vegetation, Isoprene Emitted by Broadleaved Trees Totals Approximately Five Hundred Million Metric Tons of Carbon Per Year Worldwide, the Blue Ridge Mountains Are Named for the Blue Haze of Isoprene Rising From Their Forests, No Tree Has Ever Applied for a Title V Operating Permit, No Forest Has Ever Filed for New Source Review, and the Largest Unpermitted Emission Source in American History Runs on Sunlight

Title V of the Clean Air Act requires an operating permit for every major source emitting one hundred tons per year of any air pollutant, with lower thresholds in ozone nonattainment areas. The Environmental Protection Agency’s own inventory counts 38.7 million tons of biogenic volatile organic compounds per year, more than twice the 16.5 million tons from all human activity, and the agency’s methods document states plainly that the dominant natural source of VOC is vegetation. Isoprene, emitted continuously in daylight by broadleaved trees, totals roughly five hundred million metric tons of carbon per year worldwide. No permit application has ever been filed. The emissions peak at midday, in summer, exactly as the photochemistry requires.

By Thaddeus Grimble · October 10, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Threshold

The Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990 added Title V to the Act, requiring states to administer a comprehensive operating-permit program for sources emitting air pollutants, modeled on the permit provisions of the Clean Water Act.1 Before Title V, the Act required construction permits only for new or modified major stationary sources, under Section 165. Title V extended the permit obligation to the operation of existing sources themselves. After the effective date of a state’s approved program, every covered source had twelve months to submit its permit application, and the states were to collect annual fees from sources sufficient to cover the reasonable costs of administering the program.

A source is subject to the program if it is a major source: one that emits or has the potential to emit one hundred tons per year or more of any regulated air pollutant.1 In ozone nonattainment areas, the statute tightens the net. The major-source threshold for volatile organic compounds falls to fifty tons per year in serious areas, twenty-five tons per year in severe areas, and ten tons per year in extreme areas.1 And in severe and extreme areas that fail to attain the ozone standard, Section 185 of the Act imposes fees on major sources of VOCs of $5,000 per ton emitted.1 The program has now stood for thirty-six years. In those thirty-six years, the operator this investigation concerns has never once applied.

II. The Operator

The operator is the American forest, and its product is isoprene. Isoprene, 2-methyl-1,3-butadiene, C5H8, is a highly volatile and reactive unsaturated hydrocarbon produced continuously in daylight by many terrestrial plants, and in great abundance by broadleaved trees.4 On a mass basis it is the most important biogenic volatile organic compound emitted by vegetation, with an annual global emission of approximately 0.5 × 1015 grams of carbon, a flux similar in magnitude to the total annual emission of methane from all natural sources combined.4 Oaks, poplars, sweetgums, cottonwoods, and eucalyptus are among the documented emitters.10 Conifers contribute their own share as monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes, released in smaller amounts, often by conifers and stressed vegetation.8

Biogenic volatile organic compounds account for approximately ninety percent of global annual VOC emissions.5 The emissions are functions of leaf mass, emission factors, temperature, and light conditions; rising temperatures generally amplify them, and a two-to-three-degree Celsius rise in mean global temperature is expected to increase total biogenic VOC emissions by thirty to forty-five percent worldwide.8 The operator does not throttle back in heat. It throttles up.

The operator’s product is isoprene, emitted continuously in daylight by broadleaved trees at an annual global flux of roughly five hundred million metric tons of carbon, comparable to all the methane emitted by all natural sources combined. It is, by mass, the most important volatile organic compound vegetation produces.

A careful reader will note that the Clean Air Act was written with factories in mind. This investigation notes it too, and proceeds anyway, on the authority of the government’s own inventory. When the Environmental Protection Agency counts an emission, this publication takes the agency at its word. The word “tons” is doing the work. The threshold is doing the rest.

III. The Scale of the Violation

The Environmental Protection Agency’s 2014 National Emissions Inventory, the agency’s own accounting, reports the following: anthropogenic volatile organic compounds, 16,478 thousand tons; biogenic volatile organic compounds, 38,679 thousand tons.2 Nature out-emits the entire human economy by a factor of 2.3 to 1, and the agency prints both numbers in the same table.

The agency’s preferred-methods document for biogenic sources states the finding in plain language: “The dominant natural source of VOC as nonmethane hydrocarbons (NMHC) is vegetation.” It continues: “For the United States, natural source VOC is estimated to be higher than anthropogenic source VOC.”3 Forests and agriculture contribute the largest share. The Southeastern and South Central portions of the United States account for approximately forty-three percent of the national natural estimate.

The agency’s inventory: 38,679 thousand tons of biogenic volatile organic compounds against 16,478 thousand tons from all human activity. The agency’s methods document: natural source VOC is estimated to be higher than anthropogenic source VOC, and the dominant natural source is vegetation. This is not an allegation. It is the Environmental Protection Agency describing the operator.

Against the Title V threshold of one hundred tons per year, the national biogenic total exceeds the major-source cutoff by a factor of 386,790, computed from the agency’s own figures.11 In a serious ozone nonattainment area, where the threshold is fifty tons per year, the exceedance factor is 773,580. In an extreme area, at ten tons per year, it is 3,867,900. The refinery down the road files its application at one hundred and one tons. The forest files nothing at thirty-eight million.

IV. The Visible Evidence

The operator does not conceal its emissions. It advertises them across the visible spectrum. The Blue Ridge Mountains, extending from Pennsylvania to Georgia, are known and named for their distinctive blue hue.6 The cause is documented: a volatile hydrocarbon compound known as isoprene is what gives the mountains their colorful appearance; large quantities of isoprene are released by the temperate forests that line the mountain range, and the isoprene reacts with other molecules when released into the atmosphere, creating a blanket of haze that appears blue when viewed from a distance.6

The trees put the blue in Blue Ridge, from the isoprene released into the atmosphere.7 The compound is colorless, volatile, and, rising from the trees, smells vaguely like tires.7 A mountain range named for its own unpermitted emissions is the rarest of regulatory phenomena: a source that has been in open, notorious, continuous violation for the entire thirty-six-year life of the permit program, its noncompliance visible from the next state over, and the subject of scenic byways, national parks, and license-plate slogans.

A mountain range named for its own unpermitted emissions. The violation is visible from the next state over, and the subject of scenic byways, national parks, and license-plate slogans.

V. The Ozone Record

Volatile organic compounds are ozone precursors. In the presence of sunlight, VOCs react with nitrogen oxides in the atmosphere to form ground-level ozone, a harmful pollutant regulated under a National Ambient Air Quality Standard of 70 parts per billion, set in 2015.5 Ozone is beneficial high in the Earth’s atmosphere, where it shields against ultraviolet radiation; at ground level it causes breathing problems, plant damage, and an increased greenhouse effect.5

The operator’s contribution to the standard’s violation is not theoretical. In 2026, researchers reported that roughly thirty-five percent of urban trees in Beijing are isoprene emitters, that the isoprene flux accounts for most of the reactive biogenic VOC emissions in the city, and that urban vegetation accounts for approximately one-fourth of Beijing’s ground-level ozone.9 The flux’s reactivity scales with heat: biogenic VOC reactivity rates at 35°C are seven times as high as at temperatures near 20°C, and in hot conditions the rates exceed those of anthropogenic VOCs.9

The National Emissions Inventory exists to support precisely this kind of attainment planning: the agency counts the precursors so the states can write the plans. The inventory counts the operator’s thirty-eight million tons. The plans regulate the other sixteen million. Section 185’s $5,000-per-ton fees hang over major VOC sources in severe and extreme nonattainment areas that fail to attain.1 The operator has never paid a dollar of them, and the areas keep failing to attain, and the haze keeps rising, blue, over the ridgeline, every summer afternoon.

The inventory counts the operator’s thirty-eight million tons so the states can write their attainment plans. The plans regulate the other sixteen million.

VI. New Construction

Title V governs operation. Construction is governed separately, and more strictly. New or modified major stationary sources must obtain preconstruction permits under the New Source Review program, and in attainment areas the Prevention of Significant Deterioration provisions require state determinations of Best Available Control Technology before a shovel of dirt may move.12 No new major source may break ground without the agency’s advance review of its controls.

Every spring, the operator breaks ground on millions of new emission units without advance review of any kind. Each new leaf is a new emission surface; each spring flush is the construction of new emitting capacity, unpermitted, unreviewed, and unaccompanied by any Best Available Control Technology determination. The operator expands its permitted-through-nothing capacity annually, on a schedule set by the photoperiod, and has never once filed for a permit modification.

The agency has, in writing, conceded the shape of this problem. When the one-hundred-ton threshold threatened to sweep more than six million small sources of greenhouse gases into Title V, the agency promulgated the Tailoring Rule, calling the unmodified threshold an “absurd result” that would affect “an extraordinarily large number of small sources.”12 The agency has admitted, in its own rulemaking, that its thresholds produce absurd results when applied beyond the industrial world. It then confined the absurdity to greenhouse gases and left the VOC threshold exactly where it was, at one hundred tons, fifty tons, twenty-five tons, ten.

Every spring, the operator constructs millions of new emission units, each new leaf a new emission surface, without a preconstruction permit, a Best Available Control Technology determination, or a permit modification. The agency has admitted in writing that its own thresholds produce absurd results. It confined the absurdity to greenhouse gases.

VII. The Definitional Void

Title V applies, in the agency’s implementation, to man-made sources, and this investigation discloses the fact plainly, as the genre requires. The Environmental Protection Agency has never treated a forest as a stationary source. Its own documents sort the operator’s emissions into the categories “natural” and “biogenic,” counts them carefully, and permits none of them. The thirty-eight million tons sit in the inventory beside the sixteen million, counted and unregulated, in the same table, every inventory cycle.

The statute’s text does not do the exempting; the implementation does. A program written to capture every source emitting one hundred tons per year of any air pollutant, with thresholds falling to ten tons in the dirtiest air basins in the country, exempts by interpretation the single largest emitter of volatile organic compounds in the nation. The void is not in the arithmetic. The void is in the definition, and the definition is the one place the agency declined to look.

There is also the question of whom to bill. The Title V program funds itself through annual fees collected from permitted sources, sufficient to cover the reasonable costs of administering the program.1 The operator has never been billed. It has no mailing address, no registered agent, and no account number, unless the agency is prepared to invoice a stand of oaks, which it is not, although the stand could afford it: at Section 185’s $5,000-per-ton rate, the national biogenic total would price the fee program’s annual revenue in the hundreds of billions.

VIII. The Enforcement Impossibility

The Environmental Protection Agency possesses formidable tools. It can assess civil penalties under 42 U.S.C. § 7413. It can issue compliance orders. It can refer criminal cases. Against the operator, every tool misfires. There is no person to penalize, because the operator is not a person. There is no facility to order into compliance, because the facility is the eastern deciduous forest. There is no control technology to require, because Best Available Control Technology for isoprene would be the absence of leaves, and the agency has no procedure for ordering an oak to defoliate.

The agency could demand the application that Title V requires. The application would have to list, as the source, approximately three hundred million acres of American forestland; as the pollutant, volatile organic compounds in the tens of millions of tons; and as the responsible official, no one. The permitting authority has no form for no one.

The program has stood for thirty-six years. In that time the operator has emitted, by the agency’s own accounting, well over a trillion tons of volatile organic compounds without a permit, without a fee, without a preconstruction review, and without a single Best Available Control Technology determination.11 It has never applied. It has never been granted. It has never been penalized, never been billed, never been throttled, and never once filed for a permit modification for its spring construction program. The refinery files its application. The power plant pays its fee. The chemical plant submits to its BACT review. The operator emits thirty-eight million tons a year, names a mountain range after the haze, and operates on.
