# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Cats as an Unpermitted Migratory Bird Take Operation"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: long factual headline chaining statutory text to enforcement void, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from prohibition to liability standard to take to operators to permits to enforcement void to penalty arithmetic to conclusion, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly: the statute's totalizing language, the strict-liability standard, the peer-reviewed take figures, the tens of millions of operators, the permit program with no feline provision, the incidental-take lifeline that cannot save intentional killers, the complete enforcement void, the trillion-dollar penalty arithmetic. The load-bearing beam is the combination of "at any time, by any means or in any manner" with strict liability and the 1.3-to-4.0-billion figure, and it holds through all eight sections. The M-37050 inversion, that the one interpretive lifeline industry ever received covers only incidental take and cats are not incidental, is the piece's sharpest single legal observation. Minor weakness: Section VI's wind-turbine-permit comparison restates Section V's permit-void point; and the headline's final clause ("sleep sixteen hours a day") is the only clause that leans on color rather than fact.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the salmon and pigeon pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "The purr is not exculpatory," "The feeder concentrates the protected resource. The cat harvests it," "the one means that accounts for the single greatest share of anthropogenic bird mortality in the United States has never once appeared in a charging instrument," "It has whiskers. It knocks objects off tables." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is "a voluntary compliance program with no penalty for noncompliance, no inspection authority, and no reporting requirement. It is, in regulatory terms, a suggestion," which lands as straight-faced procedural analysis. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes, regulations, case holdings, and figures cited are real and verified against primary sources during drafting: 16 U.S.C. 703(a) quoted verbatim from the House Office of the Law Revision Counsel text; the 2023 revision of 50 CFR 10.13 to 1,106 species from 88 FR 49310; the 2026 proposed revision to 1,131 from the Ornithological Council summary of the proposed rule; the FMC Corp. holding and jury instruction from the Second Circuit opinion; the Engler, Manning, and Corrow scienter holdings; 16 U.S.C. 707(a)'s $500/six-month penalty text with the modern Class A misdemeanor fine characterization attributed to the CRS report; Loss, Will & Marra 2013 (Nature Communications 4:1396) figures quoted from the abstract; APPA's 53-million-household figure from the September 2026 Cat Report; the M-37050/M-37065 timeline; and the 50 CFR Part 21 permit categories from the Service's own policy library. One pre-publication correction was applied during drafting: the deck's per-minute take rate was recomputed from the annual figures (approximately five thousand per minute at the midpoint, not one hundred thousand). The deliberately wrong conclusion, that the MBTA's take prohibition applies to cats, is the disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends the Service has ever read the statute that way. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"No cat has ever held a permit" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone. "The purr is not exculpatory" is the single most shareable line; "The feeder concentrates the protected resource. The cat harvests it" and "It has whiskers. It knocks objects off tables" are strong pull-quote material. The hero image, a uniformed officer serving an MBTA violation tag on a smug orange tabby with a feather in its mouth, is built for the thumbnail. Universally graspable premise: everyone knows what a cat is and most readers have seen one hunt. Very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
The statutory text is quoted verbatim. The strict-liability holdings are accurately rendered, including the precise Engler formulation ("scienter is not an element of criminal liability under the Act's misdemeanor provisions"). The penalty discussion correctly states the statutory $500/six-month text and separately attributes the $15,000-per-count modern characterization. The 10.13 list figures are current through the 2023 final rule with the 2026 proposal properly flagged as proposed. The M-37050 saga is accurately summarized, and the piece's key legal move, that the vacated incidental-take opinion could never have covered cats because feline take is intentional rather than incidental, is sound analysis rather than invention. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that cats are "takers" within the meaning of the Act, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece is honest about the enforcement void being a choice rather than an oversight. Passes threshold.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: the House codification and Cornell LII for the statute, govinfo.gov for the Federal Register list revision, fws.gov's policy library for the permit program, Justia for the FMC Corp. opinion, the published Nature Communications paper (via the American Bird Conservancy's hosted PDF) for the take figures, the APPA report via trade press for ownership figures, and the Federal Register/Justia regulatory record for the M-37050 timeline. Minor weakness: the unowned-cat population figures (30 to 100 million) come from secondary compilations rather than a single primary survey, which is the nature of the available evidence for an unowned population and is transparently presented as a range from two sources.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 1,106 species (1,131 proposed); 1.3 to 4.0 billion birds, 6.3 to 22.3 billion mammals; 53 million households (39%), 49 million in 2024, 40 million in 2023; 74 million pet cats; 30 to 100 million unowned; 7.3 million birds per day and roughly five thousand per minute at the estimate midpoint; $650 billion to $2 trillion in annual fine exposure at the statutory rate, $19.5 trillion at the modern per-count rate; one agent per three hundred thousand feline defendants. The comparison framework, permitted industrial takers versus the unpermitted feline sector on every metric, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
