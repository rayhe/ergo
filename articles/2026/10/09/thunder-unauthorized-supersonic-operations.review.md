# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Thunder as Unauthorized Supersonic Operations"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: long factual headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from prohibition to operator to scale to missing application to noise record to definitional void to enforcement impossibility, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly, and the load-bearing beam is the Section 91.818 application-requirements inversion: a regulatory scheme so complete it demands the operator's name, aircraft models, flight count, date range, and time of day, applied to an operator that can supply none of them. The Boom Supersonic contrast in Section III, thirteen months of process for twenty flights against billions of unauthorized booms, is the piece's sharpest single observation. Minor weakness: Section V (night operations) is the shortest section and partially restates the night clause already introduced in Section IV; it earns its keep with the 3 AM squall-line beat but could have been folded. The headline's final clause ("runs on weather") is slightly less crisp than the lightning piece's "runs on rain," but the parallel is intentional and lands.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the lightning and mosquito pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "The Office of Environment and Energy has no form for forever," "the cloud would deserve it," "a boomer that cannot hold a pen," "Its thump is the thing the X-59 was built to avoid being," "the margin by which weather exceeds paperwork." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is "unless the agency is prepared to serve a summons on a cumulonimbus cloud," delivered as straight-faced procedural analysis. The Section II pivot, taking NOAA at its word on the government's own vocabulary, is the voice at its best. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes, regulations, and agency statements cited are real and quoted verbatim where quoted: 14 CFR 91.817(a)'s Mach 1 prohibition from the eCFR text, the five 91.818 application contents and the night-operations justification clause from the FAA's own grant letter, the 1973 ban and Oklahoma City test figures, the NOAA shock-wave and 54,000-degree statements quoted exactly, the NWS 20-to-25-million strike count, the Boom authorization terms (March 24, 2023 petition; April 7, 2024 grant; twenty flights; R-2508; 30,000 feet MSL), the X-59 milestones, and the 0.11 psf NPRM figure. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that thunder is an unauthorized supersonic operation under Part 91, is the disclosed conceit; the piece is honest about the definitional escape hatch, thunder is not a civil aircraft, in the same way prior pieces disclosed theirs. No invented figures: the "billions" and "1.3 billion" counts are computed conservatively from cited sources and flagged as computed. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"Thunder has never filed for a flight authorization" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone. The Boom Supersonic contrast, thirteen months of paperwork for twenty flights versus billions of booms with none, is the single most shareable paragraph; "no form for forever" and "the cloud would deserve it" are strong pull-quote material. The hero image, an FAA inspector holding a DENIED authorization beneath a lightning strike with a NOTICE OF VIOLATION staked in the mud, is built for the thumbnail. Slightly less universally graspable than the mosquito premise because the setup requires one paragraph of aviation-regulation exposition, the deck does that work. Very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The regulatory citations are accurate: 91.817(a)'s prohibition quoted verbatim from the eCFR, 91.818's application contents rendered faithfully from the FAA grant letter including the night-operations justification language, the 1973 ban's history correctly summarized. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that thunder's shock wave is a supersonic operation within the meaning of Part 91, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends the regulation defines thunder as an aircraft; Section VII discloses the gap plainly. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: the eCFR via Cornell LII for the regulation text, the FAA's own special flight authorization grant letter for the 91.818 contents and the Boom terms, the FAA's NPRM PDF for the 0.11 psf proposal, NOAA JetStream for the shock-wave science, the National Weather Service via the Iowa mesonet archive for the strike counts, and NASA for the Quesst program history. The 2026 developments (X-59 first supersonic flight date, NPRM publication) rely on aviation trade press (Simple Flying) rather than primary FAA/NASA releases, transparently attributed; the NPRM's substance is corroborated by the FAA's own PDF. Minor weakness, honestly noted.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 1,253 booms over six months; 40,000 Air Force claims; 54,000 degrees Fahrenheit, five times the sun's surface; 20 to 25 million strikes per year; twenty authorized flights at 30,000 feet; thirteen months of process; Mach 1.4 at 55,030 feet; 0.11 pounds per square foot; 1.3 billion computed boom events over fifty-three years. The comparison framework, the compliant industry versus the unpermitted atmospheric sector on every metric, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
