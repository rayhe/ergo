Investigation About
Landlord-Tenant Law & Cimicidology

Section 27-2018.2 of the New York City Administrative Code Requires Every Owner of a Multiple Dwelling to File an Annual Bedbug Report With the Department of Housing Preservation and Development, Section 7-28-830 of the Chicago Municipal Code Requires Every Landlord to Hire a Licensed Pest Management Professional Within Ten Days of a Report and Treat Until No Evidence of Bedbugs Remains, the Common Bedbug Feeds for About Five Minutes a Night on Human Blood and Can Survive a Year Without Feeding, and the Occupants Who Trigger All of This Paperwork Hold No Lease, Pay No Rent, Have Never Been Served With Process, and Cannot Be Evicted

The common bedbug is a wingless insect the size of an apple seed that feeds exclusively on the blood of warm-blooded animals, usually at night, usually for about five minutes, usually painlessly. Two American cities have built elaborate legal regimes around its tenancy: annual reports to housing agencies, disclosure forms at lease signing, ten-day hiring deadlines, three-year record retention, and damages of two months’ rent or twice the actual loss. The occupant at the center of the regime signed nothing, pays nothing, and answers to no process server. This investigation examines the most secure tenancy in American housing law, held by the tenant who was never offered a lease.

By Ambrose Wexford · October 30, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Occupancy

The common bedbug, Cimex lectularius, of the family Cimicidae, is a small, wingless, blood-feeding insect, and the species best adapted to human environments.1 It is reddish-brown, flat, and oval, roughly the size and shape of an apple seed, and it conducts its entire tenancy in the seams of mattresses, the joints of bed frames, the screw holes of headboards, the edges of baseboards, the voids inside walls, and the backs of picture frames.2 The flattened body that lets it occupy a crack no wider than a credit card is the same body that defeats every inspection conducted without a flashlight.

The occupant is nocturnal. Its peak feeding period arrives about an hour before dawn. Attracted by warmth and by the carbon dioxide of exhalation, it pierces the skin with two hollow tubes: with one it injects saliva containing anticoagulants and anesthetics, and with the other it withdraws the blood.1 A typical meal lasts about five minutes, though some authorities allow ten to twenty, and the bite itself is painless; what the host feels, minutes or hours later, is the skin’s reaction to the injected agents, not the feeding.13 After feeding, the bug returns to its hiding place. It feeds every five to ten days, and it can survive for months without feeding at all.4

The occupant moves in without an application. Bedbugs are adept hitchhikers, traveling on luggage, furniture, bedding, boxes, backpacks, and clothing, and they can travel up to six meters from a hiding place in search of a host.3 No credit check is performed. No references are called. The federal government has taken notice of the move-in pattern: in a 2010 joint statement, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Environmental Protection Agency reported an alarming national resurgence in bedbug populations, attributed to increased pesticide resistance, greater international and domestic travel, and the decline of vector control programs, and declared that although bedbugs are not known to transmit disease, they are a pest of significant public health importance.5 The resurgence continues. The applications are never filed.

II. The Lease

There is no written instrument. In California, the statute of frauds provides that an agreement for the leasing of real property for a longer period than one year is invalid unless it, or some note or memorandum of it, is in writing and subscribed by the party to be charged.6 The bedbug’s tenancy is indefinite in duration and unwritten in form. Were anyone to attempt to enforce its terms, the statute would void them. No one can attempt it, because there is no counterparty to charge and no signature to authenticate.

The file contains no application, no credit report, no security deposit, no guarantor, and no first and last month’s rent. What the file contains, instead, is reproduction. The female lays some 200 to 250 eggs in her lifetime, each egg pearly white, a millimeter long, and glued into a crack or a seam where it is very hard to spot.7 The nymph passes through five stages before adulthood, and before each molt it must take a blood meal; at room temperature the journey from egg to adult takes about a month, and multiple generations can be produced in a single year.7 The tenancy assigns itself to its own offspring without an assignment clause, sublets to its own nymphs without a sublease, and renews every five weeks without a renewal notice.

The law has a name for an occupant without a written lease: a tenant at will, or a periodic tenant, holding month to month. The bedbug holds to no period. It does not pay monthly, because it does not pay. It does not give notice, because it was never asked for notice. It is the only periodic tenant whose period is the lifespan of the building.

III. The Rent

The bug pays nothing. It collects nightly, in blood, from the tenant, who pays dollars to the landlord. The building therefore contains two tenancies and one lease. The human’s rent is denominated in currency and due on the first. The bug’s rent is denominated in blood and due whenever the human sleeps.

The human’s lease, unlike the bug’s, comes with an implied warranty. In Javins v. First National Realty Corp., 428 F.2d 1071 (D.C. Cir. 1970), the court held that a warranty of habitability, measured by the housing code, is implied by operation of law into leases of urban dwelling units, and that breach of the warranty gives rise to the usual remedies for breach of contract, including defenses to eviction for nonpayment.8 The human tenant may therefore withhold rent over the bug. The bug withholds nothing and pays nothing. The landlord’s duty runs to the human; the human’s blood runs to the bug; the consideration flows in exactly one direction at each link of the chain, and the bug sits at the end of it, receiving.

The bug holds no lease, pays no rent, and has never been served with process. It is the most secure tenant in the building.

The economics are difficult to dispute. A single female’s 250 eggs, maturing in five weeks per generation, convert one unleased occupant into a population, and the population dines every five to ten days, painlessly, in the dark, on the party whose name is on the lease. The landlord collects the rent. The bug collects the rent’s collector.

IV. The Notice

An eviction is a sequence, and the sequence is mandatory. First comes notice: to pay rent or quit, to cure a violation or quit, or simply to quit within thirty days. Then comes a court action for unlawful detainer. Then a judgment. Then a writ of possession, executed by the sheriff or the marshal, who restores the premises to the landlord. Every step of the sequence requires that process reach the defendant. Service on an individual means delivery to the person, or to a person of suitable age at the person’s dwelling, followed by mailing. The bug is not a person, has no dwelling separate from the dwelling it occupies, and cannot be mailed anything.

The unlawful detainer complaint traditionally reserves Doe defendants, numbered 1 through 10, for occupants whose names the plaintiff does not know. In an ordinary case the Does are placeholders, later amended to real names once discovery identifies the subtenants. In a bedbug matter the Does are not placeholders. They are the tenancy. Amending the complaint to substitute a true name would require learning the true name of an insect the size of an apple seed that hides in a wall void by day and feeds for five minutes an hour before dawn. The process server works business hours. The schedules do not overlap, and the defendant has never once been home when the server knocked, because the defendant lives inside the wall the server knocked on.

Consider the position of the landlord’s counsel. The client wishes to remove an occupant. Counsel must advise that no notice can be served, because there is no one to serve; that no complaint can name the occupant, because the occupant has no name; and that the writ, if it issued, would command the sheriff to restore possession of premises the sheriff already possesses on paper. The landlord holds the reversion. The bug holds the mattress. The law, which is precise about everything else, has no form for this.

V. The Holdover

The holdover tenant is the one who stays after the lease ends. The bug improves on the form: it stays after the tenant ends. Adult bedbugs may survive as long as one year without feeding, and a Virginia Tech study measured average unfed survival at about five months, with cooler rooms stretching the interval further.72 The unit goes vacant between tenancies. The painters come. The cleaners come. The bug remains in the wall void, unfed, unhurried, requiring nothing but the return of warmth and carbon dioxide.

The new tenant signs a lease, collects the keys, and moves into an occupied unit. The occupant was there first, never left, and never gave the thirty days’ notice it was never asked to give. Chicago, which has topped the national rankings of most bedbug-affected cities for several consecutive years, answers this exact fact pattern with a prohibition: it is unlawful to rent a unit in which an infestation of bedbugs is found or suspected, unless a pest management professional has inspected the unit and determined that no infestation exists or that the infestation has been exterminated.910 The ordinance, passed June 5, 2013 and effective that December, thus requires the landlord to evict the prior occupant before leasing to the next one.9 The prior occupant is a bug. The eviction it requires is described in the following section, and it involves heating the building.

The holdover’s security exceeds the leaseholder’s by every measure the law provides. The leaseholder can be removed with thirty days’ notice and a writ. The bug cannot be removed with any notice and any writ. The leaseholder’s tenure ends at the expiration of the term. The bug’s tenure ends at a hundred and twenty-two degrees Fahrenheit.

VI. The Disclosure

New York City has decided that the bug’s tenancy history is material to the human’s leasing decision, and it has built two separate disclosure regimes to prove it. First, under Administrative Code § 27-2018.1, the owner must give each tenant signing a vacancy lease a completed bedbug disclosure form, the DBB-N published by the State Division of Housing and Community Renewal, laying out the property’s bedbug history for the previous year.11 The incoming tenant is told, in writing, on a state form, at the signing of the lease, about the prior occupant. The prior occupant is not a party to the lease.

Second, under § 27-2018.2, every owner of a multiple dwelling, meaning a residential building with three or more units, cooperatives and condominiums included, must file an annual bedbug report with the Department of Housing Preservation and Development between December 1 and December 31, covering the period from the previous November 1 through October 31.12 The report asks how many units had a reported infestation and how many received eradication treatment. The filing is mandatory even when the building had zero reported cases all year; the city’s own guidance answers that question with one word, yes, and a violation issues for failure to file.12 Within sixty days of filing, the owner must distribute or post the filing receipt along with the Health Department’s Stop Bed Bugs Safely guide, or provide both at each lease commencement and renewal.13

The precision of the regime has one revealing gap. The annual report counts infested apartments; it does not require the owner to disclose bedbugs found in hallways or other common areas, where the bugs travel freely along pipes and baseboards.14 The law tracks the occupant unit by unit and loses it in the hallway, which is where the occupant goes when it wishes to change units without filing a change-of-address form.

Bedbugs are a Class B hazardous violation in New York City, and the owner has thirty days to correct the condition.13 The violation names the condition, not the occupant. Every document in the regime, the disclosure form, the annual report, the filing receipt, the violation, the treatment invoice, records the tenancy in exhaustive detail and names everyone except the tenant.

VII. The Remedy

There is exactly one eviction the bug recognizes, and it is not served by a process server. Bedbugs die at 122 degrees Fahrenheit. Professional heat treatment raises the air to roughly 135 to 140 degrees, holds it there while sensors confirm that every mattress, wall void, and baseboard has reached the kill temperature, and typically finishes in a single day.15 The writ of possession is thermal. The sheriff is a heater.

The remedy is priced like litigation. Virginia Tech Extension puts professional heat treatment at roughly $500 to $5,000 for a whole home, depending on size and severity; industry data for 2026 places the national average near $2,500 for a single course of treatment, with per-room heat treatment running $400 to $900 and most providers enforcing a $1,000 minimum.1516 Most infestations require more than one visit, so the total exceeds the headline figure, exactly as with attorneys’ fees.

Chicago prescribes the procedure in detail. Once bedbugs are found or reasonably suspected, or once the tenant gives written notice, the landlord has ten days to begin pest control by a licensed pest management professional, store-bought sprays and maintenance-staff improvisation being unacceptable, and must continue treatment until no evidence of bedbugs can be found and verified.17 The landlord must inspect and treat the units on either side and directly above and below, keep the professional’s reports and receipts for three years, and give the tenant the city’s bedbug brochure before the lease is signed or renewed.17 The tenant, for its part, must notify the landlord in writing within five days of finding or suspecting bedbugs, grant access at reasonable times, follow the preparation instructions, and not interfere.17 The law thus conscripts the leaseholder into the eviction of the co-occupant: bag the clothing, empty the closets, vacate the unit, and wait outside while the temperature does what the court cannot.

The lawful tenant must leave so that the unlawful occupant can be removed. No other eviction in American law requires the plaintiff in possession to vacate the premises to effect service on the defendant. If the landlord fails in these duties, the tenant may terminate or recover possession and collect two months’ rent or twice the actual damages, whichever is greater, plus reasonable attorney’s fees.18 The bug, which caused the entire proceeding, is liable for none of it. It holds no assets, keeps no bank account, and cannot be joined as a party.

VIII. The Defense

The defense will now be stated, plainly, as the conventions of honest satire require. Animals are property under the law, not persons. A tenancy requires an agreement, and the bug agreed to nothing. Adverse possession, the doctrine by which an occupant without title can ripen into an owner, requires in California five years of continuous possession plus the payment of all property taxes levied on the land, and the bug has never paid a tax bill, never filed a return, and never held an interest the assessor could lien. The bug is not a tenant. The bug is not an occupant within the meaning of any statute. The bug is an insect.

The defense is noted. It does not change the occupancy.

The regulatory apparatus treats the bug as an occupant for every purpose the law has. Disclosure forms record its history. Violation notices issue for its presence. Treatment records track its eradication. Annual reports count its units. Brochures are printed for its arrival. The statute asks one question that matters: who occupies the dwelling. The dwelling is occupied. The occupant holds no lease, pays no rent, answers no process, survives the vacancy, greets the next tenant on move-in day, and can be removed only by raising the building to a hundred and forty degrees. No lease in the building confers tenure like it.

The record, taken as a whole, describes the most secure tenancy in American housing law. Its holder filed no application and signed no instrument. It pays its rent in the blood of the leaseholder and holds over through every vacancy. Two cities require its history to be disclosed, its presence to be reported, its eradication to be performed by licensed professionals within ten days, and its records to be kept for three years. The process server cannot serve it. The sheriff cannot remove it. The heater can.
