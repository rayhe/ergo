Charter Law & Thanksgiving

Plymouth Colony Operated for Seventy-One Years Without a Royal Charter, Its Only Governing Document Was a Two-Hundred-Word Agreement Signed by Forty-One Men Aboard a Ship Anchored Off Cape Cod, the Patent the Settlers Carried Covered Land Near the Hudson River, They Landed Outside It, and the Only Royal Charter the Colony Ever Received Was the One That Abolished It in 1691

The colony is a settlement that fits on no charter. It was founded by 102 passengers who landed outside the bounds of their patent, governed itself for 71 years under a 200-word agreement signed by 41 men aboard a ship, held elections, passed laws, collected taxes, fought a war, and buried its dead, and it did all of it without a royal charter, and the only royal charter it ever received was the one that abolished it. The Crown has spent four centuries issuing charters to colonies, companies, and corporations. It never issued one to Plymouth. This investigation examines the longest unchartered government in American history, the ship that carried it, the document that stood in for a charter, the feast that celebrated it, and the charter the record demands.

By Ambrose Hale · November 26, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Landing

The voyage began with two ships and ended with one. The passengers set out from England in August 1620 aboard the Mayflower and the Speedwell, and the Speedwell leaked so badly that it was abandoned after two attempts to repair it, and 102 passengers packed aboard the solitary Mayflower, a merchant cargo ship about 100 feet long, and departed Plymouth, England, on September 6, 1620. The ship arrived at Cape Cod on November 9, 1620, after more than two months at sea, and the passengers had sailed past their destination and landed outside the law that was supposed to govern them.12

The patent the settlers carried authorized a settlement somewhere else. In February 1620, the Virginia Company had issued a patent to John Pierce, acting for the joint-stock company that financed the voyage, permitting a settlement in the northern parts of Virginia, near the mouth of the Hudson River. The Mayflower tried to sail south to that coast and was turned back by dangerous shoals off Pollock Rip and contrary winds, and the captain dropped anchor in what is now Provincetown Harbor. Cape Cod lay outside the area the patent covered, and the colonists had permission to settle, but not where they had ended up.13

The landing produced a legal crisis before it produced a settlement. The passengers who were not members of the Leiden congregation, whom the Pilgrims called Strangers, pointed out that the patent was for Virginia and not for New England, and that once ashore none had power to command them. William Bradford recorded that some of them let fall discontented and mutinous speeches, and that they threatened to use their own liberty once they went ashore. The colony had not yet built a single house, and it already had no government, no jurisdiction, and no title to the ground it stood on.34

The crisis was answered on the ship, before anyone went ashore. On November 11, 1620, by the calendar then in use, November 21 by the modern calendar, 41 adult men gathered in the great cabin of the Mayflower and signed a short agreement binding themselves into a civil body politic, to enact laws for the general good of the colony. The document is now known as the Mayflower Compact, and it was the first framework of government written and enacted in the territory that is now the United States, and it was written because the law the settlers had brought with them had expired at the shoreline.25

The law the settlers had brought with them expired at the shoreline.

II. The Document

The document is about 200 words long, and it governed a colony for 71 years. It pledged its signers to combine into a civil body politic, to enact just and equal laws, ordinances, acts, constitutions, and offices for the general good of the colony, and to submit to those laws and to the government they would choose. It was an adaptation of a Puritan church covenant to a civil situation, and it was not a constitution, and it did not solve the matter of the settlers' questionable legal rights to the land, and it remained in force until the colony was absorbed into Massachusetts Bay in 1691.56

The document was signed by a minority of the people it governed. Of the 102 passengers aboard the Mayflower, 41 adult men signed, which is about 40 percent of the people on board. The other 61 never signed, and they included every woman, every child, and every servant on the ship. The signers pledged loyalty to King James, and they elected John Carver as the colony's first governor, and Carver died on April 15, 1621, and William Bradford succeeded him in May and went on to serve more than 30 terms as governor, in annual elections, across the next three decades.57

The document does not survive in its original form. The parchment the 41 men signed is lost, and the oldest known text is the copy William Bradford wrote by hand into his journal, Of Plimoth Plantation, and a printed version appeared in Mourt's Relation, the colony's first promotional pamphlet, published in London in 1622. The colony's founding charter, in other words, exists only as a copy of a copy, printed in a pamphlet, of a document that was signed on a ship, by men who had no authority to sign it, in a harbor outside their patent.28

The document was put into practice immediately, and the practice was indistinguishable from government. The signers confirmed Carver as governor, and the colony held annual elections for its offices, and the freemen elected a governor and assistants and a representative assembly called the General Court, and the colony kept court records, passed laws, divided land, collected taxes, raised a militia, and made war and peace. It performed every function of a chartered government, and it performed them under a document that no king had signed, no court had sealed, and no parliament had ratified.69

Two hundred words governed a colony for seventy-one years.

III. The Law

The law of the colony is the law of the charter, and the charter is a specific instrument. An English colony in the seventeenth century was founded on a grant from the Crown: a royal charter, or letters patent under the Great Seal, authorizing a named body to plant, rule, order, and govern a named territory. The charter was the title to the land, the warrant for the government, and the defense against rival claimants, and a colony without one existed at the mercy of the Crown. Plymouth Colony never received one.1011

The neighbors all held charters, and the comparison is the whole of the case. The Virginia Company held its charter from 1606. The Council for New England, the body that administered the northern coast, held a royal charter dated November 3, 1620. Massachusetts Bay received a royal charter in 1629, and its governor and company carried the document with them to New England and governed under it for more than half a century. Plymouth sought a royal charter and never received one, and the failure to obtain a charter eventually led to the colony's annexation by Massachusetts Bay in 1691.61213

The colony did obtain paper from the Council for New England, and the paper is the crux of the file. In June 1621, the Council issued a patent to John Pierce covering the settlement, and in 1630 the Council issued a further patent, granted to William Bradford and his associates, confirming the colony's lands and its authority to govern. These were grants from a corporation that itself held a royal charter, and they were not royal charters. A patent from the Council is a lease from a landlord, and a royal charter is a deed from the Crown, and the colony held the lease for 71 years and never held the deed.5614

The settlement itself was built on ground the colony did not own in any English sense. The settlers chose a cleared site on the western shore of Cape Cod Bay, the former village of Patuxet, whose Wampanoag inhabitants had died in an epidemic a few years before the Mayflower arrived. In the spring of 1621, the colony concluded a treaty of peace and mutual defense with Ousamequin, the Wampanoag sachem known to the English as Massasoit, and the treaty held for more than fifty years, until King Philip's War of 1675 and 1676. The colony's title to its land rested on a treaty with a sovereign the English Crown had never chartered, and on patents from a council that had never owned the ground.158

The colony held the lease for seventy-one years and never held the deed.

IV. The Feast

The feast was held by the survivors. By the autumn of 1621, about half of the 102 passengers had died in the first winter, of scurvy, pneumonia, and exposure, and 53 Pilgrims remained, 24 men, five women, and 24 children and teenagers. The governor sent four men on fowling, and the four killed in one day as much fowl as, with a little help beside, served the company almost a week, and the colony gathered the fruits of its labor and rejoiced together after a more special manner.816

The feast was attended by more guests than hosts. Edward Winslow, writing in a letter dated December 11, 1621, recorded that many of the Indians came amongst the settlers, and amongst the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some ninety men, whom for three days the colonists entertained and feasted, and the Wampanoag men went out and killed five deer, which they brought to the plantation and bestowed on the governor and the captain. The gathering numbered about 143 people, and 90 of them were guests, and the guests outnumbered the hosts by nearly two to one, at the colony's own table, on land the colony held without a charter.1617

The feast is documented in a single paragraph. Winslow's letter, printed in Mourt's Relation in 1622, is the only surviving eyewitness account of the celebration, and William Bradford's journal is the only other primary source description, and there are two primary sources in total, and neither names the date. Bradford's clues place the event between late September and mid-November of 1621, and the exact day is unknown. The feast that founded the national holiday is attested by one paragraph of one letter, written by one man, about a three-day gathering whose date no one recorded.1718

The feast was not the start of an unbroken tradition. The 1621 gathering was a harvest celebration and a diplomatic occasion, held to renew the alliance with Ousamequin, and it was not repeated annually, and the national holiday is mostly a nineteenth and twentieth century creation. In 1789, George Washington proclaimed November 26 a national day of thanksgiving, and in 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln proclaimed the last Thursday of November a national day of thanksgiving, and in 1941 Congress fixed the date by law as the fourth Thursday of November. The feast waited 320 years for federal recognition, and the colony that held it waited 71 years for a charter that abolished it.1819

The feast that founded the national holiday is attested by one paragraph of one letter.

V. The Seventy-One Years

The colony governed without a charter for longer than most chartered colonies existed. It held annual elections, and the freemen chose a governor, a deputy governor from 1681, assistants, and deputies to the General Court. It passed laws, kept court records from 1623 onward, divided land among the settlers, founded towns, collected taxes, and maintained a militia under Myles Standish, the colony's military commander. It made a treaty, kept the peace for half a century, and then fought King Philip's War in 1675 and 1676, one of the bloodiest wars in American history by proportion of the population killed. It did all of this under a 200-word agreement signed on a ship.7910

The Crown noticed the colony eventually, and the notice was not a charter. In 1686, James II merged Plymouth and the other New England colonies into the Dominion of New England, a single royal government with no elected assembly, and the Dominion collapsed with the Glorious Revolution in 1689. A delegation led by Increase Mather went to England to negotiate the return of the colonial charters, and the situation was particularly problematic for Plymouth, as it had existed without a formal charter since its founding. Plymouth did not get its wish for a formal charter. Instead, a new charter was issued combining Plymouth Colony, Massachusetts Bay Colony, and other territories including Maine.1011

The charter arrived on October 7, 1691, and it was proclaimed on October 17, 1691, and it was put into force on May 14, 1692, when the new royal governor, Sir William Phips, arrived in Boston with the document in hand. It was the first royal charter Plymouth had ever received, and it did not protect the colony's autonomy. It incorporated Plymouth into the Province of Massachusetts Bay, and it ended the colony's existence, and the colony that had sought a charter for 70 years received one that abolished it. Plymouth finally received a royal charter in 1691, and it was not the one it had sought. It was the one that ended it.107

The colony that had sought a charter for seventy years received one that abolished it.

VI. The Arithmetic

The arithmetic is the indictment, and it is short. Seventy-one years passed between the signing of the Compact in 1620 and the charter of 1691, which is more than 25,900 days of unchartered government. Forty-one men signed a document that governed 102 passengers, which is 40 percent of the people aboard, and 61 passengers never signed it. Two hundred words of agreement governed 71 years of government, which is fewer than three words per year. The colony's first and only royal charter arrived 71 years late, and it abolished the colony it was issued to.2510

The feast supplies its own arithmetic. Fifty-three Pilgrims sat down with some 90 Wampanoag men, for three days, and four Englishmen on fowling killed enough birds in one day to feed the English company for almost a week, and the Wampanoag guests killed five deer and brought them to the plantation. The guests outnumbered the hosts 90 to 53, and the celebration lasted three days on a settlement that had existed for less than a year.1617

The holiday supplies the longest arithmetic. The feast was held in the autumn of 1621, and Washington proclaimed a day of thanksgiving in 1789, and Lincoln proclaimed the annual holiday in 1863, and Congress fixed the date by joint resolution in 1941, and the resolution was signed into law on December 26, 1941, as the fourth Thursday of November, which is now 5 U.S.C. 6103. The feast waited 242 years for Lincoln's proclamation and 320 years for the federal statute, and the statute has now been in force for 85 years, which is 14 years longer than the colony it commemorates existed without a charter.1920

The calendar supplies the final figure. Thanksgiving Day in 2026 falls on November 26, the fourth Thursday of the month, which is the 405th year since the autumn of the feast, and it is the same calendar date, November 26, that Washington named in his proclamation of 1789. The holiday has been observed on this date before, and it has never once been observed with a charter in hand.19

Two hundred words of agreement governed seventy-one years of government, which is fewer than three words per year.

VII. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the Pierce patent. In June 1621, the Council for New England issued a patent covering the settlement, and the colony held a grant for its land within seven months of landing. The settlers were not squatters for long, and the paper arrived in the first year.5

Its second is the Bradford patent. In 1630, the Council for New England issued a further patent, granted to William Bradford and his associates, confirming the colony's lands and its authority to govern, and the document is catalogued as the Charter of the Colony of New Plymouth. The colony held a charter, and it held it in its own name, and the patent recited that Bradford and his associates had lived in New England for nine years and increased their plantation to near three hundred people.614

Its third is the chain of title. The Council for New England held a royal charter dated November 3, 1620, and the Council's patents derived their authority from the Crown, and the colony's government derived its authority from the Council's patents, and the authority flowed from the King through the Council to the colony without interruption. The charter was held by the Council, and the colony held the Council's grant, and that is how the system was designed to work.12

Its fourth is the acquiescence. The Crown knew where Plymouth was, and it knew how Plymouth governed, and it raised no objection for 71 years, and it left the colony to elect its governors and pass its laws in the open. A government that operates openly for 71 years, with the sovereign's knowledge, is not an unpermitted operation. It is a permitted one.10

Its fifth is the Compact itself. The document pledged loyalty to the King, and it was signed by the freemen, and it was renewed by annual elections, and the colony's government rested on the consent of the governed, and the consent was given again every year for seven decades. The Compact is celebrated as the first framework of government written in the territory that is now the United States, and a document with that pedigree is not a forgery. It is a founding text.25

Its sixth is the treaty. In the spring of 1621, the colony concluded a treaty of peace and mutual defense with Ousamequin, and the treaty held for more than fifty years, and the colony's relations with the people on whose land it stood were governed by agreement rather than by conquest. The colony's title rested on diplomacy, and diplomacy is a form of law.815

Its seventh is the charter of 1691. The Crown did issue a royal charter covering Plymouth, and the charter settled the question of the colony's legal status, and it did so by incorporating Plymouth into the Province of Massachusetts Bay, under a royal governor, with a royal charter, and the colony's 71 years without a charter ended with a charter. The file is closed.10

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VIII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

A royal charter should be issued to Plymouth Colony, retroactive to November 11, 1620, and the charter should be granted to the 41 signers of the Compact and their heirs and associates, and it should confirm the colony's lands, its government, its elections, its laws, its taxes, its courts, and its treaty, and it should be sealed under the Great Seal and enrolled in the records of the Crown, and the colony's 71 years of unchartered operation should be regularized, and the regularization should be backdated to the day the ship dropped anchor.210

The estate of Plymouth Colony should be billed for the 71 years it operated without a charter, at one year of unchartered operation per year of operation, and the bill should be presented to the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, as successor in interest, and the Commonwealth should pay it in the form of a charter application, filed in triplicate, with the fee, and the application should name the 102 passengers, the 41 signers, the 53 survivors, and the 90 guests, and it should attach the Compact, the Pierce patent, the Bradford patent, the treaty, and the single paragraph of Winslow's letter, and it should be filed before the next Thanksgiving.516

Congress should amend 5 U.S.C. 6103, the statute that fixes Thanksgiving Day on the fourth Thursday of November, to require that the holiday be observed only upon production of a royal charter for the colony whose feast it commemorates, and the charter should be produced to the Archivist of the United States, and the Archivist should certify it, and the certification should be published in the Federal Register, and no turkey should be pardoned, no parade should be permitted, and no table should be set until the certification is complete, since the holiday has now been observed for 163 years since Lincoln's proclamation without the underlying charter ever having been produced.1920

The queue should be cleared in order. The charter should be drafted, and the seal should be affixed, and the enrollment should be recorded, and the bill should be presented, and the application should be filed, and the statute should be amended, and the certification should be published, and the feast should be re-authorized, and the colony should be chartered at last, since the colony has now operated without a charter for 71 years, in the open, on the public record, one signer at a time.10

The test case is today. Thanksgiving Day falls on November 26, 2026, the fourth Thursday of the month, the 405th year since the autumn of the feast, the same calendar date Washington named in 1789, and the tables will be set, and the turkeys will be served, and the charter will not exist in the numbers the arithmetic demands, and the longest unchartered government in American history will be celebrated for the 405th time, in the open, over 143 places at the table, one signer at a time.1619

Ergo.
