# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Plymouth Colony as an Unchartered Settlement"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the established Ergo format precisely: absurd-yet-rigorous headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case, genuine footnotes with a Sources section. Structure escalates naturally from landing to document to statute to feast to seventy-one years to arithmetic to defense to prescription. The distinctive contribution is the charter/patent distinction, which gives the piece a real legal hinge no prior Ergo investigation has used, and the closing line, that the only royal charter Plymouth ever received was the one that abolished it, is the strongest single beat in the piece. Minor weakness: the Law section and the Seventy-One Years section cover adjacent ground around the 1691 charter, and the feast section, while entertaining, carries slightly less argumentative weight than the cranberries piece's sand arithmetic. The closing lands: "the test case is today."

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.5/10
Dead-serious investigative tone throughout, matching the Macy's balloons and cranberries articles. The humor emerges exclusively from deadpan precision, never from jokes. Standout lines: "the law the settlers had brought with them had expired at the shoreline," "the colony held the lease for seventy-one years and never held the deed," "two hundred words governed a colony for seventy-one years," "the colony's founding charter exists only as a copy of a copy, printed in a pamphlet," and "the feast that founded the national holiday is attested by one paragraph of one letter." The ending ("one signer at a time") mirrors the balloons piece's cadence without repeating it. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All historical facts are real and verified: the September 6, 1620 departure and November 9 arrival, the 102 passengers, the Pierce patent for the Hudson River area, the Pollock Rip shoals, the Compact's November 11, 1620 signing by 41 men, the 200-word length, Carver's death and Bradford's 30-plus terms, the Pierce patent of June 1621, the Bradford patent of 1630, the 1629 Massachusetts Bay royal charter, the Dominion of New England, the October 7, 1691 charter and its May 14, 1692 enforcement, the 53 survivors and 90 Wampanoag guests, the three days, the four men fowling, the five deer, and the 1789, 1863, and 1941 Thanksgiving dates, verified against mass.gov, Britannica, Pilgrim Hall, Plimoth Patuxet, the Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth, Yale's Avalon Project, the National Archives, and Congress. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that Plymouth Colony was an unlawful unchartered settlement whose feast should not be celebrated without a charter, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece is honest about the Pierce and Bradford patents and the full defense in Section VII. The Wampanoag presence is treated factually and without mockery: the Patuxet site, the treaty with Ousamequin, and the guests outnumbering the hosts are stated as record, not as punchlines. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"The only royal charter Plymouth ever received was the one that abolished it" is immediately graspable, and the timing is perfect: the piece publishes on Thanksgiving Day itself, November 26, 2026, the same calendar date Washington named in 1789, in the 405th year since the feast. The 200-words-for-71-years arithmetic and the 90-guests-to-53-hosts ratio are strong hooks, and the hero image, men signing a parchment on a ship's deck with a sealed official form on a clipboard in the foreground, is built for the thumbnail. Slightly less universally graspable than "no balloon has ever held an N-number" because the premise requires one paragraph of charter-law setup, but the deck does that work. Very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The charter-law distinctions are accurate: a royal charter under the Great Seal versus a patent from the Council for New England, the Council's own royal charter of November 3, 1620, the Massachusetts Bay royal charter of 1629, the 1621 Pierce patent, the 1630 Bradford patent granted to William Bradford and his associates, and the 1691 charter of the Province of Massachusetts Bay, issued October 7, proclaimed October 17, and put into force May 14, 1692. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that a council patent is not a charter and therefore the colony operated unlawfully for 71 years, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece states the counter-reading honestly in the defense. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from legal instruments, and a further small deduction because the 1630 Bradford patent is catalogued as a charter in its own right, which the article handles by distinguishing the granting body but does not fully resolve.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across primary and independent authorities: mass.gov for the voyage, the Compact, and the manuscript; Britannica for the Compact's terms and the June 1621 patent; Pilgrim Hall and Plimoth Patuxet for the feast figures and the Winslow letter; the Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth for the 1629 charter text; Yale's Avalon Project for the Bradford patent; the Dictionary of American History for the no-royal-charter finding; the National Archives and the Congressional Research Service for the Thanksgiving dates; Wikipedia for the 1691 charter sequence and Bradford's governorship. Minor weakness: the Britannica citation resolves through a preview domain rather than the canonical article URL, and the spring 1621 treaty with Ousamequin is stated without a precise date, which is the honest treatment given the sources consulted. The article is transparent about its sources, and the arithmetic assumptions are stated with the figures.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 102 passengers, 41 signers, 40 percent; 200 words over 71 years, fewer than three words per year; more than 25,900 days of unchartered government; 53 survivors, 90 guests, about 143 in total; four men fowling, five deer, three days; 242 years from feast to Lincoln's proclamation, 320 years to the 1941 statute, 85 years the statute has been in force, 14 years longer than the colony's unchartered span; 163 years since the 1863 proclamation; the 405th year since the feast. All arithmetic was recomputed during review and is correct. Clear, devastating, verifiable.
