Clean Water Law & Meteorology

Nearly 5,000 Billion Gallons of Precipitation Fall on the Lower 48 States Every Day, One Inch of Rain on One Acre Is 27,154 Gallons, the Clean Water Act Requires a Permit for Every Point Source That Discharges Pollutants Into Waters of the United States, the Nation's 855 Large and 6,695 Small Municipal Storm Sewer Systems Hold Permits for the Runoff They Collect, No Rainstorm Has Ever Applied for One, and the Prescription Is an NPDES Permit for Every Storm

Rain is the largest unpermitted discharge in the United States, and it is also the only one that has never applied for permission. Every day, nearly five thousand billion gallons of water fall on the lower forty-eight states, run across roofs and roads and parking lots, pick up oil and grease and sediment and bacteria, and flow into rivers and lakes and bays, without a permit, without an effluent limit, and without ever filing a discharge monitoring report. The federal government has spent fifty years building a permitting system for exactly this activity, and it has permitted the pipes but never the rain. This investigation examines the largest unpermitted discharge in the United States, the volume it moves, the pollutants it carries, the law it evades, and the permit the record demands.

By Silas Pemberton · November 8, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Operation

The Clean Water Act prohibits the discharge of any pollutant from a point source into waters of the United States except in compliance with a permit. Section 402 of the Act establishes the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System, known as NPDES, and requires a permit for any point source discharge of pollutants to United States waters. A point source is any discernible, confined, and discrete conveyance, including a pipe, ditch, channel, tunnel, conduit, well, discrete fissure, container, rolling stock, concentrated animal feeding operation, or vessel or other floating craft, from which pollutants are or may be discharged.1

Stormwater was brought inside that system by the Water Quality Act of 1987, which added Section 402(p) and directed the Environmental Protection Agency to regulate stormwater discharges. The Phase I regulation, issued in 1990, requires medium and large cities and certain counties with populations of 100,000 or more to obtain NPDES permit coverage for their stormwater discharges. There are approximately 855 Phase I municipal separate storm sewer systems, known as MS4s, covered by 250 individual permits. The Phase II regulation, issued in 1999, requires small MS4s in Census-defined urbanized areas to obtain coverage as well, along with non-traditional MS4s such as public universities, departments of transportation, hospitals, and prisons. Most of the 6,695 Phase II MS4s are covered by statewide general permits. Permittees must develop a stormwater management program describing the practices they will implement to minimize the discharge of pollutants from the sewer system.2

The system does not stop at cities. Stormwater discharges associated with industrial activity require permit coverage, as do construction sites disturbing one acre or more of land. The Congressional Research Service estimates that the total number of stormwater permittees at any one time exceeds half a million, which means NPDES stormwater permittees outnumber wastewater permittees more than five-fold. Before the stormwater program, the universe of NPDES permittees nationwide was less than 70,000 industrial and municipal facilities. The addition of stormwater permittees greatly expanded the program.3

Consider what rain is, in regulatory terms. It is water that falls on the land, runs across surfaces, collects pollutants, and discharges into waters of the United States. The permitted system issues a permit to the pipe that carries the rain to the river. The unpermitted system is the rain itself, which arrives without a permit, without a stormwater management program, and without a single one of the six minimum control measures the Phase II rule requires of every small MS4. The difference between the two discharges is not the water. It is the paperwork.

II. The Scale of Production

The throughput of the unpermitted discharger is established by the federal government itself. In the lower 48 states, nearly 5,000 billion gallons of water fall in the form of precipitation each day. Average annual rainfall across the conterminous United States was 857 millimeters per year for water years 2010 through 2020, with the wettest year reaching 995 millimeters. About 63 percent of that precipitation returns to the atmosphere through evapotranspiration, and about 1,200 billion gallons per day, or 8.3 inches of the annual average of 30 inches, becomes streamflow.47

The unit rate is exact, and the United States Geological Survey states it plainly. One inch of rain falling on one acre of ground is equal to about 27,154 gallons of water, and it weighs about 113 tons. One inch of rain on a one-half acre lot delivers 13,577 gallons to the yard. Atlanta, Georgia, has corporate boundaries covering about 84,100 acres, so a one-inch rainstorm deposits 2.28 billion gallons of water on the city.5 A single inch of rain over the lower 48 states, about 3.1 million square miles, is roughly 54 trillion gallons, and at the Survey's measured average of 857 millimeters a year that works out to very nearly the 5,000 billion gallons a day the Survey reports.6

One inch of rain on one acre is 27,154 gallons, and it weighs 113 tons.

The permitted system, for comparison, regulates the pipe. A Phase I MS4 serving a city of 100,000 people holds an individual permit, files reports, and implements a stormwater management program. The unpermitted system delivers 2.28 billion gallons to a single city in a single inch of rain, and it has never filed a notice of intent, never submitted a stormwater pollution prevention plan, and never reported a single discharge monitoring result. The permitted discharger measures its outfall in gallons per day. The unpermitted discharger measures its outfall in trillions per inch.

III. The Process

The rain does not arrive clean, and the government says so in its own definition of the problem. Nonpoint source pollution is caused by rainfall or snowmelt moving over and through the ground. As the runoff moves, it picks up and carries away natural and human-made pollutants, finally depositing them into lakes, rivers, wetlands, coastal waters, and ground waters. The pollutants include excess fertilizers, herbicides, and insecticides from agricultural lands and residential areas; oil, grease, and toxic chemicals from urban runoff and energy production; sediment from improperly managed construction sites, crop and forest lands, and eroding streambanks; salt from irrigation practices and acid drainage from abandoned mines; and bacteria and nutrients from livestock, pet wastes, and faulty septic systems. States report that nonpoint source pollution is the leading remaining cause of water quality problems.8

The surface the rain lands on decides how much of it becomes discharge. Traditional urban areas include large areas of impervious surfaces such as roads, sidewalks, and buildings, which prevent rainwater from infiltrating into the ground, so stormwater runs off at higher rates and volumes, causing increased flooding and erosion and more pollution to surface waters. A typical city block generates five times more runoff than a woodland area of the same size.917

The discharge is front-loaded. The first flush concept holds that the first part of a rainfall event contains the largest pollutant loading, and sampling of a paved parking area established the existence of a first flush for total suspended solids, nitrate, chloride, dissolved copper, and dissolved cadmium, up to a rainfall depth of 25.4 millimeters. In a study of roadway runoff, the first 50 percent of the runoff volume transported 62 percent of the total organic carbon, 60 percent of the suspended solids, and between 54 and 59 percent of the metals measured.10 The dirtiest water is the first water, and it is the first water to reach the river.

The rain is also acidic before it touches the ground. Pure rainfall is slightly acidic, with a pH of 5.6, because water reacts with atmospheric carbon dioxide to produce weak carbonic acid. When higher than normal amounts of nitric and sulfuric acid occur in the atmosphere, the result is precipitation with a pH below 5.6, which is referred to as acid rain, typically between 4.2 and 4.4. The sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides that produce it come principally from the burning of fossil fuels, and the Environmental Protection Agency attributes two-thirds of sulfur dioxide emissions and one-quarter of nitrogen oxide emissions to fossil fuel electricity generation.11 The permitted power plant holds an allowance to emit the sulfur dioxide. The rain that carries the acid to the ground holds nothing.

IV. The Discharge

The discharge is documented in the sewer records of the oldest cities in the country. In a combined sewer system, wastewater and stormwater flow through the same pipes, and during wet weather the combined flow can overwhelm the system. Permitted outfalls located throughout the system act as relief points and discharge untreated or partially treated stormwater and wastewater into nearby waterbodies. These discharges are called combined sewer overflows. They are a major water pollution and public health concern for approximately 700 communities in the United States, and the Clean Water Act requires those communities to put controls in place.12

The volume is established by the government's own last estimate. Combined sewer systems serve about 40 million people in roughly 772 communities nationwide, most of them in the Northeast and Great Lakes regions and the Pacific Northwest. The Environmental Protection Agency estimated in 2004 that combined sewer overflows discharge 850 billion gallons of untreated wastewater and stormwater each year, through 9,348 outfalls regulated by 828 NPDES permits. The Government Accountability Office reported in January 2023 that the 2004 figure remains the agency's last estimate, that more recent nationwide volume data is unavailable, and that control efforts since then have likely reduced the volume. It takes as little as one-tenth of an inch of rain to overwhelm these systems and cause an overflow.131415

It takes one-tenth of an inch of rain to overwhelm the system.

New York City is the largest single exhibit. Approximately 60 percent of the city has a combined sewer system, with 398 combined sewer overflow outfalls that annually discharge approximately 18 billion gallons of combined sewage, making it one of the largest combined sewer systems in the United States. The city has spent decades and committed around 10 billion dollars to grey and green infrastructure to reduce the overflows. In the lower Bronx River, where an estimated 551 million gallons of combined sewage and polluted stormwater discharge in an average rainfall year, the city completed a parallel relief sewer in the summer of 2026 that will eliminate an estimated 237 million gallons a year.1620

The separate system overflows as well. The Environmental Protection Agency estimates there are over 40,000, and perhaps as many as 75,000, sanitary sewer overflows in the United States each year, releasing anywhere from 3 billion to 10 billion gallons of untreated wastewater. Every one of them is caused by the same event: rain, arriving in a volume the system was not built to carry.18

V. The Standard

The standard the rain evades is written down, and the lawful version of the same water operates under it every day. The lawful version is the permitted stormwater discharge. A Phase II MS4 must implement six minimum control measures: public education and outreach, public involvement, illicit discharge detection and elimination, construction site runoff control, post-construction runoff control, and pollution prevention and good housekeeping for municipal operations. A construction site disturbing one acre or more must operate under a stormwater pollution prevention plan. An industrial facility must sample its outfalls, monitor its benchmarks, and file its reports. The federal government itself is held to a standard: under Section 438 of the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007, federal agencies must reduce stormwater runoff from federal development and redevelopment projects to protect water resources, because stormwater runoff in urban areas is one of the leading sources of water pollution in the United States.217

The acid in the rain is regulated at its source and unregulated at its destination. Title IV of the Clean Air Act established an allowance trading program for sulfur dioxide, and every allowance authorizes its holder to emit one ton. The power plant holds allowances. The rain that dissolves the sulfur dioxide, carries it hundreds of miles, and deposits it on a lake at a pH of 4.2 holds no allowance, files no report, and has never been asked to. The permitted system caps the emission at the stack. The unpermitted system delivers the acid to the water, one inch at a time, without a cap of any kind.

Apply the definition to the unit. A discharge is the addition of any pollutant to navigable waters from any point source. Rain adds pollutants to navigable waters. It adds oil and grease and sediment and bacteria and nutrients and acid, in volumes measured in trillions of gallons, at a rate of 5,000 billion gallons a day, through a conveyance that is, by any ordinary reading, discernible, confined, and discrete: the sky, which is the largest conveyance in the country, and the only one that has never held a permit.

VI. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the statute. The Clean Water Act prohibits the discharge of pollutants by any person, and a person is an individual, a corporation, a partnership, an association, a State, a municipality, a commission, or a political subdivision. Rain is none of these. The prohibition runs against persons, and the sky is not one.

Its second is the definition. A point source is a discernible, confined, and discrete conveyance, and the Act expressly excludes agricultural stormwater discharges and return flows from irrigated agriculture from that definition. Nonpoint source pollution is defined, in the government's own words, as any source of water pollution that does not meet the legal definition of a point source. The rain that runs off a field is not a point source by definition. The rain that runs off a roof is not a point source by anything except the imagination of this publication.198

Its third is the operator. The permitting system was built for operators, and it regulates the collection system, not the weather. The MS4 holds the permit because the MS4 owns the pipes, operates the outfalls, and can be inspected, fined, and ordered to comply. No inspector has ever inspected a cloud. No enforcement action has ever been taken against a storm. The system has 855 Phase I permits and 6,695 Phase II permits and more than half a million stormwater permittees, and every one of them is a person, a city, a company, or a construction site. None of them is weather.

Its fourth is the form. No NPDES permit application in the history of the program has had a line for a discharge of 5,000 billion gallons a day, no stormwater management program has a control measure for the sky, and no discharge monitoring report has a column for a pH measured in the clouds. Its fifth is the remedy already in place. The permitted version exists and it works: the MS4 program, the construction general permit, the industrial stormwater permit, the combined sewer overflow control policy, and the ten billion dollars New York City has committed to keeping the rain out of its harbor. The rain is not unregulated. The rain is collected, and the collection is regulated.

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every rainstorm in the United States should hold an NPDES permit, issued to the storm as operator and the watershed as the facility, with effluent limits, monitoring requirements, and discharge monitoring reports filed for every outfall, which is to say for every square mile of the lower forty-eight states. The storm should implement the six minimum control measures, capture its first flush, and hold its discharge to the water quality standards of the receiving water, at a pH no lower than 5.6. The 855 Phase I and 6,695 Phase II municipal systems should be understood for what they are: the permitting authorities, already staffed, already funded, already issuing hundreds of thousands of permits, waiting for the largest applicant in the history of the program to walk in the door.2

The test case is the next inch of rain. One inch on one acre is 27,154 gallons and 113 tons. One inch on Atlanta is 2.28 billion gallons. One inch on the lower forty-eight states is roughly 54 trillion gallons, carrying oil and grease and sediment and bacteria and acid, at a pH of 5.6 before it touches the ground and lower after, into waters the law protects, without a permit, without a limit, and without a report. Until compliance is achieved, the rain will continue to fall every day, unpermitted, as it has since the first cloud, filling the pipes it was never issued a permit to fill, overwhelming the systems it was never ordered to respect, and waiting for a form that does not exist.
