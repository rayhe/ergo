Every Year American Honeybees Manufacture 134 Million Pounds of Honey From Flower Nectar by Extracting It With Their Proboscises, Treating It With the Enzyme Invertase Secreted From Glands in Their Heads, Evaporating Its Water Content From 70 Percent to Under 19 Percent by Fanning Their Wings, and Waxing Each Finished Cell Shut, the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act Requires Every Facility That Manufactures, Processes, Packs, or Holds Food for Consumption in the United States to Register With the Food and Drug Administration, the Agency's Own Definition of Manufacturing Lists Evaporating, Extracting, Treating, Waxing, and Packaging as Examples, the 2.6 Million Colonies Operating in the United States Hold Zero Registrations Between Them, the 2026 Biennial Renewal Window Opened on October 1 and Closes on December 31, and the Prescription Is Form FDA 3537

The honeybee is a food manufacturer operating at industrial scale. A single colony fields 30,000 to 60,000 workers, flies 55,000 miles to produce a pound of honey, and chemically transforms a raw agricultural commodity into a distinct finished product through enzymatic treatment, controlled evaporation, and wax packaging. In 2024 the industry produced 134 million pounds worth about $360 million. The Food and Drug Administration, which requires every domestic and foreign facility that manufactures, processes, packs, or holds food for consumption in the United States to register, renew biennially, and submit to inspection, has registered none of them. The current renewal window is open. This investigation examines the largest unregistered food manufacturing sector in the United States, its uninspected facilities, its missing sanitation records, and the filing the record demands.

By Ephraim Vane · November 2, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Factory

The manufacturing process begins at the flower. A forager bee extends her proboscis, draws nectar up, and stores it in her crop, an organ also called the honey stomach, which is used solely for storage: the bee does not digest the nectar at all. A stomach holds about 70 milligrams of nectar, and filling it requires visiting up to 1,500 flowers.12

The forager returns to the hive and regurgitates the nectar directly into the crop of a processor bee at the entrance. The processor bee carries it to the honeycomb, regurgitates it into a hexagonal wax cell, and the ripening begins. With every regurgitation, the bees add invertase, an enzyme secreted from glands in the worker's head. Invertase breaks the nectar's sucrose down into glucose and fructose, the two simple sugars that give finished honey its sweetness, its resistance to fermentation, and its tendency to crystallize. The enzymatic step takes about 30 minutes and continues at a measurable rate for as long as the nectar sits uncapped.12

Then comes the drying. Fresh nectar is about 70 percent water; by definition, finished honey contains less than 18.6 percent. The processor bees deposit the treated nectar into cells at roughly 20 percent moisture, then fan their wings at about 11,000 beats per minute above the open cells, generating airflow across the comb that evaporates the water out over one to three days. When the moisture drops to roughly 18 percent, the bees seal the cell with an airtight wax cap, secreted from glands on the worker's abdomen. If the cap is not airtight, the honey absorbs moisture from the air and becomes susceptible to bacterial or fungal growth.12

Consider what has occurred. A raw agricultural commodity has been extracted, enzymatically treated, evaporatively dehydrated, and wax-packaged into a distinct finished product with a shelf life measured in millennia. In the Code of Federal Regulations, the Food and Drug Administration defines manufacturing and processing as making food from one or more ingredients, or synthesizing, preparing, treating, modifying, or manipulating food, including food crops or ingredients. Its list of examples includes evaporating, extracting juice, treating to manipulate ripening, waxing, and packaging.3 The regulation reads like a description of the hive.

II. The Scale of Production

In 2024, United States honey production totaled 134 million pounds, produced by 2.60 million colonies at an average yield of 51.7 pounds per colony. The average price was $2.69 per pound, which puts the value of the crop at about $360 million: 134 million pounds multiplied by $2.69. Producer stocks stood at 43.4 million pounds on December 15, 2024.4

The labor behind the tonnage is staggering. A bee visits 50 to 100 flowers during a single collection trip. To make one pound of honey, the workers of a hive collectively visit about two million flowers and fly about 55,000 miles, more than twice around the Earth. It takes about 556 worker bees to gather a pound. The average worker, laboring five or six weeks in the active season, produces about one-twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her entire lifetime. A colony numbers 30,000 to 60,000 bees in season, and a strong hive can put up 60 to 100 pounds of surplus honey a year above the roughly 300 pounds the colony needs for itself.567

The workforce is replenished by a single executive. Each hive has one queen, the largest bee in the colony, whose sole function is reproduction: she lays up to 2,000 eggs per day, more than her own body weight, around the clock through the laying season. The workers feed her, house her, and, if her performance disappoints, build an elongated cell and raise her replacement.8

By every operational definition, the colony is a food manufacturing facility. It has a raw material intake system, a multi-stage chemical process, a drying operation, a packaging line, a managed workforce in the tens of thousands, and an executive suite of one. It lacks only the paperwork.

The regulation's list of manufacturing activities reads like a description of the hive: extracting, treating, evaporating, waxing, packaging. The drafters described the bees, then exempted everyone but them.

III. The Registration Record

Section 415 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act requires the registration of food facilities. The implementing regulation, 21 CFR 1.225, provides that the owner, operator, or agent in charge of a domestic or foreign facility must register if the facility is engaged in the manufacturing, processing, packing, or holding of food for consumption in the United States, whether or not the food enters interstate commerce. The regulation defines a facility as any establishment, structure, or structures under one ownership at one general physical location that manufactures, processes, packs, or holds food for consumption in the United States.910

The obligations are not nominal. Registrations must be renewed biennially, between October 1 and December 31 of each even-numbered year, and submitted electronically unless the agency has granted a waiver. Failure to register, to renew, or to update is a prohibited act under section 301(dd) of the Act. An expired registration is treated as a failure to register. Updates reflecting a change of operator or agent in charge are due within 60 calendar days. Registration is effected on Form FDA 3537, which must be filled out, signed, and submitted by the owner, operator, or agent in charge, with a certification statement attesting that the information is true and accurate.91112

The 2026 biennial renewal period opened on October 1, 2026, and closes on December 31, 2026. It is open now. The agency reminded the industry of the deadline this quarter.13

Against this record, the colony's compliance file is empty. No hive has ever been registered as a domestic facility. No queen, beekeeper, or worker has ever submitted Form FDA 3537. No biennial renewal has ever been filed, in 2026 or in any even-numbered year since registration began in 2003. The 2.6 million colonies operating in the United States hold zero registrations between them, and the current renewal window, now more than a month old, has produced zero filings from the sector.

The regulation anticipates the objection that no one is in charge. It defines the registrant, for purposes of the subpart, as the owner, operator, or agent in charge of the facility. The beekeeper owns the boxes but does not manufacture the honey; the manufacturing is performed by the bees, under no human direction whatever, by a process no beekeeper controls, adjusts, or even observes at the enzymatic level. The owner of the facility is not its operator. The operator of the facility is a superorganism.10

IV. The Labeling File

The agency's posture toward honey has not been one of benign neglect. It has been one of energetic confusion, directed at everyone in the supply chain except the manufacturer.

In February 2018, the Food and Drug Administration issued draft guidance proposing that jars of pure, single-ingredient honey carry an obelisk symbol, the character †, immediately after the added sugars daily value on the Nutrition Facts label, directing consumers to contextual language explaining that all the sugars are naturally occurring in honey. The agency had classified honey, even when packaged as such and with nothing added, as an added sugar. The docket received more than 3,000 comments, almost universally negative, from producers who pointed out that there are no sugars added to honey because honey is sugar. In September 2018, Commissioner Scott Gottlieb announced a revised approach, conceding that the standard added sugars declaration would mislead consumers into thinking table sugar or corn syrup had been added to a product to which nothing had been added.14

The agency has policed honey imports with considerably more success. In February 2013, the Department of Justice announced charges against the two largest domestic honey processors, Honey Holding and Groeb Farms, following an interagency honey laundering sting: Chinese honey had been transshipped through third countries and misdeclared to evade more than $180 million in antidumping duties, and some shipments were adulterated with chloramphenicol, an antibiotic the FDA does not approve for use in honey. Both companies entered deferred prosecution agreements, paying $1 million and $2 million respectively. The case was the largest food fraud prosecution in American history, breaking the previous $80 million record set by the same enforcement team. The FDA had issued an import alert for honey containing chloramphenicol back in October 2002.1516

The pattern is unmistakable. The agency will litigate the punctuation on a honey jar, prosecute the importer of adulterated honey, and alert the ports against antibiotic-laced shipments. The one party it has never asked to identify itself is the party that made the honey.

V. The Sanitation File

Under 21 USC 342(a)(4), a food is deemed adulterated if it has been prepared, packed, or held under insanitary conditions whereby it may have become contaminated with filth, or whereby it may have been rendered injurious to health. The government need not find contamination in the finished product; the conditions suffice.17

The conditions in the sector are the worst on record. Between April 2024 and April 2025, American beekeepers lost an estimated 55.6 percent of their managed colonies, the highest annual loss since national tracking began in 2010. Winter losses alone reached 40.2 percent. The survey, conducted by Auburn University with the Apiary Inspectors of America and Oregon State University, drew responses from 2,453 beekeepers managing 219,097 colonies, about 8.4 percent of the country's honey-producing colonies. The previous year had set the prior record at 55.1 percent.18

The leading suspect is a parasite. USDA scientists traced the early 2025 collapses to viruses spread by Varroa destructor, a mite that feeds on the bees' fat bodies and vectors deadly pathogens, and found the mites resistant to amitraz, the principal miticide, in nearly every sample tested. A separate survey of commercial operations found 62 percent of hives dead since June 2024, with the economic damage above $600 million. Varroa remains the top reported stressor in every quarter the Department measures.1920

A registered facility that lost 55.6 percent of its workforce in a single year to an uncontrolled parasite infestation would be operating under a consent decree. The unregistered sector has received no inspection, no warning letter, and no Form FDA 483. This is not for lack of things to observe: the colonies maintain their brood nests at 91 to 97 degrees Fahrenheit with a precision no thermostat in a registered plant could match, and they do it with no written sanitation procedures, no pest control records, and no corrective action documentation of any kind.21

VI. The Import Double Standard

The United States is the world's largest honey importer, accounting for about a third of global import volume. In 2024, imports reached 255,000 tons, up 28 percent, drawn principally from India, Argentina, and Vietnam, while domestic production declined. Every foreign facility that manufactured, processed, packed, or held that honey for consumption in the United States was required to register with the FDA and designate a United States agent, a person residing or maintaining a place of business in this country, reachable for routine and emergency communications.2210

The domestic manufacturer faces no such requirement in practice. The foreign hive's honey arrives with a registration number, a United States agent, and a paper trail. The domestic hive's honey arrives with nothing, though the bees that made it flew 55,000 miles per pound and the beekeeper merely lifted the frames. The agency has built a system in which honey manufactured abroad by registered foreign facilities is more completely documented than honey manufactured at home by nobody.

VII. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the farm. The regulation defines a farm as an operation under one management in one general physical location devoted to the growing of crops, the harvesting of crops, the raising of animals, including seafood, or any combination thereof, and the agency exempts farms from registration. The beekeeper who harvests and packs honey is, by this definition, a farm, and the Department of Agriculture's own statisticians agree: the national honey survey draws its sample from operations with at least five colonies that meet USDA's definition of a farm.104

Its second is the queen. The regulation requires registration by the owner, operator, or agent in charge, and the defense nominates the queen: she is the only permanent executive, she directs the workforce through pheromone, and she never takes a day off. Its third is the form. Form FDA 3537 must be submitted electronically through the agency's portal, and the colony has no email address, no Unique Facility Identifier, and no authorized individual to sign the Section 13 certification statement. Its fourth is the calendar. The renewal window closes December 31, 2026. This investigation publishes November 2. The bees have 59 days.

Its fifth is the oldest defense in the book: nobody told us. The agency has never issued guidance to hives, never held a stakeholder meeting with a colony, and never published a compliance guide in waggle dance.

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VIII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every honey-producing colony in the United States should be registered as a domestic food facility before December 31, 2026, on Form FDA 3537, with the beekeeper named as owner and the queen designated agent in charge. The beekeeper is disqualified from the operator field: he does not manufacture the product. The manufacturing is performed by the colony, and the registration should say so.

Each facility should come into compliance with current good manufacturing practice, including written sanitation procedures, pest control records documenting Varroa treatments, and corrective action documentation for colony losses. Updates reflecting a change of agent in charge, including supersedure and swarming events, should be filed within 60 calendar days as the regulation requires.12

The agency should exercise the inspection authority Congress granted it in section 704 of the Act. Upon completing an inspection, the investigator must give the owner, operator, or agent in charge a written report of the conditions observed, on Form FDA 483, before leaving the premises. The premises, in this case, is a wooden box containing 60,000 venomous insects. This explains the inspection gap. It does not excuse it.23

Until compliance is achieved, the factories will continue to operate at full capacity, unregistered, as they have for millennia.
