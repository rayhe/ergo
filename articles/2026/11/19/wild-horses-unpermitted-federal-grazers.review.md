# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Wild Horses as Unpermitted Federal Grazers"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The piece follows the Ergo template faithfully and holds together as a systematic case: an absurdly exhaustive headline and a deck that restates the whole indictment, eight Roman-numeral sections escalating cleanly from the animal itself, through the range, the history, the grazing law, the forage, the scale arithmetic, a genuinely conceded defense, and an impossible prescription ending in "Ergo." The escalation works because each section adds a new load-bearing fact, the mestengo etymology, the Lahontan 518-against-7-to-10 count, the 43 CFR 4130.8-1 definition that prices a horse by name, the $23.40 private lease rate, the 405,000-ton forage count, rather than merely restating the joke. The six pullquotes are well placed and mostly sharp, and the footnotes and Sources section are complete and properly linked. The main drag is the headline acreage: the 26.9-million-acre figure rests on the Bureau's 2018 herd-area statistics, which is honestly dated in the footnote but still carries the headline, and the headline, while in-format, buries its punchline, "No horse has ever filed a grazing application," at the very end of a very long sentence.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0/10
The voice holds the Ergo register almost throughout: flat, procedural, and relentlessly literal, with the humor carried entirely by the precision of the measurements and the legal citations rather than by any aside or wink. The cadence is the article's strength, with short declarative sentences and parallel constructions, "The count is 85,466. The level is 25,592. The difference is 59,874 animals," doing the satirical work that adjectives never do. The few slips are small but audible: "The horse has never been billed" appears in two sections and lands softer the second time, and the closing test-case paragraph restates the headline arithmetic in a long polysyndetic chain instead of landing a fresh image the way the turkeys piece does with its unfiled petition. One terminology slip was caught and fixed before publication: the defense originally called the wild horse program the largest animal management program in the federal government, an unsourced superlative, which was cut to the sourced $142 million figure. Still, the final "Ergo." lands because the preceding prescription has earned its flatness, and the piece never once steps out of character to admire its own joke.

## 3. Ethics — 8.5/10
Spot-checked claims against their sources: the population counts (85,466 on range, 61,523 horses, 23,943 burros, high AML 25,592) match the Bureau's own program data; the 2026 fee ($1.69 per animal unit month, effective March 1, 2026) matches the Bureau's press release; the AUM definition, including one horse, matches 43 CFR 4130.8-1 verbatim; the unauthorized grazing settlement tiers (private rate, twice for willful, three times for repeated willful) match 43 CFR 4150.3; the 1971 Act's unanimous passage and "living symbols" language match the cited histories; and the scale arithmetic is internally consistent. The defense section is the piece's ethical strong point: it fairly presents the dispositive escape hatches, above all that Congress created a separate regime for wild horses in the 1971 Act, that a grazing permit requires a person who owns livestock and base property, that the appropriate management level is a target rather than a permit cap, and that the Bureau already removes animals and spends $142 million a year doing it, before the disclosed "overruled" joke. Three real factual problems were found and fixed before publication: an unsourced superlative about the program's size was cut, a claim that mustangs share the range with specific wildlife species was narrowed to the sourced general claim about permitted livestock and wildlife habitat, and the prescription's impoundment citation was completed with the 43 CFR 4150.4 authority. Minor nits: the 26.9-million-acre headline figure rests on 2018 Bureau statistics, though herd-area boundaries are set by Congress and do not change; the $23.40 private lease rate is an average taken from a secondary legal guide rather than the National Agricultural Statistics Service tables; and the letters-to-Congress claim rests on an advocacy memorial page rather than a congressional record. Nothing is invented or misattributed in substance, the deliberately wrong conclusion is honestly fenced by a complete defense, and the piece serves the educational mission well.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.5/10
The premise is instantly graspable in one sentence, wild horses graze federal land without permits while ranchers pay for the same grass, and it lands harder than the pollen or rain premises because nearly everyone has seen the subject and the price tag is a number anyone can repeat. The piece is rich in quotable, tweet-length lines and surprising-but-true facts that travel well, from the fee schedule pricing a horse by name, to the Lahontan herd at 518 animals against a level of 7 to 10, to the government spending about eighty-two dollars managing the herd for every dollar of grazing fees the herd does not pay. Concrete anchors help too: the Nevada count at more than three times its level, the $1.69 fee against the $23.40 private rate, and the 405,000 tons of forage billed to no one. The headline, however, fails as a social hook: at roughly 60 words it cannot be quoted, posted, or remembered, so all sharing work falls to the deck and pullquotes instead. Still very high share potential, docked mainly for a headline that buries its own punchline.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
Every citation checked against the primary sources holds up: the Taylor Grazing Act of 1934 and the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 are correctly described as the permit system's foundation; the Public Rangelands Improvement Act formula, the 1966 base value of $1.23, and the $1.35 floor are correctly stated; the 2026 fee of $1.69 per animal unit month, effective March 1, 2026, matches the Bureau's announcement; 43 CFR 4130.8-1 is quoted essentially verbatim, including the definition of one animal unit month as one horse; 43 CFR 4150.3 is stated correctly, including the private-rate measure, the doubling for willful violations, the tripling for repeated willful violations, and the bar on further grazing until payment; and the 1971 Act (16 U.S.C. 1331) is accurately described, including that it authorizes removal of excess animals. The article is honest about the decisive escape hatches: Section VII correctly states that the 1971 Act is a separate congressional regime, that permit qualifications require a person owning livestock and base property, that the animal unit month lists the horse for domestic animals under permit, and that the management level triggers removal rather than billing. Two legal nits were found and fixed before publication: the prescription's impoundment authority was cited only to 4150.3 until the 4150.4 impoundment-and-disposal provision was added, and the shared-range sentence was narrowed to what the cited sources support. None of these changes the verdict, because the deliberately wrong conclusion, that every wild horse requires a grazing permit, is the genre's disclosed conceit, stated only after the article itself has laid out the law that defeats it, and the "defense rests, it is overruled" turn is the joke rather than a misstatement of law.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10
All five spot-checked claims check out in substance: the March 1, 2026 population estimate and the high AML match the Bureau's program data page; the 2026 fee matches the Bureau's press release; the AUM definition matches the Code of Federal Regulations text; the 1971 population of about 17,300 matches the American Museum of Natural History figure carried by Live Science; and the $142.0 million fiscal year 2025 appropriation and the $101.4 million off-range holding figure match the Congressional Research Service report. The sourcing behind those true claims is nevertheless weak for a load-bearing investigation in places, with the permit mechanics, the base-property requirement, and the season-of-use contrast routed through secondary state grazing guides rather than the regulation text itself, the 790-pound AUM forage figure routed through a Montana guide attributing Montana State University Extension, and the $23.40 private lease rate routed through a legal marketing page rather than the National Agricultural Statistics Service tables. The 26.9-million-acre figure rests on 2018 Bureau statistics, honestly dated in the footnote, and the two forage totals in the scale section use different bases, the AUM conversion for all animals and the daily rate for horses only, which is labeled but invites confusion. One detail went beyond its cited source and was fixed before publication: the shared-range sentence named wildlife species the cited source did not support. No load-bearing claim proved false.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
All of the listed arithmetic recomputes correctly within rounding: 85,466 animals times 12 months is 1,025,592 animal unit months a year; at $1.69 that is $1,733,250, fairly stated as about $1.7 million; at $23.40 that is $23,998,853, fairly stated as about $24.0 million; doubled for willful violations that is $47,997,706, fairly stated as about $48.0 million; tripled for repeated willful violations that is $71,996,558, fairly stated as about $72.0 million; 85,466 animals times 790 pounds a month is 67,518,140 pounds, fairly stated as about 33,800 tons a month and about 405,000 tons a year; 61,523 horses times 20 pounds a day is 1,230,460 pounds, fairly stated as about 615 tons a day and about 225,000 tons a year; 61,523 horses times 10 gallons a day is 615,230 gallons, fairly stated as about 225 million gallons a year; 85,466 minus 25,592 is 59,874 excess animals; 85,466 minus 73,130 is 12,336, a 16.9 percent increase fairly stated as about 17 percent; and $142.0 million divided by $1.733 million is 81.9, fairly stated as about eighty-two dollars. Precision is consistent at two or three significant figures, every key figure is hedged with "about" and footnoted, and the summed figures are explicitly flagged as arithmetic rather than measurement, with the article itself noting the assumptions it rests on. The only nits are the two forage totals resting on different bases, since the AUM-based 405,000 tons and the daily-rate-based 225,000 tons for horses alone describe different animal sets by different methods, which is labeled but easy to misread as a contradiction; and a small internal tension, since the $82 ratio divides a fiscal year 2025 appropriation by a fee-year 2026 bill, though both figures are individually sourced and the mixing is visible in the text. None of these is a material error, and the 3.3-times Nevada ratio and the 5-to-8-times East Pershing ratio also check out.
