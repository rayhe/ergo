# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Earthworms as Unpermitted Dredge-and-Fill Operators"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The piece follows the Ergo template faithfully and holds together as a systematic case: an absurdly exhaustive headline and a deck that restates the whole indictment, eight Roman-numeral sections escalating cleanly from the worm itself, through the plough, the history, the water law, the payload, the scale arithmetic, a genuinely conceded defense, and an impossible prescription ending in "Ergo." The escalation works because each section adds a new load-bearing fact, the ten tons per acre, the 53,767 worms per acre, the 38 inches of castings over the Chedworth villa, the $68,445 per day penalty, the 6.5-foot burrow against the five-foot OSHA line, the $22.4 trillion a day in fines, rather than merely restating the joke. The six pullquotes are well placed and mostly sharp, and the footnotes and Sources section are complete and properly linked. The main drag is the prescription, which borrows the ladder-scaled-to-the-worm gag, a joke that lands softer than the permit arithmetic around it, and the headline, while in-format, buries its punchline, "No worm has ever applied for a permit," at the very end of a very long sentence.

## 2. Voice Coach — 8.5/10
The voice holds the Ergo register almost throughout: flat, procedural, and relentlessly literal, with the humor carried entirely by the precision of the measurements and the legal citations rather than by any aside or wink. The cadence is the article's strength, with short declarative sentences and parallel constructions, "A casting is a discharge, and a burrow is an excavation," doing the satirical work that adjectives never do. The slips are audible: "The worm" opens a dozen sentences, "The burrow" opens most of the rest, and the repetition dulls the flatness it is meant to serve. The Darwin material risks warmth, the bassoon experiment and the vegetable preferences are charming on their own terms, and the piece mostly resists admiring them, keeping the flat register. Still, the final "Ergo." lands because the preceding prescription has earned its flatness, and the piece never once steps out of character to admire its own joke.

## 3. Ethics — 8.5/10
Spot-checked claims against their sources: the ten tons per acre per year, the 20 ounces per worm, the 53,767 and 26,883 per-acre counts, and the 320 million tons for Great Britain match the 1881 contemporary reviews on Darwin Online; the 14-inch length, 0.39-ounce weight, and 6.5-foot burrow match National Geographic; the five hearts, no lungs, skin breathing, and hermaphrodite anatomy match the cited natural history; the Minnesota invasion figures, 15 species, seven invading hardwood forests, 11,000 wormless years, match the Minnesota DNR and the Fish and Wildlife Service; the 404 permit requirement and the $68,445 per day penalty match the cited statute and inflation-adjustment reporting; the Sackett holding and the $40,000-a-day threat match the opinion; and the scale arithmetic is internally consistent. The defense section is the piece's ethical strong point: it fairly presents the dispositive escape hatches, above all the Sackett narrowing, that the Act regulates persons and the worm is not one, that OSHA protects employees and no employee enters a burrow, that the Minnesota invasion was caused by humans, and that there is no known way to remove introduced worms, before the disclosed "overruled" joke. Minor nits: the penalty figure is routed through a secondary site rather than the eCFR text itself; the "600 million years" worm age rests on a popular history piece rather than a primary source; and the "worm eats when it rains" line in Section V is a generalization rather than a cited observation. Nothing is invented or misattributed in substance, the deliberately wrong conclusion is honestly fenced by a complete defense, and the piece serves the educational mission well.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.5/10
The premise is instantly graspable in one sentence, worms move ten tons of soil per acre every year without a permit while the fine is $68,445 a day, and it lands well because nearly everyone has seen the subject and the numbers are repeatable. The piece is rich in quotable, tweet-length lines and surprising-but-true facts that travel well, from the Roman villa buried under 38 inches of worm castings, to Darwin testing the worms with a bassoon, to a burrow deeper than the OSHA five-foot line, to $22.4 trillion a day in fines. Concrete anchors help too: the 328 million acres of American cropland, the 8.8 trillion worms on it, and the 164 million dump-truck loads a year. The headline, however, fails as a social hook: at roughly 60 words it cannot be quoted, posted, or remembered, so all sharing work falls to the deck and pullquotes instead. Still very high share potential, docked mainly for a headline that buries its own punchline.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
Every citation checked against the sources holds up: section 404 of the Clean Water Act requires a permit for the discharge of dredged or fill material into the waters of the United States under 33 U.S.C. 1344, with permits issued by the Army Corps of Engineers under EPA guidelines; the section 309(d) civil penalty, originally $25,000 per day, now stands at $68,445 per day per violation under the inflation-adjustment rules at 40 CFR Part 19; Sackett v. EPA, 598 U.S. 651 (2023), is accurately described, including the continuous-surface-connection test, the relatively permanent waters requirement, and the $40,000-a-day threat to the Sacketts; and the OSHA excavation standard is stated correctly, including the definition of an excavation, the five-foot protective-system threshold at 29 CFR 1926.652(a)(1), the competent-person definition and daily-inspection duty, and the four-foot egress rule with 25 feet of lateral travel. The article is honest about the decisive escape hatches: Section VII correctly states that section 404 reaches discharges into waters of the United States rather than castings on a lawn, that the Act regulates persons, that OSHA protects employees, and that the Minnesota invasion was human-caused. None of this changes the verdict, because the deliberately wrong conclusion, that every worm requires a section 404 permit, is the genre's disclosed conceit, stated only after the article itself has laid out the law that defeats it, and the "defense rests, it is overruled" turn is the joke rather than a misstatement of law.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10
All five spot-checked claims check out in substance: the Darwin figures match the 1881 reviews on Darwin Online; the burrow depth matches National Geographic; the Minnesota invasion matches the DNR; the Sackett holding matches the opinion; and the 328-million-acre cropland figure matches the USDA Economic Research Service. The sourcing behind those true claims is nevertheless secondary for a load-bearing investigation in places, with the anatomy routed through popular natural-history sites rather than a zoology text, the penalty figure routed through a legal summary rather than the eCFR, and the worm age routed through a magazine piece rather than the fossil record. The 18-tons-per-acre Kent figure is honestly Darwin's own observation from the same reviews, and the scale section flags its own arithmetic as arithmetic rather than measurement. One detail went beyond its first citation and was fixed before publication: the Kent 18-ton figure was given the Reading Mercury review link that actually states it, rather than resting on the Spectator review alone. No load-bearing claim proved false.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
All of the listed arithmetic recomputes correctly within rounding: 328 million acres times 10 tons is 3.28 billion tons per year; 3.28 billion divided by 365 is 8,986,301 tons, fairly stated as about 9 million tons a day; 3.28 billion divided by 20 tons per truck is 164 million truckloads a year, and divided by 365 is 449,315, fairly stated as about 450,000 a day; 328 million acres times $68,445 is $22,449,960,000,000, fairly stated as about $22.4 trillion per day; times 365 is $8,194,235,400,000,000, fairly stated as about $8.2 quadrillion per year; 26,883 worms per acre times 328 million acres is 8,817,624,000,000, fairly stated as about 8.8 trillion worms; 32 million acres times 10 tons is 320 million tons, as Darwin's contemporaries calculated; 0.2 inches per year times 100 years is 20 inches per century; 38 inches divided by 0.2 inches per year is 190 years, fairly stated as less than two centuries; 3 to 4 feet is 36 to 48 inches, divided by 0.2 inches per year is 180 to 240 years, fairly stated as less than four centuries; and 6.5 feet minus 5 feet is 1.5 feet past the threshold. Precision is consistent at two or three significant figures, every key figure is hedged with "about" and footnoted, and the summed figures are explicitly flagged as arithmetic rather than measurement, with the article itself noting the assumptions it rests on. The only nits are the two fine totals resting on different bases, the per-acre daily figure and the annual figure, which is labeled but easy to misread as a single claim; and a small internal tension, since the $22.4 trillion prices 2026 violations at a 2025 penalty rate, though both figures are individually sourced and the mixing is visible in the text. None of these is a material error.
