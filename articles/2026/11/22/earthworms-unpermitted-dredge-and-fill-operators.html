Earthworms Move More Than Ten Tons of Soil to the Surface of Every Acre Every Year, Charles Darwin Spent Thirty-Nine Years Proving It, the Clean Water Act Requires a Permit to Discharge Dredged or Fill Material Into the Waters of the United States, One Nightcrawler Digs Six and a Half Feet Deep, and No Worm Has Ever Applied for a Permit

The earthworm is an excavation contractor that fits on no permit. It digs burrows six and a half feet deep, brings more than ten tons of soil to the surface of every acre each year, and deposits the material on the ground in little mounds, and it does all of it without a single filing, without a competent person on site, and without ever having applied for the privilege, and it has done so for six hundred million years. The United States has spent fifty years building a permit system for the discharge of dredged or fill material, and the worm has spent all of it ignoring the system. This investigation examines the largest unpermitted dredge and fill operation in the United States, the animal that runs it, the soil it moves, the law it evades, and the permit the record demands.

I. The Worm

The earthworm is a terrestrial annelid, a segmented worm of the family Lumbricidae, and there are about 3,000 species of earthworm known worldwide. They range in size from about 0.4 inches to 9 feet in length, though most of the familiar species are a few inches long. The common earthworm, Lumbricus terrestris, known in the United States as the nightcrawler, is indigenous to Europe and is now abundant in North America and western Asia. It grows up to 14 inches long and weighs up to 0.39 ounces. It is the species this investigation is about, and it is the species under every lawn in the country.54

The worm is built for the work it does, and for nothing else. It has five hearts, or more precisely five pairs of aortic arches that pump blood through its body. It has no lungs, and no nose, and it breathes through its skin, which must stay moist for dissolved oxygen to pass into its bloodstream. It has no eyes, and no legs, and it moves by contracting the longitudinal and circular muscles wrapped around each of its segments, gripping the soil with tiny bristles called setae. It is blind, and it is deaf. Darwin established the deafness by experiment, sounding a tin whistle and playing a piano close by, and having his son play a bassoon loudly at the worms, and the worms did not react.38

The worm is a simultaneous hermaphrodite, meaning each worm carries both male and female reproductive organs, and two worms mate by lying together and exchanging sperm, after which each forms a cocoon and both produce young. It cannot reproduce alone, and it requires another worm, and the population therefore doubles by pairs. The worm eats soil to extract the organic matter in it, and it eats dead leaves, and it drags whole leaves down into its burrow to line it and to feed on at leisure. In a single night, a nightcrawler can travel as far as 62 feet along the soil surface in search of food.35

Earthworms fall into three ecological groups, defined by where they live in the soil. The epigeic worms live in the leaf litter on the surface. The endogeic worms live in the topsoil and move horizontally through it. The anecic worms, the deep burrowers, build permanent, near-vertical burrows that reach several feet into the soil, and they come to the surface at night to feed. The nightcrawler is anecic. Its burrow is its home, its workplace, and its excavation, and it is permanent: the same burrow is used for the life of the worm, and the worm defends it.5

The worm is numerous beyond ordinary counting. In a single hectare of land, about 2.47 acres, there may be 500,000 to 2 million individual worms, and the total wet biomass of all those worms can equal ten times the total weight of all the animals living aboveground on the same plot. In some places in Britain, there is a ton of earthworms per acre. The worm is the largest workforce in the soil, and it works every night, and it has never once filed a timesheet.56

Five hearts, no lungs, no eyes, and a burrow six and a half feet deep.

II. The Plough

The scale of the worm's work was measured by Charles Darwin, and the measurement has stood for a century and a half. Darwin estimated that in many parts of England, a weight of more than ten tons of dry earth annually passes through the bodies of worms and is brought to the surface on each acre of land. The castings of the worm amount on average to a uniform layer of mould one-fifth of an inch thick every year. At that rate, the whole superficial bed of vegetable mould passes through the bodies of worms in the course of every few years.2

The per-worm figures are as precise as the per-acre ones. Darwin assumed, following the estimate of Dr. Hensen, that 53,767 worms exist in an acre of garden ground, and that half that number, 26,883 worms, may be found in ordinary grass and arable land. Each worm annually ejects about 20 ounces, or one and one-quarter pounds, of earth. On a down in Kent, Darwin found the worms had thrown up digested soil amounting to 18 tons per acre. The arithmetic is Darwin's own, and the conclusion is Darwin's own: the land was in fact regularly ploughed, and still continues to be thus ploughed, by earthworms, long before man existed.2

Darwin's contemporaries did the multiplication for Great Britain. There are 32,000,000 acres of land in Great Britain suitable for the activity of worms, and if ten tons of earth pass through these creatures on every one of such acres in the year, 320,000,000 tons of earth are brought to the surface by them in Great Britain alone, in a single year. The whole of the superficial mould over any such expanse has passed, and will again pass, every few years through the bodies of worms. The plough is one of the most ancient and most valuable of man's inventions, but the worm was ploughing before the plough existed.2

The worm is strong for its size, and the strength is part of the operation. Earthworms can easily shift stones 60 times their own weight while burrowing. They bring soil up from a depth of three to eight feet, and they carry it to the surface grain by grain, and they deposit it there as castings. On sloping ground, the fine earth is carried away by rain to lower levels, where it forms extremely fertile soil. The worm moves the earth, and the rain moves it again, and neither of them holds a permit for either movement.67

Ten tons of earth per acre, every year, moved by animals Darwin called lowly organised creatures.

III. The History

The worm has been observed for as long as there have been observers. Aristotle described earthworms as the intestines of the earth. The description is exact: the worm takes the earth in at one end, passes it through its intestines, and deposits it at the other, and the earth that comes out is not the earth that went in. It has been finely triturated in the gizzard of the creature, and triturated with the fibrous parts of the leaves on which it feeds, and the mould which results is a totally different substance from the substance on which the worm first begins to act.62

Darwin first published his views on the worm in 1837, in a paper read to the Geological Society of London, and he continued his observations for the next forty years, supplementing them with numerous experiments, after the persevering fashion with which he had familiarized his readers. His last book was the worm book. The Formation of Vegetable Mould through the Action of Worms, with Observations on their Habits was published on October 10, 1881, just six months before he died, and it was reviewed enthusiastically, and it sold well. It was the culmination of 39 years of research into what is often considered among the humblest of creatures.15

Darwin's experiments on the worm were exhaustive, and they are part of the record. He tested the worms' sensitivity to light, warmth, and sound by placing lamps, candles, and hot pokers close to them, blowing tobacco smoke over them, sounding a tin whistle, and playing a piano close by, and having his son play a bassoon loudly. He found the worms chose their food with discrimination, preferring cabbage, carrot, celery, and horseradish before other vegetables, and he found they selected which part of a leaf to pull down first into their burrows. He pronounced them distinctly intelligent by reason of the way they make and line their burrows, and draw in fallen leaves to close them up.81

The worm's work is written into the archaeological record, because the worm buries the record. Coins, gold ornaments, and stone implements, if dropped on the surface of the ground, will infallibly be buried by the castings of the worms in a few years, and will thus be safely preserved until the land at some future period is turned up. The tesselated pavement of a Roman villa at Abinger, in Surrey, was covered with at least fourteen inches of worm castings. The remains of a Roman villa at Chedworth, in Gloucestershire, were concealed under thirty-eight inches of similar soil. The villa at Brading, in the Isle of Wight, had been buried by worms to the depth of from three to four feet. The Roman towns of Silchester and Uriconium were buried the same way, and one of the fallen blocks at Stonehenge has sunk considerably below the level of the surrounding ground through the same agency.7

Darwin closed the book with the sentence that has outlived the book. "When we behold a wide, turf-covered expanse, we should remember that its smoothness, on which so much of its beauty depends, is mainly due to all the inequalities having been slowly levelled by worms. It is a marvellous reflection that the whole of the superficial mould over any such expanse has passed, and will again pass, every few years, through the bodies of worms. The plough is one of the most ancient and most valuable of man's inventions; but long before he existed the land was in fact regularly ploughed, and still continues to be thus ploughed by earth-worms. It may be doubted whether there are many other animals which have played so important a part in the history of the world, as have these lowly organised creatures."7

The Roman villa at Chedworth lies under thirty-eight inches of worm castings.

IV. The Law

The law of the discharge is written against exactly this kind of operator. The Clean Water Act makes it unlawful to discharge any pollutant into navigable waters except in compliance with a permit, and dredged or fill material is a pollutant. Under section 404 of the Act, 33 U.S.C. 1344, a permit is required for the discharge of dredged or fill material into the waters of the United States, and the permit is issued by the Secretary of the Army, through the Army Corps of Engineers, under guidelines written by the Environmental Protection Agency. The permit is the instrument behind nearly every road crossing, pipeline, subdivision, or pond that touches a wetland or stream. No worm has ever held one.12

The penalty for discharging without the permit is set by section 309(d) of the Act, and it has grown with inflation. The statute originally capped civil penalties at $25,000 per day for each violation. Under the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act, the agencies raise the caps every year based on the Consumer Price Index, and the current maximum is $68,445 per day for each violation in federal court. Every day of noncompliance counts separately, and every violation counts separately, and the exposure at a single facility can climb into the millions within weeks. A court-imposed civil penalty under section 404(s)(4) currently tops out at the same $68,445 per day for each day the violation continues, and a restoration order to undo the unauthorized fill can be ordered on top of any penalty.13

The scope of the waters the Act reaches was narrowed by the Supreme Court in 2023, and the narrowing is part of the worm's case and part of the case against it. In Sackett v. Environmental Protection Agency, 598 U.S. 651 (2023), decided May 25, 2023, the Court held that the term "waters of the United States" refers only to geographic features described in ordinary parlance as streams, oceans, rivers, and lakes, and to adjacent wetlands that are indistinguishable from those bodies of water due to a continuous surface connection. Michael and Chantell Sackett had bought property near Priest Lake, Idaho, and begun backfilling the lot with dirt to prepare for building a home, and the Environmental Protection Agency informed them that their backfilling violated the Clean Water Act and ordered them to restore the site, threatening penalties of over $40,000 per day. The Sacketts sued, and the Court ruled for the Sacketts, and all nine Justices agreed that many wetlands, including the Sacketts', do not qualify as waters of the United States.14

The second body of law reaches the worm from below. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration's excavation standard, 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, governs every excavation, and OSHA defines an excavation as any man-made cut, cavity, trench, or depression in an earth surface, formed by earth removal. Under 29 CFR 1926.652(a)(1), each employee in an excavation shall be protected from cave-ins by an adequate protective system, by sloping, shoring, or shielding, except when the excavation is made entirely in stable rock, or is less than 5 feet in depth and examination of the ground by a competent person provides no indication of a potential cave-in. A competent person is defined as one capable of identifying existing and predictable hazards, and who has authorization to take prompt corrective measures to eliminate them, and the competent person must inspect the excavation, the adjacent areas, and the protective systems daily, before work begins and as needed throughout each work shift. In trench excavations 4 feet or more deep, a stairway, ladder, ramp, or other safe means of egress must be located so that no employee need travel more than 25 feet laterally to reach it.1516

The worm's burrow exceeds every threshold the standard sets. A nightcrawler burrows during the day, typically keeping close to the surface, and is capable of digging down as deep as 6.5 feet. Six and a half feet is one and a half feet deeper than the five-foot line at which a protective system becomes mandatory. The burrow is a cavity in an earth surface, formed by earth removal. It is vertical, and it is unshored, and it is unsloped, and it is unshielded, and no competent person has ever inspected it, and there is no ladder within 25 feet, and no ladder at all. The worm works in it every day, and it has worked in it for six hundred million years, and the excavation has never once been inspected.415

The law requires a permit to discharge dredged material. The worm discharges it daily.

V. The Payload

The payload is the whole dispute, and it is small, and it is everywhere. A casting is the residue of the worm's food, passing through its body and getting mixed with its intestinal secretions, and it is deposited on the surface of the ground as a small mound of finely triturated earth. The earth in a casting has been swallowed, ground in the gizzard, mixed with the fibrous parts of the leaves the worm eats, and voided in a form very different from the form in which it existed before it passed into the worm. The casting is dredged material in the plain sense of the words: material excavated from below the surface and deposited on the surface, by an operator, in the open, where anyone can see it.2

The burrow is the other half of the payload, and it is the excavation the casting came out of. The nightcrawler builds a permanent, near-vertical burrow, lined at the top with leaves it has dragged down from the surface, and it retreats into the burrow by day and emerges at night to feed. The burrow opens directly to the surface of the soil, and the castings accumulate in mounds at the burrow opening, and the quantity of soil on the surface indicates that the worm must actively ingest soil and bring it to the surface for deposit. The worm excavates the burrow, and the worm deposits the spoil, and the worm does both in the same motion, and the spoil pile is the evidence.5

The payload is also the reason the worm cannot be permitted like other excavators. An excavator must file a plan, and the plan must show where the material will go, and the worm's plan is written in no office. A permit must name the operator, and the operator must be a person, and the worm is not a person. A permit must state the volume, and the volume changes every night, because the worm eats when it rains and rests when it is dry, and the rain decides the schedule. A permit must be inspected, and the inspection must reach the work, and the work is six and a half feet underground, in a hole the width of a finger, in the dark.12

The payload moves twice, and the second movement is the one the Act was written for. The worm brings the fine earth to the surface, and on sloping ground the fine earth is carried away by rain to lower levels, where it forms extremely fertile soil. The rain washes the worm's discharge downhill, into ditches, into streams, into the waters of the United States. The worm discharges the material, and the rain conveys it, and the conveyance is continuous, and the surface connection is visible after every storm. The worm is the discharger, and the rain is the outfall, and neither of them has ever applied for a permit.2

A casting is a discharge, and a burrow is an excavation.

VI. The Scale

Do the arithmetic the record permits. For 2024, total United States cropland acreage reported in the Department of Agriculture's Major Land Uses data series was 328 million acres. At Darwin's rate of more than ten tons of dry earth brought to the surface per acre per year, the worms of American cropland move more than 3.28 billion tons of soil to the surface every year, without a permit. That is about 9 million tons a day. At 20 tons per dump truck, it is 164 million truckloads a year, or about 450,000 truckloads a day, every day, in every season, in the open, on the public record.172

The fines follow the same arithmetic. Under section 309(d), the civil penalty is up to $68,445 per day for each violation. If each acre of cropland is one violation, and each day is one day of violation, the daily exposure is 328 million acres times $68,445, which is about $22.4 trillion per day. Over a year, at that rate, the exposure is about $8.2 quadrillion. The figure is arithmetic, not measurement, and the assumption is stated with it: that each acre is one violation, which no court has ever held. The arithmetic is correct anyway, and the worms are real, and the permits do not exist.1317

The worm count follows the same arithmetic. Darwin assumed 26,883 worms per acre on ordinary grass and arable land. On 328 million acres of cropland, that is about 8.8 trillion worms. At 20 ounces of earth per worm per year, the flock is the largest excavation workforce in the country, and it is the only one that has never been counted by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, never been issued a hard hat, and never been visited by an inspector. In Great Britain alone, Darwin's contemporaries calculated, the worms bring 320 million tons of earth to the surface in a single year, on 32 million acres, at the same ten tons per acre.217

The volume comparison is the sharpest. A casting layer one-fifth of an inch thick per year becomes 20 inches in a century, and the Roman villa at Chedworth was buried under 38 inches, which is less than two centuries of worm work. The villa at Brading was buried three to four feet deep, which is less than four centuries of worm work. The whole superficial bed of vegetable mould passes through the bodies of worms in the course of every few years, which means the ground under every building in the country has been excavated and redeposited, and excavated and redeposited again, since the building was built, and the foundation was never notified.27

The depth comparison is sharper still. A nightcrawler burrows to 6.5 feet, which is 1.5 feet past the five-foot line at which federal law requires a protective system. A worm burrow is deeper, relative to its width, than any trench a human crew would be permitted to enter unshored, and the worm enters it every day, headfirst, in the dark, with no ladder, no shoring, and no competent person watching. The worm is also worth more than the soil it moves: in some places in Britain, there is a ton of earthworms per acre, and the ton is alive, and it works for nothing.4156

Three point two eight billion tons a year, at sixty-eight thousand four hundred forty-five dollars a day per violation, is twenty-two point four trillion dollars a day.

VII. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the statute itself. Section 404 regulates the discharge of dredged or fill material into the waters of the United States, and the worm deposits its castings on land. A casting on a lawn is not a discharge into a navigable water, and a burrow in a garden is not a wetland, and after Sackett, a wetland must have a continuous surface connection to a relatively permanent body of water connected to traditional interstate navigable waters, and most worm castings have no such connection. The rain may carry the fine earth downhill, but the worm does not carry it there, and the rain is not the worm's agent.1412

Its second is the word the statute uses. Section 404 permits are issued to persons, and the application must be signed, and the applicant must be capable of complying with the conditions, and a worm is not a person, and it has no hands, and it cannot sign, and it cannot pay a penalty of $68,445 a day, and it cannot be served with process, because it lives six and a half feet underground and it is deaf. The Act was written for developers, and the worm is not a developer.1213

Its third is the excavation standard. OSHA protects employees, and the worm is not an employee, and no employee enters a worm burrow, and the burrow is the width of a finger, and no human could fit in it if he tried. The standard defines an excavation as a man-made cut, and the worm's burrow is not man-made. It is worm-made, and the standard does not reach it, and the competent person the standard requires would have to be a second worm, and the second worm is also deaf.15

Its fourth is the history of the invasion. In Minnesota, every terrestrial earthworm is a non-native invasive species from Europe or Asia, at least fifteen species introduced so far, and at least seven of them are invading the hardwood forests and causing the loss of tree seedlings, wildflowers, and ferns. The glaciers killed any native North American earthworms in the region, and for the last 11,000 years since the glaciers receded, Minnesota ecosystems developed without earthworms. The worms arrived with soils and plants brought from Europe, and with ship ballast dumped on shore, and with fishing bait carried into remote areas by anglers. The invasion was caused by humans, and the worms were the cargo, and the state that calls them invasive sells them by the pound at every bait shop in the state.910

Its fifth is the impossibility of the remedy. Once earthworms are introduced to an area, there is currently no known way to get rid of them. The worms move less than half a mile on their own over 100 years, so their spread is the work of humans, and the humans who spread them are the ones the law can reach, and the law has never reached them, because the bait was legal when it was sold. The state cannot deport 8.8 trillion worms, and it cannot fine them, and it cannot permit them, and it cannot even find them, because they are underground.1011

Its sixth is the benefit. Darwin proved the work is the foundation of agriculture: the worm makes the vegetable mould, and the mould is the only soil man employs in his agricultural operations, and the whole of the superficial mould over any turf-covered expanse has passed through the bodies of worms. The worm levels the ground, and it drains the land with its burrows, and it opens a way for seeds and the roots of plants. The finest farmland in the country is worm work, and the permit system has never improved a single acre of it.27

Its seventh is the age of the operation. Archaeological evidence suggests worms have been around for 600 million years, and the Clean Water Act was enacted in 1972. The worm was moving ten tons per acre per year for 600 million years before the permit existed, and it has moved ten tons per acre per year for the 54 years since, and the Act has never once been enforced against it, and no inspector has ever found a burrow, because the burrows are everywhere.8

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VIII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every worm should be registered as a dredge and fill operator, and every burrow should be permitted under section 404, and every casting should be weighed, and logged, and reported, and every burrow deeper than five feet should be sloped, shored, or shielded, as 29 CFR 1926.652 requires, and every burrow should have a competent person assigned to it, and the competent person should inspect the burrow daily, before work begins and as needed throughout each work shift, and every burrow four feet or deeper should have a ladder within 25 feet of lateral travel, and the ladder should be scaled to the worm.121516

The Corps of Engineers should establish a Worm Division, register the burrows, and collect the $22.4 trillion a day in unpaid penalties from the cropland flock, and the $8.2 quadrillion for the year, and the fines for the 320 million tons a year in Great Britain, which is outside the Corps' jurisdiction but inside the arithmetic. The estates of the 8.8 trillion worms should be billed. Darwin's closing sentence is not a permit exemption, and Aristotle's description is not a franking privilege.1317

The queue should be cleared in order. The Corps should process the registrations before any further worm is hatched, and the burrows should be assigned numbers, and the castings should be weighed, and the permits should be issued, and the inspections should be conducted, and the fees should be collected, and the bait shops should be licensed as distributors of unpermitted operators, and the anglers should be charged as transporters, and the rain that carries the fine earth downhill should be permitted as a conveyance, since the flock has now exceeded its five-foot excavation limit for six hundred million years in the open, on the public record, one casting at a time.915

The test case is the next rain. The worms will eat, and the castings will appear, and the burrows will deepen, and the fine earth will wash downhill, and the permits will not exist, and the largest unpermitted dredge and fill operation in the United States will operate for another year, in the open, under every lawn in the country, one worm at a time.24
