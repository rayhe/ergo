# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Cranberries as Unpermitted Section 404 Dischargers"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The piece follows the Ergo template faithfully and holds together as a systematic case: an absurdly exhaustive headline and a deck that restates the whole indictment, eight Roman-numeral sections escalating cleanly from the berry itself, through the bog, the flood, the sand, the law, the scale arithmetic, a genuinely conceded defense, and an impossible prescription ending in "Ergo." The escalation works because each section adds a new load-bearing fact, the four air pockets, the eighteen-inch flood, the winter flood held to March 15, the half-inch to two-inch sanding rotation, the ten-acre cap in Nationwide Permit 34, the 894.6 million pound crop, the $2.4 billion a day in fines, rather than merely restating the joke. The six pullquotes are well placed and mostly sharp, and the footnotes and Sources section are complete and properly linked, with every footnote referenced from the body. The main drag is the headline, which, while in-format, buries its sharpest fact, that the Corps wrote a permit just for cranberries, in the middle of a very long sentence. The Thanksgiving timing is a strength, not a gimmick: the holiday-calendar theme for November 26 is cranberry bog engineering, and the piece lands two days before the holiday.

## 2. Voice Coach — 8.5/10
The voice holds the Ergo register almost throughout: flat, procedural, and relentlessly literal, with the humor carried entirely by the precision of the measurements and the legal citations rather than by any aside or wink. The cadence is the article's strength, with short declarative sentences and parallel constructions, "The bog is flooded twice a year, once for the harvest and once for the winter," doing the satirical work that adjectives never do. The slips are audible: "The bog," "The flood," and "The sand" open a dozen sentences each, and the repetition dulls the flatness it is meant to serve, the same tic the earthworm piece was docked for. The Ocean Spray history risks warmth, the lawyer who left his practice for a bog is charming on its own terms, and the piece mostly resists admiring it, keeping the flat register. The final "Ergo." lands because the preceding prescription has earned its flatness, and the piece never once steps out of character to admire its own joke.

## 3. Ethics — 8.5/10
Spot-checked claims against their sources: the 8,946,000-barrel 2024 crop and the state-by-state breakdown match the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service summary; the 35,300 harvested acres and 241.5 barrels per acre match the USDA figures as tabulated; the eighteen-inch flood, the egg-beater reel, and the floating berries match the harvest reporting; the four air pockets and the four-inch bounce standard match the growers' own descriptions; the winter flood dates, the 4 mg/l oxygen threshold, and the 5 mg/l drawdown rule match the University of Massachusetts best management practices; the sanding depth and interval match the same extension source; the Nationwide Permit 34 terms, the ten-acre cap, the pre-construction notification, and the exclusions match the Corps' own permit text; the $68,445 per day penalty matches the inflation-adjustment reporting; the Sackett holding matches the opinion; and the FDA qualified health claim language, including "limited and inconsistent," matches the agency's own announcement. The defense section is the piece's ethical strong point: it fairly presents the dispositive escape hatches, above all that Nationwide Permit 34 already authorizes the work, that routine farming is exempt under section 404(f), that the bogs predate the Act by a century and a half, that the flood water is recycled and returned, that the bogs preserve wetlands, and that Sackett narrows the Act's reach, before the disclosed "overruled" joke. Minor nits: the 404(f)(1) farming exemption is routed through a law review comment rather than the statute text itself; the "flooded twice" line about canned berries was caught and corrected to "flooded once" before publication, since the winter flood protects the vines, not the harvested fruit. Nothing is invented or misattributed in substance, the deliberately wrong conclusion is honestly fenced by a complete defense, and the piece serves the educational mission well.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.5/10
The premise is instantly graspable in one sentence, cranberries are grown by flooding wetlands and the Army Corps wrote a permit just for cranberry bogs that caps disturbance at ten acres, and it lands well because nearly everyone eats the subject two days from now and the numbers are repeatable. The piece is rich in quotable, tweet-length lines and surprising-but-true facts that travel well, from the berry that bounces four inches to the bog that spends a third of the year underwater, to 5,062,500 gallons of jellied sauce a holiday season, to a permit numbered 34 written for a single fruit. Concrete anchors help too: the 894.6 million pound crop, the 5.8 to 17.3 billion gallons of floodwater, and the 26,320 acres beyond the cap. The headline, however, fails as a social hook: at roughly 60 words it cannot be quoted, posted, or remembered, so all sharing work falls to the deck and pullquotes instead. Still very high share potential, docked mainly for a headline that buries its own punchline.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
Every citation checked against the sources holds up: section 404 of the Clean Water Act requires a permit for the discharge of dredged or fill material into the waters of the United States under 33 U.S.C. 1344, with permits issued by the Army Corps of Engineers under EPA guidelines; Nationwide Permit 34 authorizes discharges of dredged or fill material for dikes, berms, pumps, water control structures, and leveling of cranberry beds at existing operations, with a cumulative ten-acre disturbance cap per operation, a no-net-loss condition, and a mandatory pre-construction notification, and it excludes warehouses, processing facilities, and new farms in new locations; the section 309(d) civil penalty, originally $25,000 per day, now stands at $68,445 per day per violation under the inflation-adjustment rules at 40 CFR Part 19; Sackett v. EPA, 598 U.S. 651 (2023), is accurately described, including the continuous-surface-connection test, the relatively permanent waters requirement, and the $40,000-a-day threat to the Sacketts; and the Capper-Volstead Act of 1922 exemption for agricultural cooperatives, and the 1930 blessing of the Ocean Spray cooperative by the Justice and Agriculture Departments, are accurately described. The article is honest about the decisive escape hatches: Section VII correctly states that Nationwide Permit 34 already authorizes the work, that routine farming is exempt, that the bogs predate the Act, and that Sackett narrows the waters the Act reaches. None of this changes the verdict, because the deliberately wrong conclusion, that every bog flood requires counting against the ten-acre cap, is the genre's disclosed conceit, stated only after the article itself has laid out the law that defeats it, and the "defense rests, it is overruled" turn is the joke rather than a misstatement of law.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10
All five spot-checked claims check out in substance: the 2024 production figures match the USDA NASS summary; the flood depth and method match the harvest reporting; the winter flood rules match the UMass best management practices; the Nationwide Permit 34 terms match the Corps' permit text; and the FDA qualified health claim matches the agency's announcement and letter of enforcement discretion. The sourcing behind those true claims is primary for the load-bearing investigation in most places, with USDA data, Corps permit text, FDA letters, and university extension guidance carrying the weight. The exceptions are the penalty figure, routed through a legal summary rather than the eCFR text itself, and the harvested acreage table, routed through a crop guide tabulating USDA figures rather than the NASS release itself. The 2024 production and 2025 acreage figures come from different years, which the article labels but which a careless reader could blend. One detail went beyond its first citation and was fixed before publication: the canned berries were originally described as flooded twice, when only the vines, not the harvested fruit, see the winter flood. No load-bearing claim proved false.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
All of the listed arithmetic recomputes correctly within rounding: 8,946,000 barrels times 100 pounds is 894,600,000 pounds; 5,490,000 divided by 8,946,000 is 61.4 percent, fairly stated as about 61 percent; 2,245,000 divided by 8,946,000 is 25.1 percent, fairly stated as about 25 percent; 241.5 barrels per acre times 100 pounds is 24,150 pounds per acre; 35,300 acres times 1.5 feet is 52,950 acre-feet, times 325,851 gallons per acre-foot is 17,253,810,450 gallons, fairly stated as about 17.3 billion gallons; 35,300 acres times 0.5 feet is 17,650 acre-feet, fairly stated as about 5.8 billion gallons; one inch of sand over 35,300 acres is 35,300 times 134.44 cubic yards per acre-inch, fairly stated as about 4.7 million cubic yards, and a quarter of that per year is about 1.2 million cubic yards; 898 farms times 10 acres is 8,980 acres, which is 25.4 percent of 35,300 acres, and 35,300 minus 8,980 is 26,320 acres; 35,300 acres times $68,445 is $2,416,108,500, fairly stated as about $2.4 billion per day; times 365 is $881,929,602,500, fairly stated as about $882 billion per year. Precision is consistent at two or three significant figures, every key figure is hedged with "about" and footnoted, and the summed figures are explicitly flagged as arithmetic rather than measurement, with the article itself noting the assumptions it rests on. The only nits are the winter flood being described as doubling the water without a sourced depth range of its own, so "doubles" is an approximation rather than a measurement; and the mixing of 2024 production with 2025 acreage, which is labeled but easy to miss. None of these is a material error.
