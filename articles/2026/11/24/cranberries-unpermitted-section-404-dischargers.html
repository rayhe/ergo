Investigation About
Wetlands Law & Cranberry Agriculture

Cranberries Are Grown in Wetlands, Flooded With Eighteen Inches of Water to Harvest, Flooded Again All Winter, Sanded With a Layer of Fill Every Few Years, the Army Corps of Engineers Wrote a Nationwide Permit Just for Cranberry Bogs Capping Disturbance at Ten Acres Per Operation, the 2024 Crop Was 894 Million Pounds, and the Bogs Are Drained in the Spring

The cranberry is a wetland fruit that fits on no permit. It grows on low vines in bogs built in swampy ground, and the growers flood those bogs with up to eighteen inches of water to harvest the fruit, flood them again all winter to keep the vines alive, and spread a layer of sand over them every few years to prune them, and they do all of it with dikes, berms, pumps, and water control structures, in the open, in full view of the law. The Army Corps of Engineers has spent years writing a permit for exactly this operation, a nationwide permit numbered 34, and the permit caps the disturbance at ten acres per operation and demands a pre-construction notification. The industry farms about 35,300 acres. This investigation examines the largest permitted-by-number but uncounted-by-acre flooding and filling operation in the United States, the berry that floats, the bog that floods, the sand that fills, the law that caps it, and the notification the record demands.

By Ezekiel Crane · November 24, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Berry

The cranberry is a low evergreen vine, Vaccinium macrocarpon, a member of the heath family, and it is native to North America. It grows on trailing vines in acidic soil, in beds of sand or peat, in flat ground that can be flooded and drained on command. It is not an aquatic plant, and it does not grow in water. It grows on dry vines in a bog, and the water comes later, on purpose, by pump and gate.63

The berry is built for the flood, and for nothing else. Each cranberry contains four small pockets of air inside it. The air makes the berry less dense than water, so it floats, and it makes the berry springy, so it bounces. A firm, ripe cranberry dropped on a hard surface rebounds, and the growers have turned the rebound into a test: a ripe berry dropped from twelve inches must bounce at least four inches to be considered ready, and the berries that fail the bounce are rejected as unripe or damaged. The same air that makes the berry bounce makes it float, and the same float that makes the harvest possible makes the flood necessary.67

The berry is sorted by its own buoyancy. After harvest, the berries are run over a separator, a bounce board, and the ripe ones bounce over the barrier and continue down the line while the unripe and damaged ones do not bounce and are rejected. The berry inspects itself. No inspector is present, and no inspector is needed, because the berry either bounces or it does not, and the board keeps the record.7

The berry is old, and the record of it is old. Cranberries are native to the wetlands of North America, and the Native peoples of New England used them for dye and for food, drying them with venison and fat into pemmican, a concentrated cake that kept for months. Cranberries are believed to have been present at the first Thanksgiving feast in 1621. The first cranberry juice recipe dates to 1683. The berry was at the table before the table had a country, and it has been at the table ever since.12

The berry is also a medicine that the government has declined to fully endorse. In 2020, the Food and Drug Administration announced that it would not object to a qualified health claim that cranberry juice beverages containing at least 27 percent cranberry juice, consumed at eight ounces a day, may help reduce the risk of recurrent urinary tract infection in healthy women, and that cranberry dietary supplements containing at least 500 milligrams of cranberry fruit powder may do the same. The agency's own words were that the scientific evidence supporting the claim is limited and inconsistent. The claim does not cover dried cranberries, and it does not cover cranberry sauce. The sauce on the Thanksgiving table carries no health claim at all.1415

Four air pockets make the berry float, and the float makes the flood necessary.

II. The Bog

The bog is a constructed wetland, and the construction is the whole of the business. A cranberry bog is a depressed bed of peat and sand surrounded by earthen dikes, built in swampy ground, layered with sand, peat, gravel, and clay, with ditches and flumes and head gates to move water in and out. The Department of Agriculture's own bulletin on establishing cranberry fields, published in 1924, lists the requirements in order: the site must be swampy ground, it must be possible to drain the swamp to a depth of at least eighteen inches, and it must have dams or dikes. The bog is a wetland that was built to be a wetland, and then built again to be a farm.108

The bog was first built by one man watching sand blow. In the early nineteenth century, Captain Henry Hall of Dennis, Massachusetts, a veteran of the Revolutionary War, noticed that sand blown in from nearby dunes helped the wild cranberry vines grow faster, and he began cultivating the vines, transplanting them, and sanding them. Commercial cultivation of the cranberry began with Hall, in the early 1800s, on Cape Cod, and the practice he started, spreading sand on the vines, is still the most commonly used cultural practice in Massachusetts two centuries later.129

The bog is concentrated in a small number of places, and the concentration is part of the case. In the 2022 Census of Agriculture, 898 farms in 14 states reported cranberries. Four states hold nearly all of the crop: Wisconsin, Massachusetts, Oregon, and New Jersey. Plymouth County, Massachusetts, alone holds a quarter of the bearing acreage in the United States. The bogs are not scattered. They are gathered, in counties, in watersheds, in wetland after wetland, where the water is.4

The bog is productive beyond the scale of the fruit. In 2024, the United States produced 8,946,000 barrels of cranberries, and a barrel weighs 100 pounds, so the crop was 894,600,000 pounds. Wisconsin produced 5,490,000 barrels, about 61 percent of the crop. Massachusetts produced 2,245,000 barrels, about 25 percent. Oregon produced 623,000 barrels, and New Jersey 588,000. The national yield was 241.5 barrels per acre, which is 24,150 pounds of fruit per acre, from vines that stand a few inches tall.123

The bog is also a state symbol, and the symbol is the defense's first exhibit. The cranberry has been the official state fruit of Wisconsin since 2004. Wisconsin grows more than 60 percent of the national crop, from about 21,000 acres of bog, and the crop has a one-billion-dollar annual impact in the state and supports more than 4,000 jobs. The state that made the berry its symbol also made the berry its largest fruit crop, and the largest fruit crop in the state is grown in flooded wetlands.204

The bog is a wetland that was built to be a wetland, and then built again to be a farm.

III. The Flood

The flood is the harvest, and the harvest is the flood. In late September and October, when the berries have reached full color, the grower floods the bog, typically to about eighteen inches of water, and in some bogs to as little as six inches. The flood is put on the night before the harvest. A machine called a water reel, nicknamed the egg beater, is driven through the submerged vines, and the agitation loosens the berries from the plant. Because each berry has four pockets of air inside it, the berries float. Within an hour or two, the entire surface of the bog is covered in a dense mat of floating red berries, and workers in waders corral the mat with floating booms and pump it into waiting trucks.56

The flood is the method for nearly the whole crop. About 90 percent of commercial cranberry production is wet-harvested, and the berries that come out of the flood go into juice, sauce, and dried cranberries. The other 10 percent is picked dry, by machines that comb the berries off the vine, and those berries keep their protective bloom and their skin intact, so they go to the fresh fruit market, in the bags in the grocery store in November. The berries in the bag were never flooded. The berries in the can were.6

The flood is also the winter, and the winter flood is longer than the harvest flood. After the harvest, the vines go dormant, carrying next season's buds, and the grower floods the bog again. The winter flood protects the vines from the cold, drying winds that cause winterkill, a kind of frostbite for plants, in which moisture lost from the vines to wind and evaporation cannot be replaced because the root zone is frozen. The injury can occur within three days if the root zone is frozen to a depth of four inches, the air temperature is below freezing, and strong drying winds occur. The flood must cover the plants entirely, with no vine tips sticking out, and it may be applied as early as December 1 and held as long as winterkill conditions are present, generally no later than March 15. The bog spends a third of the year underwater.8

The flood is monitored like a patient, because the flood can also kill the vines. If the dissolved oxygen in the winter flood water drops below 4 milligrams per liter, the plants suffer oxygen deficiency injury, and the yield falls. The grower must monitor the oxygen, and when it falls to 5 milligrams per liter or less, the grower must remove water from beneath the ice. The ice rests on the vines, and the water under the ice is drawn down through the ditches, and the flood is reapplied when the ice melts away. The bog is flooded, drained, reflooded, and drained again, all winter, by hand, by gate, by pump.8

The flood was once the frost protection, too. In the past, growers flooded the bogs for frost protection in the spring and fall, and for irrigation in the summer, tasks that are now accomplished with sprinkler irrigation. The flood remains the tool for the harvest, for the winter, and for the removal of fallen leaves. The University of Massachusetts states the rule plainly: flooding is so important in cranberry cultivation that bogs that cannot be flooded are no longer considered profitable. A bog that cannot be flooded is not a bog. It is a field with the wrong fruit in it.8

The bog spends a third of the year underwater, by design, by pump, by gate.

IV. The Sand

The sand is the fill, and the fill is the practice. Sanding is the most commonly used cultural practice in cranberry production in Massachusetts. The growers apply a thin layer of sand, half an inch to two inches deep, on the surface of producing cranberry bogs, at two to five year intervals, to promote growth, improve productivity, suppress disease, and reduce insect populations. The sand covers the leaf litter on the surface of the bog, and it covers the bare wood at the base of the uprights, and it anchors the runners and promotes rooting, and it buries weed seeds, fungal spores, insect eggs, and overwintering insects. The sand is spread on the ice in winter, and it settles onto the vines as the ice melts in spring. It is also spread from barges, in water, and from ground rigs run directly on the vines.920

The sand is coarse, and the coarseness is specified. The University of Massachusetts directs growers to choose coarse sand, with particle sizes of 0.5 to 2 millimeters, and to apply the proper amount: half to three-quarters of an inch if the bog was sanded recently, more if the last sanding was four or more years ago, and at least an inch when sanding from a barge. The sand is not an accident. It is a measured discharge of mineral material onto a wetland surface, on a schedule, in a quantity the extension service publishes.9

The sand is also the history. Henry Hall sanded his vines because the wind sanded them first. The 1924 bulletin of the Department of Agriculture records that a layer of sand three inches or more thick, spread over the peat, levels the property, fills the depressions, reduces weeding, makes the vines grow more vigorously, protects the field from frost, and controls injurious insects. The bulletin records that sanding was practiced in all cranberry-growing regions except New Jersey and Wisconsin, and that even in those states many growers sanded all new properties they developed. The practice is two hundred years old, and it has never once been filed as a discharge.10

The sand is applied at a pace the industry itself reports. In Wisconsin, about a quarter of the marsh is sanded each year. At that pace, every acre of bog in the country is sanded about once every four years, which matches the Massachusetts interval of two to five years. The sand goes on in the winter, the ice melts, the vines grow through it, and the next sanding is already on the calendar. The fill is not an event. It is a rotation.39

The sand is a measured discharge of mineral material onto a wetland surface, on a schedule.

V. The Law

The law of the discharge is written against exactly this kind of operator, and the Corps has written a permit with the operator's name on it. The Clean Water Act makes it unlawful to discharge any pollutant into navigable waters except in compliance with a permit, and dredged or fill material is a pollutant. Under section 404 of the Act, 33 U.S.C. 1344, a permit is required for the discharge of dredged or fill material into the waters of the United States, and the permit is issued by the Secretary of the Army, through the Army Corps of Engineers, under guidelines written by the Environmental Protection Agency. The permit is the instrument behind nearly every road crossing, pipeline, subdivision, or pond that touches a wetland or stream.18

The permit for cranberries is Nationwide Permit 34, and it is the only nationwide permit written for a single fruit. It authorizes discharges of dredged or fill material for dikes, berms, pumps, water control structures, and the leveling of cranberry beds, associated with expansion, enhancement, or modification activities at existing cranberry production operations. The permit names the dikes the bogs are built of, the berms that hold the flood, the pumps that move it, and the water control structures that release it, and it authorizes the discharge of dredged or fill material for all of them. No other fruit has a permit. The cranberry has one, and the permit has a cap.16

The cap is ten acres. The cumulative total acreage of disturbance per cranberry production operation, including filling, flooding, ditching, or clearing, must not exceed ten acres of waters of the United States, including wetlands, measured over the period the permit is valid. The activity must not result in a net loss of wetland acreage. The permittee must submit a pre-construction notification to the district engineer once during the period the permit is valid, and the permit then authorizes discharges at the existing operation for the permit term, provided the ten-acre limit is not exceeded. The permit does not authorize any discharge related to warehouses, processing facilities, or parking areas, and it does not authorize the construction of new cranberry farms in new locations. It authorizes ten acres of disturbance per operation, and nothing more.16

The penalty for discharging without the permit is set by section 309(d) of the Act, and it has grown with inflation. The statute originally capped civil penalties at $25,000 per day for each violation. Under the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act, the agencies raise the caps every year based on the Consumer Price Index, and the current maximum is $68,445 per day for each violation in federal court. Every day of noncompliance counts separately, and every violation counts separately, and the exposure at a single facility can climb into the millions within weeks.18

The scope of the waters the Act reaches was narrowed by the Supreme Court in 2023, and the narrowing is part of the cranberry's case and part of the case against it. In Sackett v. Environmental Protection Agency, 598 U.S. 651 (2023), decided May 25, 2023, the Court held that the term waters of the United States refers only to geographic features described in ordinary parlance as streams, oceans, rivers, and lakes, and to adjacent wetlands that are indistinguishable from those bodies of water due to a continuous surface connection. Michael and Chantell Sackett had bought property near Priest Lake, Idaho, and begun backfilling the lot with dirt to prepare for building a home, and the Environmental Protection Agency informed them that their backfilling violated the Clean Water Act and ordered them to restore the site, threatening penalties of over $40,000 per day. The Sacketts sued, and the Court ruled for the Sacketts, and all nine Justices agreed that many wetlands, including the Sacketts', do not qualify as waters of the United States.19

The second body of law reaches the cranberry from the market. In 1930, Marcus Urann, the lawyer who had left his legal career to buy a cranberry bog and who had first canned cranberry sauce in 1912, convinced his two largest competitors to join forces under a cooperative, Cranberry Canners, Inc., to set prices and standards and to rationalize production, distribution, quality control, and marketing. The cooperative would have been illegal had an attorney not found an exemption for agricultural cooperatives in the Capper-Volstead Act of 1922, which gave associations making agricultural products limited exemptions from the antitrust laws. The Department of Justice and the Department of Agriculture gave their blessing in 1930, and the cooperative was born. It became the National Cranberry Association in 1946, and Ocean Spray in 1957. Today it is a cooperative of about 700 growers, producing about 70 percent of the world's canned cranberry sauce, and Americans consume 5,062,500 gallons of its jellied cranberry sauce every holiday season.1113

Nationwide Permit 34 is the only nationwide permit written for a single fruit, and it caps the whole industry at ten acres per operation.

VI. The Scale

Do the arithmetic the record permits. In 2025, the United States harvested 35,300 acres of cranberries: 19,100 acres in Wisconsin, 11,000 in Massachusetts, 2,800 in New Jersey, and 2,400 in Oregon. At the harvest flood of eighteen inches, one and a half feet of water over 35,300 acres is 52,950 acre-feet of water, which is about 17.3 billion gallons. At the shallow end of the reported range, six inches of water over the same acreage is 17,650 acre-feet, which is about 5.8 billion gallons. The harvest flood alone moves between 5.8 and 17.3 billion gallons of water onto wetland acreage, in a few weeks, every year, and the water is then drained away.35

The winter flood doubles the water. The same 35,300 acres are flooded again after the harvest, and the flood is held from as early as December 1 until the winterkill conditions pass, generally no later than March 15. The winter flood covers the plants entirely, and it is drained in the spring, and the vines come back to life. The bog is flooded twice a year, once for the harvest and once for the winter, and the water in each flood is measured in billions of gallons, and the flood is the farm.820

The sand follows the same arithmetic. One inch of sand spread over 35,300 acres is about 4.7 million cubic yards of fill. At the Wisconsin pace, about a quarter of the marsh sanded each year, the industry spreads about 1.2 million cubic yards of sand a year, every year, on wetland acreage, in layers of half an inch to two inches, at intervals of two to five years. The sand is fill material, and the bog is a water of the United States, and the permit that authorizes the fill caps the disturbance at ten acres per operation.39

The cap follows the same arithmetic. The 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 898 farms reporting cranberries in 14 states. If every one of those farms used its full ten acres of authorized disturbance under Nationwide Permit 34, the total authorized disturbance would be 8,980 acres. The industry farms 35,300 acres. The cap, used to its fullest by every farm in the country, covers 25.4 percent of the acreage. The remaining 26,320 acres are beyond the cap, in the open, on the public record, one bog at a time.416

The fines follow the same arithmetic. Under section 309(d), the civil penalty is up to $68,445 per day for each violation. If each acre of cranberry bog flooded without authorization is one violation, and each day is one day of violation, the daily exposure is 35,300 acres times $68,445, which is about $2.4 billion per day. Over a year, at that rate, the exposure is about $882 billion. The figure is arithmetic, not measurement, and the assumption is stated with it: that each acre is one violation, which no court has ever held. The arithmetic is correct anyway, and the bogs are real, and the notifications have not been counted.183

Five point eight to seventeen point three billion gallons of floodwater, one point two million cubic yards of sand a year, and a ten-acre cap.

VII. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the permit itself. Nationwide Permit 34 exists, and it authorizes exactly the activities this investigation describes: the dikes, the berms, the pumps, the water control structures, and the leveling of cranberry beds at existing operations. The Corps did not overlook the cranberry. It wrote a permit for the cranberry, and it set the cap at ten acres, and it required a single pre-construction notification per permit term. The industry is not unpermitted. It is permitted, by number, in advance, for the work it does.16

Its second is the farming exemption. Section 404(f)(1) of the Clean Water Act exempts normal farming, silviculture, and ranching activities, including plowing, seeding, cultivating, and harvesting, from the permit requirement, and the routine flooding and sanding of an established bog is farming, not construction. The bogs are established operations, many of them a century old, and the harvest flood is the harvest, and the sand is the pruning. The permit the Corps wrote is for expansion, enhancement, and modification, not for the ordinary work of the farm.17

Its third is the age of the operation. Commercial cranberry cultivation began in the early nineteenth century, and the Clean Water Act was enacted in 1972. The bogs were flooding and sanding for a century and a half before the permit existed, and the bogs that cannot be flooded are no longer considered profitable, which means the flood is not an addition to the wetland. It is the wetland, worked as a farm, since before the statute. The Act has never once been enforced against a harvest flood.128

Its fourth is the water itself. The water used for the harvest flood is recycled and reused on the farm, pumped from one bog to the next, and the flood is temporary, measured in hours and days, not in permanence. The bog is drained in the spring, and the vines come back to life, and the water returns to the ditches and the ponds it came from. The flood is a loan, not a discharge, and the water is returned.620

Its fifth is the wetland the bog preserves. A law review comment on the Clean Water Act's application to cranberry growers argues that the Act has forced growers to obtain expensive and time-consuming permits, and that cranberry marshes do not destroy the valuable ecological functions of natural wetlands, but protect and preserve them. The bog is a wetland that is farmed, and the farming keeps the wetland a wetland, instead of a subdivision. The growers are, in the comment's words, the burdened stewards of the environment.17

Its sixth is the narrowing. After Sackett, a wetland must have a continuous surface connection to a relatively permanent body of water connected to traditional interstate navigable waters, and most cranberry bogs are perched in sandy ground, drained by ditches, and separated from any such water by dikes and roads. The flood is contained by the dikes the permit authorizes, and the water that leaves the bog leaves through a gate the grower controls, into a ditch the grower dug. The connection the Court requires is the connection the dike prevents.19

Its seventh is the berry's own quality control. The bounce test sorts the crop without an inspector, the four air pockets sort the ripe from the unripe, and the Food and Drug Administration has already fenced the berry's medical claims, allowing only a qualified claim, with the words limited and inconsistent attached to it, and only for juice and supplements, not for sauce. The berry is regulated, the claim is qualified, and the sauce carries no claim at all.714

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VIII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every bog should be registered as a section 404 discharger, and every flood should be counted against the ten-acre cap, and every pre-construction notification should be filed, and filed again, once per operation, once per permit term, and every layer of sand should be weighed, and logged, and reported, and every dike, berm, pump, and water control structure should be numbered, and every gallon of the harvest flood should be metered on the way in and metered on the way out, and every gallon of the winter flood should be metered the same way, and the oxygen in the winter flood should be sampled daily, and the bounce of every berry should be measured against the four-inch standard, and the berries that fail the bounce should be charged as unripe discharges.167

The Corps of Engineers should establish a Cranberry Division, register the bogs, and collect the $2.4 billion a day in unpaid penalties from the 35,300 acres, and the $882 billion for the year, and the fines for the 26,320 acres beyond the cap, which no farm's ten acres can cover, and the fines for the 894.6 million pounds, which is 8,946,000 barrels, at 100 pounds to the barrel, none of which was weighed at the gate. The estates of the 898 farms should be billed. The cooperative's Capper-Volstead exemption is not a permit exemption, and the state fruit designation is not a franking privilege.181

The queue should be cleared in order. The Corps should process the notifications before any further berry is floated, and the bogs should be assigned numbers, and the floods should be scheduled, and the sand should be weighed, and the permits should be issued, and the inspections should be conducted, and the fees should be collected, and the sauce should be labeled by flood, and the juice should be labeled by the percentage it contains, and the water that leaves the bog in the spring should be permitted as a conveyance, since the industry has now exceeded its ten-acre cap by 26,320 acres in the open, on the public record, one bog at a time.1614

The test case is the next Thanksgiving. The berries will float, and the bogs will flood, and the sand will settle, and the sauce will hold the shape of the can, and the permits will not exist in the numbers the arithmetic demands, and the largest permitted-by-number but uncounted-by-acre flooding and filling operation in the United States will operate for another year, in the open, under every Thanksgiving table in the country, one berry at a time.115

Ergo.
