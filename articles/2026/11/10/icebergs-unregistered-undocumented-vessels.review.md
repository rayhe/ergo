# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Icebergs as Unregistered, Undocumented Vessels"

## 1. General Editor: 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: long factual headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from operation to scale to process to discharge to standard to defense to prescription, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly, and the load-bearing beam is the documentation threshold against the undocumented delivery: a five net ton minimum against a fleet whose single most famous unit is reconstructed at about two million tonnes, more than forty times the tonnage of the ship it sank, delivered 491 times a year on average into the busiest shipping lanes on earth. The defense section concedes the real escape hatches (documentation law regulates vessels engaged in trade and requires an owner to file; an iceberg cannot be steered and so cannot be a vessel; it has nowhere to hang a light; the Ice Patrol already works) before overruling them, which is the genre's honesty mechanism, and it is unusually candid in admitting that the system registers the ship and the iceberg has no ship. Minor weakness: Section II carries three distinct counts in sequence, the Greenland calving estimate, the Antarctic giants, and the Ice Patrol time series, which slightly crowds the section, though each earns its place on a distinct metric (how many bergs are born, how large the largest get, and how many reach the lanes).

## 2. Voice Coach: 9.0/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the avalanche and rain pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "The difference between the documented fleet and the undocumented one is not the tonnage. It is the paperwork," "A lookout sees the tip. The tip is the smallest part of the vessel, and it is the only part that ever enters the record," "B-15 was named, measured from space, followed for years, and documented by no one," and "waiting for a form that does not exist." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is the hero image's inspector in a suit and hard hat holding a clipboard beside an unlit berg at night, which lands as straight-faced procedural evidence. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics: 9.0/10
All statutes, regulations, and study results cited are real and quoted or paraphrased accurately where quoted: the federal vessel documentation requirements and the five net ton threshold, the COLREGs definition of vessel and the Rule 23 lighting requirements, the SOLAS automatic identification system carriage rules, the Greenland calving estimate and the one percent that reach the Atlantic, the B-15 and A-76 dimensions, the Ice Patrol season counts and the 491 mean, the ninety percent submerged fraction and the density figures behind it, the Titanic collision time and the 1,517 deaths per the United States inquiry against 1,503 per the British inquiry, the two million tonne reconstruction of the Titanic iceberg, and the 1914 SOLAS adoption by 13 countries. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that an iceberg is a vessel requiring a certificate of documentation, is the disclosed conceit; the piece is honest about the escape hatch, that documentation law requires an owner to file and an iceberg has none, in Section VI, the same way prior pieces disclosed theirs. The two million tonne figure is explicitly framed as a reconstruction estimate, not a measured mass. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability: 9.0/10
"Your iceberg never filed for documentation" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone, and it carries a second hook most Ergo premises lack: the reader already knows the Titanic story, and the piece reframes it as a paperwork failure in which the undocumented party outweighed the documented ship by more than forty to one and then melted before the registry could record it. The five net tons against two million tonnes is the single most shareable image; "Four hundred and ninety-one undocumented vessels a year, on average, drift into the busiest shipping lanes on earth" and "Nine-tenths of the vessel is underwater, and the tenth that shows has never been measured, named, or registered" are strong pull-quote material. The hero image, an inspector with a clipboard beside an unlit berg at night, is built for the thumbnail. High share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy: 8.5/10
The vessel documentation framework is accurately summarized: federal law requires a certificate of documentation with an endorsement for a trade, a certificate may issue only to a wholly United States-owned vessel of at least five net tons not documented under foreign law, the application must be filed by the owner, and the National Vessel Documentation Center administers the registry. The collision regulations definition of vessel and the Rule 23 lighting requirements for a power-driven vessel underway are quoted accurately, and the SOLAS automatic identification system carriage thresholds of 300 gross tonnage on international voyages, 500 gross tonnage otherwise, and all passenger ships regardless of size are stated correctly. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that an iceberg is a vessel, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends any documentation application has a line for a drifting berg. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text.

## 6. Research Rigor: 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: the United States Code and the Code of Federal Regulations for the documentation rules, the collision regulations for the vessel definition and lighting, SOLAS Chapter V for the identification system, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration for the Greenland calving estimate and the three-year drift, the United States National Ice Center via the Australian Antarctic Program for the naming system, the International Ice Patrol's own bulletin and the Canadian National Research Council for the season counts and the 491 mean, the Journal of Ocean Engineering and Technology for the Titanic iceberg reconstruction, and the United States Geological Survey for the ninety percent submerged figure. Minor weakness: the Titanic's 46,328 gross tons is stated without a direct citation in the footnotes, the 1,038 icebergs observed in 1912 comes from a review article rather than the Patrol's own bulletin, and the ninety percent figure is carried by a secondary page citing the Survey rather than a Survey publication itself. The disputed size of the largest ever sighted berg, the 1956 USS Glacier report, is avoided entirely, and the piece uses only the satellite-measured B-15 record instead.

## 7. Data Presentation: 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 15,000 to 30,000 icebergs calved a year in Greenland against 150 to 300 reaching the Atlantic, 295 by 37 kilometres and 11,000 square kilometres for B-15 against 170 by 25 kilometres and 4,320 square kilometres for A-76, 491 a year on average south of 48 degrees north against a maximum of 2,202 in 1984 and zero in 1966 and 2006, 385 in the 2023 ice year against 1,546 in 2014, 917 against 1,025 kilograms per cubic metre for the density ratio behind the ninety percent submerged figure, 50 to 100 feet high and 200 to 400 feet long for the Titanic berg against 46,328 gross tons for the ship, about two million tonnes for the berg, 1,517 dead per the United States inquiry against 1,503 per the British inquiry, 11:40 p.m. against 2:20 a.m. for the collision and the sinking, and five net tons against two million tonnes for the threshold against the delivery. The comparison framework, lawful documentation threshold versus undocumented tonnage on the same scale, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
