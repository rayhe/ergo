A Medium Banana Contains About 450 Milligrams of Potassium, About 0.0117 Percent of Which Is the Radioactive Isotope Potassium-40, Delivering a Dose of About 0.1 Microsieverts, Americans Eat About 27 Pounds of Bananas Per Person Every Year, the United States Imports About 4.7 Million Tons of Them Worth $2.5 Billion, Banana Shipments Set Off the Radiation Detectors at American Ports, and No Banana Has Ever Held a License From the Nuclear Regulatory Commission

The banana is a radioactive source that fits in the palm of a hand. It carries potassium-40, an isotope with a half-life of more than a billion years, and it delivers a dose the Environmental Protection Agency measures at 0.1 microsieverts, in a unit the radiation protection community named after the fruit itself. Americans eat about 27 pounds of bananas per person every year, and the United States imports about 4.7 million tons of them, and the shipments set off the radiation detectors the government installed at the ports to catch smuggled nuclear material. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission licenses the possession, use, and transfer of radioactive material in the country, and it has licensed no banana. This investigation examines the largest unlicensed radioactive material distribution network in the United States, the isotope it carries, the detectors it trips, the law it evades, and the license the record demands.

By Abner Whitfield · November 17, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Fruit

Nearly every banana sold in international trade is a Cavendish. Cavendish cultivars are the source of 99 percent of banana exports. The Cavendish is a sterile triploid, parthenocarpic, which means the fruit forms without pollination and the plant makes no seeds. New plants come only from cuttings of old ones, suckers and tissue culture, so every plantation is a cutting of a cutting. The world grew about 140 million tonnes of bananas in 2023, of which about 19.3 million tonnes crossed a border, and nearly all of the exported fruit was the same clone.12

The Cavendish replaced the Gros Michel in the 1950s and 1960s, after Panama disease destroyed the earlier clone, which was itself genetically identical across the whole trade. The industry tore out one clone and replanted with another, and the trade has now run on a single clone for seventy years. The banana on sale in every American supermarket is, biologically speaking, one plant, copied a hundred billion times a year.12

A medium banana weighs 126 grams and contains about 450 milligrams of potassium. About 0.0117 percent of all natural potassium is potassium-40, a radioactive isotope with a half-life of more than a billion years. About 89 percent of its decays are beta emissions to calcium-40. About 11 percent are electron capture to argon-40, accompanied by a gamma ray at 1.46 megaelectronvolts. One banana carries about 15 becquerels of activity, fifteen atoms decaying every second.34

The banana is not the most radioactive food. The Environmental Protection Agency's RadTown tables put bananas at 3,520 picocuries of potassium-40 per kilogram, while raw lima beans carry 4,640 and Brazil nuts carry 5,600, plus radium. But the banana is the most widely eaten of them. It is the most popular fresh fruit in the United States by weight, and the only radioactive source in the country that is sold by the bunch, in the produce aisle, to children.514

One plant, copied a hundred billion times a year, and every copy is radioactive.

II. The Dose

The Environmental Protection Agency puts the dose from eating one banana at 0.01 millirem, or 0.1 microsieverts. The figure has a name: the banana equivalent dose. The unit was popularized in 1995 by a scientist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, as a teaching tool for explaining tiny doses to the public. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission itself calls the banana equivalent dose a fake unit, informal, about 0.1 microsieverts, useful for comparison and not for dosimetry.3567

The Agency offers its own comparison. About 100 bananas equal the dose a person in the United States receives each day from natural environmental sources. The Commission puts average annual background radiation at 310 millirem, about 8.5 microsieverts a day. A chest X-ray is about 100 microsieverts, or 1,000 bananas. A banana a day for a year is about 36 microsieverts on paper.367

The comparison runs in the other direction as well. The body already carries the isotope. The Health Physics Society estimates about 4,200 becquerels of potassium-40 in a 70 kilogram person, and an instrument designer who spent a career building contamination monitors put the figure at about 4,400 decays per second. One banana carries about one three-hundredth of the potassium-40 already inside the person eating it. The eater is more radioactive than the eaten.3810

The dose is a calculated yardstick, not a detector reading. The body regulates its own potassium and excretes the excess in urine almost immediately, so eating bananas does not build up potassium-40 in the eater. The banana equivalent dose is arithmetic applied to a fruit. The arithmetic is correct, and the fruit is radioactive, and the two facts sit side by side in the record without reconciling.5

One hundred bananas equal one ordinary day.

III. The History

The detectors came first, and the bananas tripped them. After 2001, United States Customs and Border Protection installed hundreds of radiation portal monitors at seaports, land border ports of entry, and crossings across the country, to catch nuclear material that might be smuggled in for a weapon or a dirty bomb. The devices had to be set sensitive enough to catch uranium-235 and plutonium-239, which give off very little radiation. At that sensitivity, naturally occurring radioactive material sets them off.9

In 2006, the Government Accountability Office tested the detectors and reported that they frequently missed, or failed to correctly identify, potentially dangerous materials, with false negative rates from about 45 percent to slightly more than 80 percent across the vendors tested. The machines that could not reliably find the dangerous material had no trouble finding the fruit.9

In 2007, the chairman of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, Dale E. Klein, visited the Port of Seattle and toured the radiation detectors operated by Customs and Border Protection. While he was there, one cargo container triggered the alarms. It was a shipment of Chinese fireworks, and isotopic analysis showed the culprit was potassium-40. The agents told him about one particular port that receives nothing but bananas, and that virtually every shipment sets off the detectors.9

The pattern was older than the port program. In the 1980s, a monitor installed on a mill's incoming train track spur in South Africa gave random false alarms, even when the spur track was empty. The shipping manifests, secured under threat of subpoena, showed the cause in all five identifiable cases: freight boxcars full of bananas on the main line, about 100 feet away. The detector could not be relocated, so the customer built a special concrete block wall to shield the monitor from the main line track.10

The same problem appeared inside the nuclear plants. Walk-through portal monitors, used by workers exiting at the end of a shift, often alarmed on workers who had a banana with their lunch. At some plants, workers milked the system for overtime pay for the delays caused by frisking with other instruments to determine whether the radiation was from potassium-40 or from real contamination. The banana was the only source in the building that arrived in a lunchbox.10

The banana has since become a teaching instrument. A physics lesson at the University of California, Davis, hands students the gamma spectrum of a banana shipment that set off a customs monitor, and asks them to identify the sources in it. The spectrum shows the potassium-40 peaks of the fruit, and beside them, in the exercise, the peaks of cesium-137 hidden in the same container. The student's job is to tell the banana from the bomb. The banana is the calibration standard and the cover story at once.11

Virtually every shipment sets off the detectors.

IV. The Law

Section 81 of the Atomic Energy Act provides that no person may transfer or receive in interstate commerce, manufacture, produce, transfer, acquire, own, possess, import, or export any byproduct material except as authorized by a license issued by the Commission. The Commission's regulations carry the requirement into Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Section 30.3 states it plainly: except as otherwise authorized, no person shall receive, possess, use, transfer, own, or acquire byproduct material except as authorized in a specific or general license.1516

The regulations provide for small quantities. Section 30.18 allows a person to receive, possess, use, and transfer exempt quantities of byproduct material without a license, in the amounts listed in Schedule B. But the exemption has a limit, and the limit is written into the same section. Section 30.18(d) prohibits a person from commercially distributing exempt quantities of byproduct material, knowing or having reason to believe that such quantities will be transferred to persons exempt from licensing, except in accordance with a license issued under Section 32.18. The Commission said so in writing in 1996, in a letter explaining that the exemption belongs to the recipient, not the distributor, and that a company that commercially distributes exempt quantities must hold a distribution license.15

The regulations provide for carriers as well. Section 30.13 exempts carriers, freight forwarders, and the United States Postal Service from the licensing requirements, but only to the extent that the transport or storage of byproduct material occurs in the regular course of carriage for another, or storage incident thereto. The exemption is valid only during transportation, and only for the entities that provide the transportation. It covers the truck. It does not cover the grocer.15

The Department of Transportation draws its own line. Section 173.403 of Title 49 defines radioactive material, for transport, as material containing radionuclides where both the activity concentration and the total activity in the consignment exceed the values in the table in Section 173.436. For potassium-40, the exempt concentration is 100 becquerels per gram, and the exempt consignment activity is 1,000,000 becquerels. The banana carries about 0.13 becquerels per gram. A standard refrigerated container holding about 20 metric tons of bananas carries about 2.6 million becquerels in total. One prong of the definition is met, and the other fails by a factor of about 770. The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration confirmed in a 2019 interpretation that a consignment below the table values is not a Class 7 radioactive material.1718

The law, read as written, licenses the distributor and exempts the recipient, licenses the possessor and exempts the carrier, and measures the shipment twice and finds it radioactive once. The banana is distributed commercially, in exempt quantities, to persons exempt from licensing, by distributors that hold no distribution license. The only license in the whole chain is the one nobody holds.15

V. The Shipment

The United States grows virtually no bananas. In 2024, local production in North America was 3.6 thousand tons, against imports of 5.3 million tons. The largest suppliers of the American banana are Guatemala, Ecuador, and Costa Rica. Virtually every banana in the American market is an import, and every import crosses a border, and every border crossing passes a radiation detector.1219

In 2024, the United States imported about 4.7 million tons of bananas, worth about $2.5 billion, and consumed about 4.1 million tons. The fruit arrives by refrigerated ship and truck, in 40 foot reefers, as food, not as radioactive material. No shipping paper lists a radionuclide. No placard is displayed. No trained hazmat employee certifies the load. The manifest says bananas.12

Every container passes a radiation portal monitor at the port. The monitors are set sensitive enough to catch the bomb materials, which emit little, so they catch the fruit, which emits more. The customs officers are trained to recognize the false positives. Potassium-rich cargo, fertilizer, cat litter, ceramic tile, and bananas, all trigger the same instruments, and the officers clear them, and the shipments move on, and the detectors sound again on the next ship.920

The shipment is a commercial distribution of exempt quantities of radioactive material to persons exempt from licensing, carried out by distributors that hold no distribution license, in the regular course of business, at a scale no licensed distributor has ever approached. The banana is the largest commercial distribution of radioactive material in the country, and the only one that has never filed a form.15

The manifest says bananas. The detector says otherwise.

VI. The Scale

Do the arithmetic the record permits. Americans have about 27 pounds of fresh bananas available per person every year, more than any other fresh fruit, and 96 percent of American households buy bananas at least once a month. More than 100 billion bananas are eaten worldwide every year.1314

The import is 4.7 million metric tons, which is 10.4 billion pounds. At about 0.28 pounds per banana, that is roughly 37 billion bananas a year, about 102 million a day, about 1,180 a second, every second, all year. The arithmetic does not pause for the detectors.123

The activity follows the count. Thirty-seven billion bananas at 15 becquerels each is about 560 billion becquerels, about 15 curies of potassium-40 a year, delivered to the ports of the United States in refrigerated containers, under no license, with no survey, and no filing. The year's import carries about 127 million times the potassium-40 in a single human body.34

The dose follows the activity. Thirty-seven billion bananas at 0.1 microsieverts each is about 3,700 sieverts of committed effective dose, summed banana by banana. The figure is arithmetic, not a measurement, and the body excretes the potassium, and the dose does not accumulate, and the arithmetic is correct anyway.35

The container follows the shipment. A single refrigerated container of about 20 metric tons carries about 2.6 million becquerels of potassium-40, more than two and a half times the exempt consignment activity of 1,000,000 becquerels, at a concentration of about 0.13 becquerels per gram, about 770 times below the exempt concentration of 100 becquerels per gram. The shipment exceeds one limit and satisfies the other, and the regulation measures both, and the banana passes one test and fails the other, and the container rolls on.517

About 1,180 bananas a second, every second, all year.

VII. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the definition. Potassium-40 is naturally occurring radioactive material. The Atomic Energy Act gives the Commission authority over byproduct material, source material, and special nuclear material. Potassium-40 is none of the three. It is regulated by the states, if it is regulated at all, and the Commission has no jurisdiction over the banana by definition. Potassium-40 does not even appear in Schedule B, the table of exempt quantities. The schedule lists potassium-42 and potassium-43. And Section 32.18, the distribution license the prescription demands, forbids licensing byproduct material contained in any food, beverage, cosmetic, drug, or other commodity designed for ingestion by a human being, and requires that the material be used for its radioactive properties. The banana is food, and its radioactivity is not the point of it.151621

Its second is the dose. The Environmental Protection Agency says these foods do not pose a radiation risk. One hundred bananas equal one day of natural background. The Commission's own background figure is 310 millirem a year. The banana's contribution is a rounding error on a rounding error.35

Its third is the body. Potassium is regulated by homeostasis. The excess is excreted in urine almost immediately. The banana equivalent dose is a calculated yardstick, not a dose that accumulates, and eating more bananas does not make a person more radioactive. The eater is the control group and the experiment at once.5

Its fourth is the threshold. The Department of Transportation's exempt concentration for potassium-40 is 100 becquerels per gram. The banana carries about 0.13. The shipment is not a Class 7 radioactive material, and the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration has said so in writing. The consignment arithmetic fails the concentration prong, and the concentration prong is half the definition.1718

Its fifth is the detector. The detectors work. They detect the potassium-40, identify it by its gamma spectrum, and clear the shipment. The alarm is the system functioning, not the system failing. The banana is the most common false positive in the system because the system was built to detect exactly the signal the banana emits.911

Its sixth is the food. The banana is food, regulated as food by the Food and Drug Administration. The packers register as food facilities. The system that governs the banana is the food system, and it governs it completely, from the plantation to the produce aisle.12

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VIII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every banana should hold a specific license under Part 30 of Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations, authorizing the receipt, possession, use, and transfer of its potassium-40. Every distributor should hold a distribution license under Section 32.18, authorizing the commercial distribution of exempt quantities to persons exempt from licensing. Every shipment should travel as a Class 7 radioactive material, with placards, shipping papers, and a certified hazmat employee, in Type A packaging, surveyed at the port and filed with the Commission. The 37 billion bananas should be licensed individually, by the bunch, or by the container, and the potassium-40 in every American body should be reported as a possessed quantity, since the eater is the licensee of record.151617

The queue should be cleared in order. The Commission should process the 37 billion licenses before any further banana is eaten, and the detectors should be recalibrated to the banana, and the manifests should list the radionuclide, and the grocers should post the license, and the lunchboxes should be surveyed on exit.10

The test case is the next year. Four point seven million tons will arrive, 37 billion bananas, 15 curies, 3,700 sieverts, and the detectors will sound, and the manifests will say bananas, and the licenses will not exist, and the fruit will be eaten, and the potassium will be excreted, and the dose will not accumulate, and the largest unlicensed radioactive material distribution network in the United States will operate for another year, in the open, in the produce aisle, one bunch at a time.1213

Ergo.
