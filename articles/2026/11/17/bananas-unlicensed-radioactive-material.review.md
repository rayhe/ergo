# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Bananas as Unlicensed Radioactive Material"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The piece follows the Ergo template faithfully and holds together as a systematic case: an absurdly exhaustive headline and a deck that restates the whole indictment, eight Roman-numeral sections escalating cleanly from the fruit itself, through dose and detector history, into the regulatory text, the shipment, the scale arithmetic, a genuinely conceded defense, and an impossible prescription ending in "Ergo." The escalation works because each section adds a new load-bearing fact, the clone, the 0.1-microsievert unit named after the fruit, the port alarms, the 10 CFR 30.18(d) distribution limit, the 37-billion-banana count, rather than merely restating the joke. The five pullquotes are well placed and mostly sharp, and the footnotes and Sources section are complete and properly linked. The main drag is repetition in the middle: Sections IV and V make the same commercial-distribution argument twice, and the headline, while in-format, buries its punchline, "No banana has ever held a license," at the very end of a very long sentence.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0/10
The voice holds the Ergo register almost throughout: flat, procedural, and relentlessly literal, with the humor carried entirely by the precision of the measurements and the legal citations rather than by any aside or wink. The cadence is the article's strength, with short declarative sentences and parallel constructions, "It covers the truck. It does not cover the grocer," doing the satirical work that adjectives never do. The few slips are small but audible: "workers milked the system for overtime pay" leans colloquial, and the closing test-case paragraph restates the headline arithmetic in a long polysyndetic chain instead of landing a fresh image the way the turkeys piece does with its unfiled petition. Still, the final "Ergo." lands because the preceding prescription has earned its flatness, and the piece never once steps out of character to admire its own joke.

## 3. Ethics — 8.5/10
Spot-checked claims against their sources: the GAO 2006 false-negative rates (45 percent to slightly more than 80 percent) are verbatim from GAO-07-133R; Chairman Klein's 2007 Port of Seattle anecdote (Chinese fireworks, potassium-40, "virtually every shipment sets off the detectors") is verbatim from NRC speech S-07-029; the EPA RadTown figures (bananas 3,520, lima beans 4,640, Brazil nuts 5,600 pCi/kg) check out; the potassium-40 facts (0.0117 percent, about 15 Bq per banana, 0.1 microsievert banana equivalent dose, 1995 LLNL origin) and the 49 CFR 173.436 thresholds (100 Bq/g, 1,000,000 Bq) are all correct, and the scale arithmetic (37 billion bananas, about 15 curies, 3,700 sieverts summed, about 1,180 bananas a second) is internally consistent. The defense section is the piece's ethical strong point: it fairly presents the dispositive escape hatches, above all that potassium-40 is naturally occurring radioactive material rather than byproduct, source, or special nuclear material, so the Atomic Energy Act does not apply at all, plus the DOT both-prongs test, homeostasis, and FDA food jurisdiction, before the disclosed "overruled" joke. Two real factual problems were found and fixed before publication: the chest X-ray comparison understated the standard figure fivefold (corrected to the NRC's 0.1 mSv, or 1,000 bananas), and "every banana in the American market is an import" was contradicted by the article's own footnote recording 3.6 thousand tons of North American production (softened to "virtually every"). Minor nits: several load-bearing footnotes point to content-farm pages rather than primary sources, though the facts themselves verified, and the claim that plant workers "milked the system for overtime pay" rests on a single first-person anecdote. Nothing is invented or misattributed in substance, the deliberately wrong conclusion is honestly fenced by a complete defense, and the piece serves the educational mission well.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.5/10
The premise is instantly graspable in one sentence, your banana is radioactive, it sets off port radiation detectors, and no banana has ever held a license, and it lands harder than the cicadas or turkey premises because nearly everyone eats the subject. The piece is rich in quotable, tweet-length lines and surprising-but-true facts that travel well, from the banana equivalent dose to a chest X-ray equaling 1,000 bananas to 1,180 bananas a second. Concrete anchors help too: the NRC chairman watching a shipment trip the Port of Seattle detectors, nuclear-plant workers setting off portal monitors with lunchbox bananas, and a concrete wall built in South Africa to shield a detector from a passing banana train. The headline, however, fails as a social hook: at roughly 70 words it cannot be quoted, posted, or remembered, so all sharing work falls to the deck and pullquotes instead. Still very high share potential, docked mainly for a headline that buries its own punchline.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
Every citation checked against the primary sources holds up: Section 30.3(a), Section 30.18(a) and (d), and Section 30.13 are quoted or paraphrased essentially verbatim; the December 26, 1996 NRC letter to Spectrum Techniques says exactly what the article claims about carriers versus commercial redistributors; the Section 173.436 table does list potassium-40 at 100 Bq/g and 1,000,000 Bq; Section 173.403 does require both the activity concentration and the total consignment activity to exceed the table values; and PHMSA Interpretation 18-0088 (February 15, 2019) does confirm that a consignment below both values is not a Class 7 radioactive material. The article is honest about the decisive escape hatches: Section VII correctly states that potassium-40 is naturally occurring radioactive material, that it fits none of the Atomic Energy Act Section 11(e) byproduct-material categories, that NRC jurisdiction therefore does not attach, and that the DOT definition fails on the concentration prong. Three legal gaps were found and fixed before publication: the Atomic Energy Act Section 81 paraphrase dropped the statute's "in interstate commerce" qualifier and inserted "use" (corrected to the statutory wording); Schedule B lists potassium-42 and potassium-43 but not potassium-40, so the Section 30.18 discussion proceeded as if a listed exempt quantity for banana potassium-40 existed (the defense now states the Schedule B point expressly); and the prescription's Section 32.18 distribution license could never issue for a banana anyway, since Section 32.18(b) forbids licensing byproduct material contained in food designed for ingestion and Section 32.18(c) requires the material be used for its radioactive properties (the defense now states both). None of these changes the verdict, because the deliberately wrong conclusion, that 37 billion bananas require licenses, is the genre's disclosed conceit, stated only after the article itself has laid out the law that defeats it, and the "defense rests, it is overruled" turn is the joke rather than a misstatement of law.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10
All five spot-checked claims check out in substance: the banana equivalent dose is 0.1 microsieverts (0.01 millirem), the Klein Port of Seattle anecdote matches the NRC's own 2007 speech verbatim, the South Africa boxcar story matches Stanley Pitman's first-person EDN account including the overtime detail, US 2024 banana imports are about 4.7 million tons, and Cavendish accounts for about 99 percent of banana exports. The sourcing behind those true claims is nevertheless weak for a load-bearing investigation, with the central EPA dose figures routed through obscure blogs (pippasworld, wonderade, miniwebtool, scienceinsights) rather than EPA or NRC pages, the Klein speech cited via Club Troppo instead of the NRC PDF, and the 99 percent figure cited via the dailydoseinfo newsletter rather than the 2018 Frontiers in Plant Science review itself. The import value is also shakier than presented, as IndexBox's $2.5 billion conflicts with UN Comtrade-derived figures of about $2.83 billion for the same 4.73 million tonnes, with no explanation of the discrepancy. One detail went beyond its cited source and was fixed before publication: the article said the South Africa concrete wall stands to this day, which Pitman's account does not state. No load-bearing claim proved false.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
All of the listed arithmetic recomputes correctly within rounding: 4.7 million metric tons is 10.36 billion pounds, about 37.0 billion bananas at 0.28 pounds each; 37 billion at 15 becquerels each is 555 billion becquerels, about 560 billion and exactly 15.0 curies; 37 billion at 0.1 microsieverts each is exactly 3,700 sieverts; 20 metric tons at 130 becquerels per kilogram is 2.6 million becquerels, 2.6 times the 1,000,000 becquerel limit; 0.13 becquerels per gram is a factor of 769 below 100, correctly rounded to about 770; 15 becquerels is one 293rd of 4,400, fairly stated as about one three-hundredth; and 3,520 picocuries per kilogram converts to 130.2 becquerels per kilogram. Precision is consistent at two to three significant figures, every key figure is hedged with "about" and footnoted, and the summed 3,700 sievert figure is explicitly flagged as arithmetic rather than a measurement, with the article itself noting the dose does not accumulate. The only nits are rounding propagation, since the unrounded 37.0 billion bananas give 101.4 million a day and 1,173 a second rather than the stated 102 million and 1,180, a discrepancy under one percent that disappears if the rounded 10.4 billion pound intermediate is used; a small internal tension, since a 126 gram banana at 130 becquerels per kilogram implies 16.4 becquerels rather than the stated 15, against 14.1 becquerels from the potassium content; and a unit ambiguity, since the headline and deck say "tons" while Section VI says "metric tons" and the conversion assumes metric, with short tons yielding only 33.6 billion bananas. None of these is a material error, and the 127 million times body figure also checks out at 560 billion divided by 4,400 becquerels.
