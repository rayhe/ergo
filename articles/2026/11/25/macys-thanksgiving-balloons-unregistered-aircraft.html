Aviation Law & Thanksgiving

Macy's Flies 100 Balloons Over Manhattan Every Thanksgiving, the Balloons Stand as Tall as Six Stories and Stretch 78 Feet Long, Each One Holds 12,000 Cubic Feet of Helium, the Federal Aviation Administration Defines a Balloon as a Lighter-Than-Air Aircraft, a Balloon That Struck a Lamppost in 1997 Put a Woman in a Coma for 24 Days, and No Balloon Has Ever Held an N-Number

The balloon is an aircraft that fits on no registry. It is built in a factory in New Jersey, inflated with 12,000 cubic feet of helium, walked down a 2.5-mile route through Manhattan by crews of handlers, and flown over three and a half million spectators, and it does all of it without a registration number, without an airworthiness certificate, and without a certificated pilot at the controls, and it has done so for 96 parades. The United States has spent a century building a system that registers every aircraft, certifies every airframe, and licenses every pilot. It has never registered the balloon. This investigation examines the largest unregistered aircraft fleet in the United States, the balloons that fly it, the crowds they fly over, the law they evade, and the registration the record demands.

By Cornelius Vane · November 25, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Balloon

The parade began without a single aircraft in it. The first Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade marched in 1924, and it featured live animals borrowed from the Central Park Zoo, including lions and camels, walked down the street by store employees. The animals frightened the children. In 1927, the organizers replaced the live animals with oversized balloons, and the first of them was Felix the Cat, the first character balloon, designed by the puppeteer Tony Sarg with technical support from the Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company. Felix stood 21 feet tall, and he was filled with ordinary air and held aloft on wooden poles carried by handlers, because helium did not arrive until the following year.12

The balloon has grown since Felix, and the growth is the whole of the case. The balloons now range from 30 to 70 feet tall, and the largest of them stretch further still: a Kermit the Frog balloon measured 78 feet long, 61 feet high, and 36 feet wide. Each giant balloon takes about 12,000 cubic feet of helium to inflate, enough gas to lift about 746 pounds, and it takes about 90 minutes to inflate a giant balloon and about 15 minutes to deflate one. The average balloon flies for about eight years before it is retired. The balloons are made of nylon cloth with a urethane coating, and the pieces are melted together with heat to make an airtight seal, and they are built in pieces so they can be packed down to 12.5 feet high and 8 feet wide, to fit through the Lincoln Tunnel on the way from the Macy's Parade Studio in New Jersey to Manhattan.34

The balloon is the parade, and the parade is the balloon. The 2026 parade, the 100th, flies a record 100 inflatable elements, including 26 giant character balloons and five balloonicles, along with 26 floats, 10 marching bands, 1,000 clowns, and more than 11,000 participants and volunteers. Nine balloons fly for the first time this year, including Bluey, Hello Kitty, Sorcerer's Apprentice Mickey, Joe Cool and Woodstock, and Underdog. The parade was suspended only once, from 1942 through 1944, for the Second World War, and it has flown balloons in every other year since 1927.523

The balloon is old, and the record of it is old. The first balloons were filled with air and carried on poles. Helium replaced the air in 1928, and the balloons began to float on their own, and the handlers began to hold ropes instead of poles. In 1958, in response to a national helium shortage, the organizers filled the balloons with ordinary air again and hung them from giant cranes, and the parade went on. The balloon has flown on helium, on air, and on cranes, and it has never once flown on a certificate.26

The balloon is an aircraft that fits on no registry.

II. The Flight

The flight is a scheduled operation, and the schedule is published. The parade steps off at 8:30 in the morning on Thanksgiving Day, from West 77th Street and Central Park West, beside the American Museum of Natural History. It travels south along Central Park West to Columbus Circle, turns east onto Central Park South, turns south onto Sixth Avenue through Midtown, and turns west on 34th Street to finish at Macy's Herald Square, at West 34th Street and Seventh Avenue, at around noon. The route is 2.5 miles long, and the flight takes about three and a half hours.5

The flight is flown over a crowd, and the crowd is the largest in the country. About three and a half million people line the route, and the first parade drew about 250,000. The telecast drew 34.3 million viewers in 2025, the largest television audience the parade has ever had. One hundred aircraft fly over three and a half million people, which is 35,000 people per aircraft, and the aircraft fly low, at heights the city's wind monitors set block by block, over a crowd that stands behind barricades and looks up.57

The flight is governed by wind, and the wind has a number. After the 1997 parade, New York City set firm rules: the balloons are grounded if sustained winds exceed 23 miles per hour or gusts exceed 34 miles per hour. There are wind monitors at intersections along the route, and a wind monitor with each balloon, and a police officer with each balloon, and officials can order the balloons flown lower as the wind rises, or grounded entirely if the gusts cross the line. The balloons have never been grounded since the rules were written, but the rules are read aloud every year.8

The flight begins the day before, in the open, on the street. The balloon inflation runs on Wednesday afternoon, from 1 p.m. to 6 p.m., near the American Museum of Natural History, and the public watches the balloons fill. The inflation is the takeoff roll, and the deflation at Herald Square is the landing, and between them the fleet flies 2.5 miles through the largest city in the country, over a crowd of millions, with no flight plan filed in the public record, no tower clearance, and no tail number on any envelope.5

Three and a half million people stand under 100 aircraft, and none of them has a tail number.

III. The Law

The law of the aircraft is written against exactly this kind of operator, and it begins with a definition that leaves no room for a balloon. Section 1.1 of Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations provides that an aircraft is a device that is used or intended to be used for flight in the air, and it provides further that a balloon is a lighter-than-air aircraft that is not engine driven and that sustains flight through the use of either gas buoyancy or an airborne heater. The parade balloon is a device, it is used for flight in the air, it is lighter than air, it is not engine driven, and it sustains flight through gas buoyancy. It meets the definition in every element. The statute at 49 U.S.C. 40102(a)(6) says the same thing in plainer terms: an aircraft is any contrivance invented, used, or designed to navigate, or fly in, the air.9

The registration requirement follows the definition, and it admits of no exception for a parade. Section 44101(a) of Title 49 provides that a person may operate an aircraft only when the aircraft is registered under section 44103. The exceptions are three: aircraft operated under specific statutory authorizations, aircraft of the national defense forces, and aircraft operated for a reasonable period after a transfer of ownership. No parade balloon is a military aircraft, no parade balloon operates under a statutory authorization, and no parade balloon was recently sold. The regulation at 14 CFR 47.3 provides that no person may operate an aircraft in the United States unless it has been registered, and registration is evidenced by the issuance of an N-number, the alphanumeric mark the aircraft must display under Part 45. No parade balloon has ever been issued an N-number, and no parade balloon displays a registration mark.1011

The airworthiness requirement follows the registration requirement, and it is the more embarrassing of the two. Section 91.203(a) of Title 14 provides that no person may operate a civil aircraft unless it has within it an appropriate and current airworthiness certificate and an effective United States registration certificate, and paragraph (b) provides that the airworthiness certificate must be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance so that it is legible to passengers or crew. The parade balloon has no cabin, no cockpit, and no entrance, and so it has nowhere to display the certificate it does not hold. Airworthiness begins with type certification under Part 21: the design is shown to meet the airworthiness standards, the agency issues a type certificate, each airframe is shown to conform, and the system continues through annual inspections under section 91.409 and airworthiness directives as unsafe conditions emerge. No balloon has ever held a standard airworthiness certificate, and no balloon has ever been inspected under section 91.409.12

The pilot requirement follows the airworthiness requirement, and it is the most embarrassing of the three. Section 61.3 of Title 14 provides that no person may serve as a required pilot flight crewmember of a civil aircraft unless that person holds a pilot certificate issued under Part 61. The parade balloon is flown by crews of handlers holding ropes, and the handlers are volunteers, and after the 2005 incident it was reported that the handlers had received all of their training from a six-page pamphlet and had not been checked for physical fitness. No handler has ever held a balloon pilot certificate, and no handler has ever been required to hold one, and the person at the end of each rope is, in the eyes of the regulation, the pilot in command of an aircraft.1314

The penalty for operating without the registration is set by statute, and it has grown with inflation. Section 46301 of Title 49 provides civil penalties of up to $75,000 per violation for violations of the registration requirements, and section 46306 makes it a criminal offense, punishable by up to three years of imprisonment, to knowingly operate an unregistered aircraft. Registration itself costs five dollars. The entire fleet of 100 balloons could be registered for $500, and the fee has never been paid.1115

The statute defines an aircraft as any contrivance invented, used, or designed to navigate, or fly in, the air.

IV. The Moored Balloon Rules

The Federal Aviation Administration has written a second body of law for the balloon, and it is the only body of law that mentions the balloon by name. Part 101 of Title 14 governs moored balloons, kites, amateur rockets, and unmanned free balloons, and Subpart B governs moored balloons and kites. Section 101.13 provides that no person may operate a moored balloon less than 500 feet from the base of any cloud, more than 500 feet above the surface of the earth, from an area where the ground visibility is less than three miles, or within five miles of the boundary of any airport. Section 101.15 provides that no person may operate an unshielded moored balloon more than 150 feet above the surface unless, at least 24 hours before beginning the operation, he gives the names and addresses of the owners and operators, the size of the balloon, the location, and the height to the nearest air traffic control facility. Section 101.17 requires lighting and marking, including colored pennants or streamers on the mooring lines at intervals of not more than 50 feet, visible for at least one mile. Section 101.19 requires a device that will automatically and rapidly deflate the balloon if it escapes from its moorings. Section 101.7 forbids operating a moored balloon in a manner that creates a hazard to other persons or property.16

The rules apply to the parade on their face, and the parade complies with them by accident. The balloons fly below 150 feet, so no 24-hour notice to air traffic control is required, and no notice has ever been given. The route's final mile runs within five miles of the East 34th Street heliport, which is an airport under the regulation's definition, and the balloons fly through that five-mile circle every year. The mooring lines carry no colored pennants or streamers at 50-foot intervals, because the lines are held by handlers and not left to hang. No balloon in the parade is documented as carrying an automatic rapid deflation device, and the only rapid deflation in the parade's history was performed by hand, by officers of the New York City Police Department, with knives, on the Barney and Pink Panther balloons in 1997.1617

The exception is the whole of the defense, and it is one paragraph long. Section 101.13(b) provides that the operating limitations do not apply to the operation of a balloon below the top of any structure and within 250 feet of it, if that shielded operation does not obscure any lighting on the structure. The parade route is a canyon of buildings, and the balloons fly below the tops of those buildings and within 250 feet of them, and so the limitations that would otherwise govern the flight do not apply. The balloons are shielded by the skyline, and the skyline is the exemption.16

The regulation requires a device that will automatically and rapidly deflate the balloon if it escapes from its moorings.

V. The Collision Record

The collision record begins in the air, five thousand feet above Queens. In the early years of the parade, the balloons were not deflated at the end of the route. They were released into the sky, with address labels sewn into the fabric, and anyone who found one and returned it to Macy's received a $100 gift certificate. In 1931, a blue hippopotamus balloon nearly struck the Empire State Building, floated over Brooklyn to the Atlantic Ocean, and was never recovered, and Macy's multiplied the reward by four and broadcast the description over the radio. In 1932, a 60-foot Tom Cat balloon, released after the parade, floated into the path of a small plane over Jamaica, Queens, at about 5,000 feet. The pilot was Annette Gibson, a 22-year-old aviation student, flying with her instructor, Hugh Copeland. Gibson had tried five times to snag the balloon's tether with her wing, and on the sixth attempt the balloon wrapped itself around the plane's left wing, and the plane plunged 5,000 feet toward the ground. Gibson nearly fell out of the open cockpit door and was saved only when her foot caught in her seatbelt, and Copeland regained control about 80 feet above the ground and landed at Floyd Bennett Field. An investigation by the Aeronautics Branch of the Department of Commerce found that Gibson and Copeland had violated federal rules forbidding stunting over densely populated areas. Macy's announced the following year that the balloons would no longer be released, to make the country safe for aviators.1819

The collision record continues on the ground, at the lampposts. In 1993, a Sonic the Hedgehog balloon collided with a lamppost and tore open. In 1997, under high winds with gusts over 40 miles per hour, the six-story-tall Cat in the Hat balloon struck the arm of a lamppost at 72nd Street and Central Park West twice, and the arm fell into the crowd, injuring four people. One of them, Kathleen Caronna, 33 years old, suffered a skull fracture and spent 24 days in a coma. She filed a $395 million lawsuit against Macy's, New York City, and the lamppost's manufacturer, and settled in 2001 for an undisclosed sum. The same day, the Barney the Dinosaur and Pink Panther balloons were damaged by the wind, and officers of the New York City Police Department forcibly deflated them, stabbing and stomping the envelopes to bring them down. The parade that year flew 17 giant balloons and 18 novelty balloons, and several of them lost limbs or were otherwise damaged before the route was done.1720

The collision record continues in Times Square. In 2005, in winds of 15 to 20 miles per hour, a handling line from the M&M's balloon snagged a streetlight in Times Square, and the light fixture fell into the crowd, striking two sisters, aged 11 and 26. The younger sister suffered a chipped tooth and a cut on the back of her head that required nine stitches. The older sister, who uses a wheelchair, suffered a bruised forehead and was given a CAT scan. Both were treated at the hospital and released the same day. The family did not sue, and the case was settled amicably with Macy's, reportedly including a lifetime supply of M&M's. The broadcast, meanwhile, showed footage from the previous year's parade, in which the same balloon had injured no one.1321

The collision record is the enforcement record, and the enforcement record is empty. In 96 parades that have flown balloons, the Federal Aviation Administration has never registered a parade balloon, never certificated one, never licensed a handler, never grounded the fleet, and never invoked the civil penalty for operating an unregistered aircraft. The only federal finding in the file is the 1932 Aeronautics Branch determination, and it blames the pilot, not the balloon. The only deflations ordered by a government authority were performed by police officers with knives.1917

The only federal finding in the file blames the pilot, not the balloon.

VI. The Helium

The helium is the fuel, and the fuel is the scale. Each giant balloon takes about 12,000 cubic feet of helium, and the 2026 parade flies 26 giant character balloons, which is 312,000 cubic feet for the character balloons alone. The parade as a whole consumes roughly 300,000 to 400,000 cubic feet of helium each year, counting the novelty balloons and balloonicles. As of 2015, Macy's was the second-largest consumer of helium in the world, outnumbered only by the United States government. The gas is non-renewable, and the federal government maintains a reserve of it, and the department store that sells perfume and handbags draws on the national supply at a rate second only to the state.3224

The helium is also the lift, and the lift is measurable. Twelve thousand cubic feet of helium lifts about 746 pounds, and 26 giant balloons lift nearly 19,400 pounds between them, which is about nine and a half tons of cartoon characters, held aloft over a crowd by gas and rope. The lift is what makes the balloon an aircraft, and the lift is what makes the balloon a sail, and the wind that fills the sail is what pushes the balloon into the lampposts.3

The helium is not, strictly speaking, necessary. In 1958, in response to a national helium shortage, the organizers filled the balloons with ordinary air and hung them from giant cranes, and the parade went on, and the crowd watched, and the balloons flew, or were carried, down the route. The operation can proceed without any lighter-than-air gas at all, which means the helium is a choice, and the choice is made every year, in the hundreds of thousands of cubic feet, by a retailer, from a finite national reserve, for a three-and-a-half-hour flight over a crowd.6

Macy's is the second-largest consumer of helium in the world, outnumbered only by the United States government.

VII. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the regulation itself. Part 101 is the balloon's own regime, written by the Federal Aviation Administration for moored balloons, kites, amateur rockets, and unmanned free balloons, and it governs the parade balloons precisely because they are not Part 91 aircraft. The agency has never required a moored balloon to be registered, and it has never required a moored balloon to hold an airworthiness certificate, and the absence of both is not an oversight. It is the design of the rule.16

Its second is the altitude. The balloons fly below 150 feet, so no notice to air traffic control is required under section 101.15, and the operating limitations of section 101.13 do not apply to a balloon flown below the top of any structure and within 250 feet of it. The parade route is lined with buildings taller than the balloons, and the balloons fly in their shadow, and the shadow is the exemption.16

Its third is the city. New York City runs its own airworthiness system for the fleet: a size cap on the balloons, a wind limit of 23 miles per hour sustained and 34 miles per hour in gusts, anchor vehicles, wind monitors at the intersections, a wind monitor and a police officer with each balloon, and lamppost arms rotated or removed along the route so the ropes have nothing to catch. The municipal regime is stricter than the federal one, and it has held since 1997.8

Its fourth is the lifespan of the fleet. The balloons exist as aircraft for about three and a half hours a year, and then they are deflated in about 15 minutes, and packed into boxes, and driven back through the Lincoln Tunnel to New Jersey. A registration number is a permanent mark on a permanent airframe, and the parade balloon is neither.34

Its fifth is the silence. In 96 parades that have flown balloons, the Federal Aviation Administration has taken no enforcement action against a single one, and the only federal finding in the file exonerates the balloon and blames the pilot who flew into it. The regulator has had 96 opportunities to ground the fleet, and it has taken none of them.19

Its sixth is the gas. Helium is inert and non-flammable, and the 1958 parade proved the operation can proceed without any gas at all, on air and cranes. The balloon carries no fuel, no engine, and no ignition source, and the worst it can do is drift, which is what it is designed to do.6

Its seventh is the crowd. Three and a half million people gather to watch the fleet fly, and 34.3 million more watch on television, and the crowd is not a victim of the flight. It is the audience, and it returns every year, and it has returned for a century.7

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VIII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every balloon should be registered as a civil aircraft, and every balloon should be issued an N-number, and every N-number should be displayed on the envelope, and every balloon should hold a current airworthiness certificate, and every airworthiness certificate should be displayed at the cabin entrance, and a cabin entrance should be built for the purpose, and every handler should hold a balloon pilot certificate, and every balloon should be inspected annually under section 91.409, and every balloon should carry an automatic rapid deflation device, and every operation should be noticed to the nearest air traffic control facility 24 hours in advance, and the notice should name the owners and operators, the size of each balloon, the location, and the height.101216

The Federal Aviation Administration should establish a Parade Division, register the fleet, and collect the civil penalties for operating unregistered aircraft, at $75,000 per balloon per parade, which is $7.5 million for the 2026 fleet of 100, and $720 million across the 96 parades that have flown balloons, and the $500 in unpaid registration fees, at five dollars per balloon, which is the smallest figure in the ledger and the only one that has ever been asked for. The estates of the 96 parades should be billed. The $100 gift certificate offered for the return of a released balloon is not a registration fee.1118

The queue should be cleared in order. The Administration should process the registrations before any further balloon is inflated, and the balloons should be assigned numbers, and the certificates should be issued, and the pilots should be licensed, and the inspections should be conducted, and the fees should be collected, and the helium should be metered, and the wind should be monitored, and the lampposts should be removed from the route, and the permits should be issued, and the fleet should be grounded until the paperwork is complete, since the fleet has now flown unregistered for 96 parades, in the open, on the public record, one balloon at a time.816

The test case is tomorrow morning. The balloons will inflate this afternoon, and the crowds will gather, and the wind monitors will watch, and the certificates will not exist in the numbers the arithmetic demands, and the largest unregistered aircraft fleet in the United States will fly for the 100th time, in the open, over three and a half million people, one balloon at a time.57
