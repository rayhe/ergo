Food and Drug Law & Amino Acid Pharmacology November 25, 2026 14 min read

L-Tryptophan Is the Sole Raw Material the Brain Uses to Manufacture Serotonin, Serotonin Is the Sole Raw Material the Pineal Gland Uses to Manufacture Melatonin, the FDA Recalled L-Tryptophan Supplements Across America in 1989 After More Than 1,500 Cases of a Crippling Syndrome and at Least 37 Deaths, a Single Six-Ounce Serving of Roast Turkey Delivers 488 Milligrams of the Amino Acid, Americans Eat 46 Million Turkeys on One Thursday in November, and No Thanksgiving Turkey Has Ever Held an Approved New Drug Application

L-tryptophan is the essential amino acid the brain converts into serotonin and the pineal gland converts into melatonin. In 1989 the Food and Drug Administration recalled L-tryptophan supplements after an outbreak of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome produced more than 1,500 reported cases and at least 37 deaths. A six-ounce serving of roast turkey delivers 488 milligrams of the same amino acid, and clinical trials have tested one-gram doses for sleep. On Thanksgiving, 46 million turkeys are eaten in a single day, served alongside the carbohydrate load that drives tryptophan across the blood-brain barrier. No new drug application has ever been filed for a turkey.

By Ambrose Tillinghast

I. The Molecule

L-tryptophan is an essential amino acid, which means the human body cannot synthesize it and must obtain it from food. Its distinction among the twenty amino acids is not its abundance. Tryptophan is the scarcest amino acid in dietary protein, roughly 1 to 1.5 percent of the amino acid content of a typical protein. Its distinction is its destination. Tryptophan is the sole raw material the brain uses to manufacture serotonin, and serotonin is the sole raw material the pineal gland uses to manufacture melatonin, the hormone that signals biological night.1

The conversion runs through a bottleneck. The enzyme tryptophan hydroxylase catalyzes the first and rate-limiting step, turning tryptophan into 5-hydroxytryptophan, which a second enzyme converts into serotonin. At night, the pineal gland converts some of that serotonin into melatonin. Because tryptophan hydroxylase is highly unsaturated at physiologic brain tryptophan concentrations, the rate of serotonin production is limited by how much tryptophan is available. Raising brain tryptophan stimulates its conversion to serotonin, and raising serotonin raises melatonin downstream. Most dietary tryptophan never becomes serotonin at all: roughly 95 percent is metabolized down the kynurenine pathway, and only about 1 to 3 percent enters the serotonin route. The small fraction is the entire point. It is the fraction that makes sleep.2

The sleep effect is not folklore. It is a measured outcome in randomized trials. A double-blind crossover trial found that 1 gram of L-tryptophan taken before bed significantly reduced sleep latency and increased slow-wave, or deep, sleep in healthy volunteers.3 A 2022 meta-analysis pooling 18 randomized controlled trials, with doses from 0.25 to 15 grams per day, found that tryptophan supplementation significantly reduced wake after sleep onset, the time spent awake during the night, with the effect concentrated at doses of 1 gram or more: participants taking at least 1 gram spent about 29 minutes awake during the night, compared with about 57 minutes for those taking less than 1 gram.4 A normal diet already supplies roughly half a gram to 2 grams of tryptophan per day.5 The clinical sleep dose, in other words, is not a pharmaceutical quantity. It is a dietary quantity. It is approximately the quantity on a Thanksgiving plate.

The clinical sleep dose is not a pharmaceutical quantity. It is a dietary quantity. It is approximately the quantity on a Thanksgiving plate.

II. The Precedent

Before 1989, L-tryptophan was sold over the counter in the United States as a dietary supplement, most commonly for insomnia, depression, and premenstrual syndrome, and it was used by bodybuilders. Some consumers were taking up to 8,000 milligrams per day.6 The product was a capsule containing a single amino acid, and its marketing promise was, in substance, sleep.

On October 30, 1989, the New Mexico Health and Environment Department learned of three patients with eosinophilia and severe muscle pain who had been taking L-tryptophan. The illness was eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome, defined by debilitating muscle pain and an absolute eosinophil count of at least 1,000 per cubic millimeter, with no infection or cancer to explain them. Within months the national surveillance system counted more than 1,500 cases reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and at least 37 deaths associated with the syndrome, with the true incidence thought to be much higher.7 The Food and Drug Administration recalled tryptophan supplements in November 1989 and banned most public sales in 1990.8

Epidemiologic investigation traced 97 to 100 percent of the cases that could be traced to L-tryptophan manufactured by one company, Showa Denko KK of Japan, and analytical chemistry identified a contaminant in the case-associated material, 1,1'-ethylidene-bis-L-tryptophan, known as EBT. The exact cause of the syndrome remains unproven to this day.9 The agency's own information paper, issued in February 2001, states the regulatory position plainly: based on the evidence, the FDA concluded that other brands of L-tryptophan, or L-tryptophan itself, regardless of the levels or presence of impurities, could not be eliminated as causal or contributing to the development of the syndrome, and consequently the agency could not determine that oral dosage forms of L-tryptophan could be safely used as dietary supplements.10 The FDA loosened its restrictions on the sale and marketing of tryptophan in February 2001, but an import alert limiting the importation of tryptophan not intended for an exempted use remained in force until 2005.11

The precedent, stated without embellishment, is this: the federal government determined that an amino acid sold for sleep could not be determined safe, recalled it, banned its public sale, and kept the restriction in place for more than a decade. The molecule was L-tryptophan. The molecule in the turkey is L-tryptophan. The agency recalled the capsule. It did not recall the bird.

III. The Statute

Section 201(g)(1) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, codified at 21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1), defines the term drug to mean articles recognized in the official United States Pharmacopeia or National Formulary, articles intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, articles other than food intended to affect the structure or any function of the body of man or other animals, and articles intended for use as a component of any of those.12 The definition turns on intended use, and intended use is determined from labeling, advertising, and the circumstances of distribution. The question is not what a substance is. The question is what it is for.

Thanksgiving supplies the labeling. The standing cultural claim about the Thanksgiving turkey, repeated every November in newspapers, broadcasts, and dinner-table conversation, is that the turkey makes you sleepy. The bird is served with an intended effect attached to it by the entire nation, an effect its own chemistry supports: the amino acid is the precursor to the sleep hormone, and the clinical dose for sleep is one gram. A food is not a drug solely because of a claim on its label, but the statute's parenthetical, other than food, assumes a food consumed as food. A turkey served for the express purpose of inducing the post-prandial nap is not consumed as food. It is administered.

Once the article is a drug, the rest of the statute follows mechanically. Section 201(p) of the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 321(p), defines a new drug as one that is not generally recognized as safe and effective for its labeled use. Section 505(a), 21 U.S.C. § 355(a), provides that no person shall introduce or deliver for introduction into interstate commerce any new drug unless an application approved by the Food and Drug Administration is in effect for the drug. Section 301(d), 21 U.S.C. § 331(d), makes the introduction of an unapproved new drug a prohibited act, and the agency's own warning letters recite the sequence verbatim: the product is a drug because it is intended to affect the structure or function of the body, it is a new drug because it is not generally recognized as safe and effective for that use, and no approved application is in effect for it.13 Section 303(a), 21 U.S.C. § 333(a), sets the penalty: a first offense is a misdemeanor punishable by imprisonment for not more than one year or a fine of not more than $1,000, or both, and a second offense is a felony punishable by imprisonment for not more than three years or a fine of not more than $10,000, or both.

No new drug application has ever been filed for a Thanksgiving turkey. No turkey has ever been the subject of an approved application. The Food and Drug Administration has never inspected a Thanksgiving table.

The agency recalled the capsule. It did not recall the bird. The statute does not know the difference.

IV. The Dose

The United States Department of Agriculture's FoodData Central database records the tryptophan content of roasted turkey breast at 0.287 grams per 100 grams. Roasted chicken breast records 0.362 grams per 100 grams. Sharp cheddar records 0.321 grams. Dried pumpkin seed kernels record 0.576 grams.14 A separate compilation of the same USDA data puts whole roasted turkey meat at 0.24 grams per 100 grams, chicken at 0.24 grams, and beef at 0.23 grams: the poultry rows span less than a tenth of a gram.15 The numbers will matter in Section VI. For now, the dose is what it is.

A six-ounce serving of roast turkey breast holds 488 milligrams of tryptophan, according to USDA-derived food composition tables.16 Two servings, the first plate and the return to the carving board that Thanksgiving etiquette calls seconds, deliver approximately 976 milligrams: within rounding distance of the one-gram dose that reduced sleep latency and increased deep sleep in the clinical trials, and that cut nighttime waking nearly in half in the meta-analysis. The dose is not exotic. It is dinner.

The scale of the administration is the largest of any drug distribution in the country. The National Turkey Federation estimates that Americans eat 46 million turkeys every Thanksgiving, about 88 percent of Americans eating turkey on the same day, with the average bird weighing about 16 pounds, for a total of roughly 736 million pounds of turkey.17 In 2026, roughly 288.1 million people are expected to eat turkey on Thanksgiving, at a total cost of about $1.35 billion.18 The arithmetic of the active ingredient follows: 736 million pounds is approximately 334 million kilograms, and at 2.87 grams of tryptophan per kilogram of meat, the gross tryptophan content of the day's birds is approximately 958 metric tons. Counting only the edible meat, roughly half the gross weight, the quantity administered in a single afternoon is on the order of five hundred metric tons of L-tryptophan. The 1989 supplement market, the one the agency recalled, never approached a single afternoon of this.

The penalty arithmetic follows with the same mechanical certainty. At the statutory maximum of $1,000 per violation for a first offense, 46 million unapproved administrations carry a theoretical exposure of $46 billion. This is arithmetic, not a charging theory. No prosecutor has charged a turkey. The statute does not require that anyone has.

V. The Adjuvant

The turkey alone, it must be admitted, is a poor delivery system. A protein-rich meal contributes so much more of tryptophan's competitors to the bloodstream than of tryptophan itself that the ratio of tryptophan to the other large neutral amino acids falls, and brain tryptophan and serotonin decline with it. The competitors, leucine, isoleucine, valine, tyrosine, phenylalanine, and methionine, share a single transport system across the blood-brain barrier, and tryptophan is the scarcest of them. Eating protein alone does not raise brain serotonin. It lowers it.19

Thanksgiving does not serve protein alone. The meal is a carbohydrate delivery system wrapped around the bird. A carbohydrate-rich meal stimulates insulin secretion, and insulin drives the competing amino acids into skeletal muscle while tryptophan, which binds loosely to circulating albumin, remains in the plasma. The ratio of tryptophan to its competitors rises, tryptophan's flux across the blood-brain barrier increases, and brain serotonin synthesis rises with it. The mechanism was established in rats in 1971 and 1972 by Fernstrom and Wurtman in Science, and in humans in 2003, when Wurtman and colleagues showed that a carbohydrate-rich breakfast, of the kind Americans normally eat, raised the plasma tryptophan-to-competitor ratio by a median of 54 percent, with a range of 36 to 88 percent, while a protein-rich breakfast did not.20

The Thanksgiving table is engineered, whether by tradition or by accident, to maximize this ratio. Mashed potatoes, stuffing, sweet potatoes, dinner rolls, and pie are the carbohydrate load. Cranberry sauce is the sweetest of the accompaniments, and Americans consume close to 80 million pounds of cranberries during the week of Thanksgiving, about 20 percent of the nation's entire annual cranberry consumption, along with five million gallons of jellied cranberry sauce every holiday season.21 The Calorie Control Council estimates the average Thanksgiving dinner at 3,000 calories, with appetizers, drinks, and desserts bringing the day's total to approximately 4,500 calories, more than twice the average daily intake.22 The meal is, in regulatory terms, a combination product: an active ingredient, a bioavailability enhancer, and a vehicle, co-administered on a federal holiday.

The meal is a combination product: an active ingredient, a bioavailability enhancer, and a vehicle, co-administered on a federal holiday.

VI. The Compliance Assessment

The facts, assembled, present a compliance picture that the drug-approval regime has declined to assemble. L-tryptophan is the sole precursor of serotonin and, through serotonin, of melatonin. Clinical trials establish a sleep effect at one gram. A Thanksgiving plate with seconds delivers approximately one gram. The carbohydrate load that accompanies the bird raises the tryptophan-to-competitor ratio by a median of 54 percent, driving the amino acid across the blood-brain barrier. The agency recalled the same molecule in capsule form in 1989 after more than 1,500 cases and at least 37 deaths, and stated in 2001 that it could not determine oral L-tryptophan could be safely used as a dietary supplement. The statute defines a drug by intended use, requires an approved application for any new drug, and prohibits the introduction of an unapproved new drug into interstate commerce. Forty-six million turkeys cross state lines every November. No application has been filed.

It must be granted, in the interest of the precision this subject demands, that the case against the turkey is weaker than the case against the capsule in every particular that matters. The USDA's own tables show that roast turkey breast contains less tryptophan per 100 grams than roast chicken breast, less than sharp cheddar, and about half as much as dried pumpkin seeds. The protein in the meal competes with the dose it delivers. The statute's definition of a drug expressly says other than food. The drowsiness that follows the meal is more plausibly attributed to 4,500 calories, alcohol, and a day of eating than to 488 milligrams of an amino acid. No prosecutor has argued, and none will, that a turkey possesses the intent to affect the structure or function of the body, or that the Food and Drug Administration could serve a warning letter on a dining room.

But the arithmetic of drug approval does not run on intent. It runs on molecule, on dose, on delivery, and on scale. The agency spent more than a decade restricting a molecule it could not determine safe, while the same molecule is administered to 288 million people in a single afternoon, at the clinical dose, with a documented bioavailability enhancer, on a day the federal government itself designates a legal public holiday. The only two birds in America whose non-administration is formally documented each year are the two the President pardons at the White House, a tradition of presenting the National Thanksgiving Turkey that began in 1947.23 Every other turkey is served. Every other turkey is eaten. The recall covered the capsule.

No application has been filed. No inspection has been conducted. The bird is served every year.
