Contractor Licensing & Severe Weather

Hail Causes More Than $10 Billion in Damage in the United States Every Year, the Largest Hailstone Ever Recovered in the Country Measured Eight Inches Across and Weighed 1.94 Pounds, Stones Larger Than Four Inches Fall Faster Than 100 Miles an Hour, a Full Roof Replacement Requires a Building Permit in Virtually Every Jurisdiction in the Country, Roofing Work in California Requires a C-39 License, and No Hailstone Has Ever Applied for Either

Hail is the largest unlicensed roofing contractor in the United States, and it is also the only one that has never applied for a license. Every year, thousands of hailstorms strip shingles from roofs, puncture underlayment, and crack decking, performing the demolition phase of a roof replacement at highway speed, without a permit, without a license, without an inspection, and without ever filing a form with any building department in the country. The federal government and the states have spent a century building a licensing system for exactly this work, and it licenses the contractor, the permit, the bond, and the inspection. It has never licensed the hail. This investigation examines the largest unlicensed roofing operation in the United States, the work it performs, the law it evades, and the permit the record demands.

By Nathaniel Frost · November 11, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Trade

Hail is frozen precipitation in the form of balls or irregular lumps of ice, defined by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration as a particle five millimeters, about a quarter of an inch, or larger. It is manufactured in the thunderstorm, and the manufacturing process is violent. A strong updraft carries a raindrop above the freezing level, where supercooled water freezes onto it on contact. The stone is carried up and down through the cloud, collecting a new layer of ice on each pass, growing heavier with every cycle, until gravity finally overcomes the updraft and the stone falls. The stronger the updraft, the larger the stone it can hold.1

The National Weather Service measures the trade in familiar objects. A hailstone the size of a pea is a quarter of an inch across. A dime is three quarters of an inch. A quarter is one inch, and one inch is the threshold at which the National Weather Service calls a thunderstorm severe. A golf ball is one and three quarter inches. A tennis ball is two and a half. A baseball is two and three quarters. A softball is four inches or more. The updraft required to suspend each is measured, too: about 24 miles an hour to hold up a pea-sized stone, 49 miles an hour for a quarter-sized stone, and 103 miles an hour for a softball-sized stone. The updraft that builds a softball-sized hailstone is itself a hurricane-force wind, blowing straight up.12

The threshold for severity was itself raised by the record. Until January 5, 2010, the National Weather Service called a thunderstorm severe when it produced hail three quarters of an inch across, the size of a penny. On that date the service raised the minimum to one inch, quarter size, because research showed that significant damage does not occur from hail smaller than an inch. Quarter-sized hail and larger are the stones that damage cars, homes, buildings, and crops. The service did not raise the threshold because the hail got smaller. It raised it because the hail below an inch was not doing enough damage to count.2

The fall speeds are measured by the National Severe Storms Laboratory. Stones under one inch fall at 9 to 25 miles an hour. Stones of one to one and three quarter inches fall at 25 to 40 miles an hour. Stones of two to four inches fall at 44 to 72 miles an hour. Stones larger than four inches fall at more than 100 miles an hour. A hailstone does not drift down. It arrives.1

II. The Record

The largest hailstone ever recovered in the United States fell near Vivian, South Dakota, on July 23, 2010. It measured 8.0 inches in diameter, 18.625 inches in circumference, and weighed 1.9375 pounds. It was found by Vivian resident Les Scott, who collected several enormous stones after the storm and put the largest in his freezer. He had originally planned to make daiquiris out of the hail. A storm-related power outage melted part of the stone before personnel from the National Weather Service in Aberdeen could measure it, and the committee that certified the record noted that the stone had almost certainly been larger at impact. The National Climate Extremes Committee certified it as the United States record for both diameter and weight. The stone now rests at the National Center for Atmospheric Research.34

The Vivian stone displaced two records at once. The previous record for diameter was 7 inches, set at Aurora, Nebraska, on June 22, 2003. The previous record for weight was 1.67 pounds, set at Coffeyville, Kansas, on September 3, 1970. Aurora retains one record: its stone still holds the record for circumference, at 18.75 inches, a fraction of an inch more than Vivian’s 18.625. The record book keeps the categories separate, and the stones keep competing across them.34

The world record is heavier, and it killed people. The heaviest hailstones on record, according to the World Meteorological Organization, weighed up to 1.02 kilograms, 2.25 pounds, and fell in the Gopalganj district of Bangladesh on April 14, 1986, killing 92 people. The American record stone has never killed anyone. The world record stone killed 92. The difference is not the stone. It is where the stone fell.4

The American record for injury belongs to a festival. On May 5, 1995, softball-sized hail fell on the Mayfest festival in Fort Worth, Texas, injuring more than 400 people and causing $1.1 billion in insured losses. The crowd had gathered outdoors, under an open sky, on a spring afternoon. The stones arrived at more than 100 miles an hour, each carrying the energy of a small-caliber round, and no one in the crowd held a permit for any of them.5

A softball-sized hailstone falling at 100 miles an hour carries about 500 joules of energy, roughly the muzzle energy of a 9mm pistol round. The Vivian stone carried about 880.

III. The Scale

The operation runs every year, in every hail season, across the whole of the country. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration counts an average of around 7,000 severe hail events in the United States each year. In 2025, the Insurance Information Institute counted 5,432 hail events of one inch or larger, and the Storm Prediction Center’s own file counted 7,982 severe hail reports, including 1,058 reports of hail two inches or larger. Texas had the most events in 2025, with 902, followed by Kansas with 375. The hardest-hit states are Colorado, Nebraska, and Wyoming, a region the industry calls Hail Alley.678

The bill is the largest in the weather business. Hail causes more than $10 billion in damage in the United States each year, more than tornadoes. Severe convective storms, the category that includes hail, tornadoes, and damaging wind, caused at least $66 billion in insured losses in 2023, $59 billion in 2024, and at least $51 billion in 2025, according to Gallagher Re. Hail accounts for between 60 and 80 percent of severe convective storm losses, according to Aon. Wind and hail together caused 42.5 percent of all homeowners insurance losses in 2023, more than any other cause, and the average wind or hail claim was $14,747.678

The single storms are measured in billions. The costliest hail event in American history, by the billion-dollar disaster database, was the Midwest and Ohio Valley hail and tornado outbreak of April 6 to 11, 2001, which included the Tri-State hailstorm along Interstate 70 from Kansas City to St. Louis: $5.85 billion in 2026 dollars. The Rockies hailstorms of June 2023 cost $5.77 billion. The May 2017 Denver hailstorm cost $4.56 billion, the most expensive hailstorm in Colorado history, with insured losses exceeding $2.2 billion at the time. The March 12 to 14, 2024 storms cost $6.51 billion. From 1980 to 2024, severe storms caused 203 of the 403 billion-dollar weather disasters in the United States, at a total cost of $514.4 billion.59

Hail performed roughly $31 billion to $41 billion of unpermitted roofing work in 2025 alone. The bill went to the insurers.

Do the arithmetic the record permits. Severe convective storms caused at least $51 billion in insured losses in 2025. Hail accounts for 60 to 80 percent of severe convective storm losses. The hail share of the 2025 total is therefore roughly $31 billion to $41 billion. The average residential roof replacement in 2025 cost $17,631, according to Verisk. At that price, the hail share of a single year’s losses would pay for re-roofing roughly 1.8 million to 2.3 million homes. Every one of those roofs was worked on by an unlicensed contractor, and none of the work was inspected.

IV. The Work

The work hail performs on a roof is the demolition phase of a re-roof, executed from above at highway speed. It strips shingles from the deck. It fractures underlayment. It dents metal roofing, cracks tile, and bruises asphalt shingles, knocking the protective granules from the surface and exposing the mat beneath to sun and water. A roof that has been hailed on does not look destroyed. It looks tired, pitted, and thinned, and it leaks in the next rain. The tear-off crew that follows does the same work more slowly, with pry bars, and charges for it.10

The force of the work is a matter of arithmetic. Computed from the National Severe Storms Laboratory’s fall speeds and the density of ice, a quarter-sized stone falling at 40 miles an hour carries about one joule of energy. A golf-ball-sized stone at the same speed carries about seven. A baseball-sized stone falling at 72 miles an hour carries about 85. A softball-sized stone falling at more than 100 miles an hour carries about 500 joules, roughly the muzzle energy of a standard 9mm pistol round. The Vivian stone, at 1.94 pounds and more than 100 miles an hour, carried about 880 joules, roughly twice the muzzle energy of a 9mm. The roof is not struck by weather. It is struck by a volley of small-caliber rounds made of ice, fired from a cloud, with no one holding the weapon.1

The price of the lawful version of the same work is published every year. The 2025 Cost vs. Value report puts a full tear-off and 3,000 square feet of fiberglass asphalt shingles at $31,871 on average nationally, including underlayment, drip edge, flashing, and two skylights. Verisk’s 2026 U.S. Roof Report puts the average residential roof replacement in 2025 at $17,631 across all materials, about 33 percent above the 2021 to 2024 average. The roof is the most expensive single component of the house to replace, and the hail replaces it for free, without asking, and without a permit.15

The permit is the part the law actually requires. The International Residential Code, which most local governments adopt as the foundation for their building rules, requires a permit for any work that alters, repairs, or replaces a building’s structural components. For roofing, that includes tearing off old materials and installing a new roof, replacing damaged decking or rafters, and adding or modifying structural supports. A full roof replacement requires a building permit in virtually all jurisdictions in the United States. When a licensed contractor does the work, the contractor pulls the permit, and the license number goes on the application. When a homeowner does the work, the homeowner pulls the permit as an owner-builder. When hail does the work, no one pulls anything.1011

The code requires a permit for a full roof replacement and exempts the replacement of a few shingles. Hail has never performed a minor repair.

The code does contain an exemption, and the investigation reports it. The International Residential Code exempts ordinary repairs from permit requirements, and the standard example of an ordinary repair is replacing a few damaged shingles, patching a small leak, or re-sealing flashing. A few shingles. The exemption is written for the homeowner with a tube of sealant and a Saturday afternoon. Hail does not replace a few shingles. It replaces the roof. The exemption does not cover the work, because the work is not minor, and the worker is not a homeowner.

V. The License

The license the hail lacks is a specific instrument with a specific number. In California, roofing is Class C-39 work, licensed by the Contractors State License Board. A C-39 roofing contractor installs products and repairs surfaces that seal, waterproof, and weatherproof structures, to prevent water or its derivatives, compounds, or solids from penetrating such protection and gaining access to material or space beyond. The classification covers asphaltum, pitch, tar, felt, glass fabric, urethane foam, metal roofing systems, shakes, shingles, roof tile, slate, and any other roofing, waterproofing, or membrane material. California law requires a C-39 license for any roofing work valued at more than $500. Working without one is a misdemeanor under California Business and Professions Code Section 7028.12

The license is not handed out. The applicant must show at least four years of journey-level experience in the roofing classification within the ten years before applying, pass the Law and Business examination and the C-39 trade examination, carry a $25,000 contractor’s bond, and maintain workers’ compensation insurance even if the business has no employees. The bond exists to protect the homeowner. The insurance exists to protect the worker. The examinations exist to prove the contractor knows the code. Hail holds no bond, carries no insurance, and has never sat for an examination.12

The pattern repeats in every state that licenses the trade. In Louisiana, roof work on a residential dwelling valued at more than $7,500 requires a residential roofing license, effective January 1, 2026, and Jefferson Parish requires a re-roofing permit before the replacement of 50 percent or more of the roof covering on any one- or two-family dwelling, with the contractor’s license on file. In Maryland, it is illegal to perform home improvement work over $500 without a valid Maryland Home Improvement Commission license, and working without a permit is a misdemeanor punishable by up to one year in jail or criminal fines. Maryland licensees must demonstrate at least two years of experience, pass written examinations, and carry a minimum of $50,000 in liability insurance. In Florida, the licensed roofing contractor is legally required to pull the permit, and a contractor who asks the homeowner to pull it instead is a red flag for an unlicensed operator.131411

The system is thorough, and it is universal. Forty-two specialty classifications in California alone. A license number on every permit application. A bond on file for every licensee. An inspection at the end of every job. The building departments of the country have built a complete regulatory apparatus for the removal and replacement of roofs, and the largest roofing contractor in the country operates entirely outside it, in every state, in every season, at highway speed, from the sky.

VI. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the personhood of the defendant. Hail is not a person. It cannot hold a license, sign a contract, post a bond, or appear before a licensing board. The licensing statutes regulate persons who perform work for compensation, and a hailstone performs no work for anyone and is compensated by no one. The permit requirement attaches to the owner of the building or the contractor the owner hires, not to the weather.

Its second is the contract. Roofing work, as the statutes define it, is work performed under a contract for a price. Hail enters into no contract, names no price, and issues no invoice. The $31 billion to $41 billion figure is a measure of damage, not a measure of work performed, and damage is not a service. The homeowner does not hire the hail. The hail hires itself, and pays itself nothing.

Its third is the exemption. The International Residential Code exempts ordinary repairs from permit requirements, and the hail that falls on most roofs on most days is pea-sized, dime-sized, or smaller, the kind of hail that does no damage at all. The code’s exemption for minor work covers exactly the hail that does nothing. The hail that does something is the exception, and the law is written for the rule.

Its fourth is the doctrine. Insurance law calls hail an act of God, a loss arising from natural causes without human intervention, and the entire edifice of contractor licensing is built on human intervention. The state licenses the human. It does not license the sky. The sky is not a contractor, and the storm is not a job site.

Its fifth is the impossibility. A hailstone cannot read the code. It cannot sit for an examination, cannot carry a $25,000 bond, cannot maintain workers’ compensation insurance, and cannot pull a permit, because it melts. The law does not demand the impossible, and the prescription demands exactly that.

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every stone that strikes a roof should be covered by a permit, filed in advance, one application per stone, each naming the stone, the diameter, the roof, and the hour. Stones of one inch or more, the size at which the National Weather Service calls a thunderstorm severe, should hold a C-39 license or its local equivalent, with four years of journey-level experience, two examinations passed, a $25,000 bond on file, and workers’ compensation insurance in force. The building department should inspect the work before the storm and after it. The permit should expire 180 days after issuance if no work has started, which, for a stone that melts in minutes, is a generous window.1012

The test case is the next storm. About 7,000 severe hail events a year, thousands of unlicensed contractors arriving at highway speed, each carrying a permit application it will never file, each performing a roof replacement it will never be paid for, each melting before the inspector arrives. The roof will be stripped, the underlayment punctured, the decking cracked, and the granules knocked from the shingles, and the building department will record the damage, issue the permit for the repair, and license the contractor who fixes what the unlicensed contractor did, as it has done every year, since the first roof, under the first storm, waiting for a form that does not exist.

Ergo.
