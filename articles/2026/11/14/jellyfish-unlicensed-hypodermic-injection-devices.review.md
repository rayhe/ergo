# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Jellyfish Are the Largest Unlicensed Injection Operation in the World"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the established Ergo format precisely: absurd-yet-rigorous mega-headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building the case from procedure to device to fleet to route to law to scale to defense to prescription, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. Structure escalates with real dramatic logic: the sting is defined as an injection, the device is measured at 700 nanoseconds and 5.41 million g, the fleet is introduced member by member, the route widens from beaches to nuclear reactors, the law is specified by regulation and statute, the scale is priced in bodies and dollars, the defense concedes the escape hatches, and the prescription demands a 510(k) per stinging cell. The Wallis Sands incident, one dead animal performing 150 injections in an afternoon, is the piece's sharpest single exhibit, and the nuclear plant shutdowns give the route section a second line of business no prior piece has used. Minor weakness: Section III carries five fleet members in sequence, lion's mane, box, Irukandji, man o' war, and immortal, which slightly crowds the section, though each earns its place on a distinct vector (the longest, the deadliest, the smallest, the impostor, the undying).

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0/10
Dead-serious investigative tone throughout, matching the pollen and firewood pieces in the regulatory register. Humor emerges exclusively from deadpan precision, never from jokes. Standout lines: "One animal. One hundred fifty patients. No prescription.", "The practitioner drifts away.", "Five million times the force of gravity, delivered by an animal with no brain.", "The venom is unapproved. The needle is unregistered. The patient never consented.", and "because it has no brain with which to do any of these things." Excellent voice match; the piece never winks. Minor note: "no brain" does double duty in the pullquote and again in the defense, a small repetition in an otherwise tight register.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes, regulations, and study results cited are real: the 150 million annual stings estimate from the peer reviewed dermatology literature, the Florida and Chesapeake Bay sting counts and the Philippine death toll from the National Science Foundation, the box jellyfish death estimates, the Nüchter Current Biology measurements of nematocyst discharge, the 21 CFR 880.5570 definition and Class II classification of the hypodermic needle, the 21 U.S.C. 355(a) bar on unapproved new drugs in interstate commerce, the California Business and Professions Code sections 2052 and 2053 on unlicensed practice of medicine, the lion's mane record measurements, the Wallis Sands incident, and the nuclear plant shutdowns from the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists. The 150 million figure is presented as an estimate, which is honest, since its provenance is unclear even in the literature that reports it. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that every jellyfish requires a 510(k) clearance per nematocyst and an approved new drug application for its venom, is the disclosed conceit; the piece is honest about the escape hatch, that a jellyfish is not a person and cannot file a form, in Section VII, the same way prior pieces disclosed theirs. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
"Jellyfish inject 150 million people a year without a license" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone, and it carries three hooks most Ergo premises lack: a single box jellyfish can kill more than 60 humans, the sting fires in 700 nanoseconds, and one dead jellyfish stung 150 people at a New Hampshire beach. "One animal. One hundred fifty patients. No prescription." and "The practitioner drifts away." are strong pull-quote material, and the immortal jellyfish, an animal that becomes young again when injured, is the kind of counterintuitive fact that travels on its own. The hero image, an FDA inspector with a 510(k) clipboard facing a beached lion's mane beside a box jellyfish warning sign, is built for the thumbnail. High share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act framework is accurately summarized: the 21 CFR 880.5570 definition of a hypodermic single lumen needle as a device intended to inject fluids below the surface of the skin, and its Class II classification requiring a 510(k) premarket notification; the 21 U.S.C. 355(a) prohibition on introducing a new drug into interstate commerce without an effective approved application, quoted verbatim; and the California Business and Professions Code section 2052 prohibition on diagnosing, treating, or prescribing without a certificate, punishable by a fine up to ten thousand dollars or up to one year in county jail, with section 2053 elevating the conduct to a felony where it creates a risk of great bodily harm or death. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that a jellyfish must hold a 510(k) and a new drug approval, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends any jellyfish has applied for one. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from statutory text.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: Current Biology and Nature Communications for the nematocyst physics, the National Science Foundation for the sting counts, death tolls, and bloom losses, the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists for the nuclear plant shutdowns, Cornell's Legal Information Institute for the needle regulation, govinfo for the new drug statute, and the Medical Board of California for the unlicensed practice statute. The Wallis Sands incident is corroborated by both the encyclopedia record and contemporaneous local press. Minor weaknesses: several biology figures, the 50 to 100 annual box jellyfish deaths, the venom sufficient to kill more than 60 humans, and the 24 eyes, reach the piece through secondary aggregators rather than primary literature, and the 700 nanosecond discharge figure was measured in Hydra, not in a jellyfish, a distinction the article handles by describing the shared cnidarian mechanism but does not flag explicitly. The 150 million figure is an estimate whose origin the literature itself cannot trace, and the article is transparent about that. The disputed question of whether the device or the drug is the violation is handled by charging both, which is the cleaner reading of the conceit.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 150 million stings per year, 200,000 in Florida, 500,000 in Chesapeake Bay, 50 to 100 box jellyfish deaths per year, 20 to 40 in the Philippines, at least 69 in Australia since 1883, more than 60 humans per box jellyfish, three minutes to death, 700 nanoseconds, 5,410,000 g, more than 7 gigapascals, 1,200 tentacles, 25 days of postmortem stinging, about 150 beachgoers and five hospitalizations at Wallis Sands, a 7 foot bell and 120 foot tentacles, 2,200 pounds, 24 eyes, about 60 tentacles up to 10 feet, one cubic centimeter, 50 to 100 Irukandji hospitalizations, 4.5 millimeters, 95 percent water, more than 500 million years, 1,400 megawatts, three days at Oskarshamn, two reactors for a week at Torness, four reactors at Gravelines, 10,000 species and about 100 dangerous, ten thousand dollars and one year in county jail. The comparison framework, the biological harpoon against the manufactured needle and the volunteer-free sting count against the licensed procedure count, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
