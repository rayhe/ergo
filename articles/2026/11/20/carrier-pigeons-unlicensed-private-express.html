Postal Law & Ornithology

Carrier Pigeons Have Delivered Letters for More Than 5,000 Years Without a Stamp, a Postmark, or a ZIP Code, the Postal Service Holds a Legal Monopoly on the Delivery of Letters Under 18 U.S.C. 1696, One Pigeon Could Carry 40,000 Messages Into Besieged Paris on Microfilm, Another Flew 25 Miles in 25 Minutes to Save 194 Soldiers, a First-Class Stamp Now Costs 82 Cents, and No Pigeon Has Ever Paid Postage

The carrier pigeon is a mail carrier that fits on no route. It carries letters without a stamp, without a postmark, without a ZIP code, and without ever having applied for the privilege, and it does so at sixty miles an hour, over distances of six hundred miles, guided by a magnetic field it can see. The United States has spent two centuries building a legal monopoly on the delivery of letters, and the pigeon has spent five thousand years ignoring it. This investigation examines the largest unlicensed private express in the history of the mails, the bird that flies it, the messages it carries, the law it evades, and the postage the record demands.

By Obadiah Finch · November 20, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Bird

The homing pigeon is a variety of domestic pigeon, Columba livia domestica, selectively bred from the wild rock dove for its ability to find its way home over extremely long distances. It is also called the mail pigeon, or the messenger, or colloquially the homer. Egypt was using homing pigeons for pigeon post by 3000 BCE, and messenger pigeons were used as early as 1150 in Baghdad, and by 1167 a regular service between Baghdad and Syria had been established by Sultan Nur ad-Din. The bird has carried the mail for more than five thousand years.1

A homing pigeon flies fast and far. Its average flying speed over moderate distances of about 600 miles is around 60 miles per hour, and speeds of up to 100 miles per hour have been observed in top racers for short distances. Flights as long as 1,100 miles have been recorded by birds in competitive pigeon racing. A bird released 600 miles from home in the morning can be back in its loft before nightfall.2

How it finds its way home is a question science has studied for a century without fully answering. Most researchers believe the ability rests on a map and compass model, with the compass relying on the sun. The map mechanism is still debated. Pigeons navigate using a combination of senses: magnetoreception, through a quantum-mechanical compass in a retinal protein called cryptochrome 4 that lets them see the magnetic field of the Earth, an olfactory map of wind-borne odors, the position of the sun, and visual landmarks. Researchers at Oxford University, tracking birds by GPS, found that pigeons returning to their loft flew along roads and other man-made features, making ninety-degree turns, turning at junctions, even going around roundabouts, adding miles to their journeys. The bird follows the road home the way a person does.3

The bird carries weight. An average carrier pigeon weighs about 500 grams, and pigeons can comfortably carry 75 grams, about 15 percent of their body weight, all day over any distance. As early as 1903, the German apothecary Julius Neubronner used carrier pigeons to receive and deliver urgent medication, and by 1907 he had built a 75-gram camera for his birds, which flew reconnaissance missions over enemy territory with the camera strapped to their chests.4

The bird has one limitation, and it is the whole of the postal question. A homing pigeon flies only home, to the one mentally marked point it has identified as its nest. Pigeon post can only work when the sender is actually holding the receiver's pigeons. The bird cannot deliver to an arbitrary address. It can only return.1

A pigeon flies only home. The sender must already hold the recipient's birds.

II. The Route

The first regular airmail service in New Zealand was flown by pigeons. In 1896, a writer for the New Zealand Herald, reporting from Great Barrier Island, filed his story to the mainland using a pigeon named Ariel. In 1897, the Great Barrier Pigeongram Agency began, and the first regular airmail service in New Zealand was a bird. Before the service, the only regular connection between the community on Great Barrier Island, 90 kilometres northeast of Auckland, and the mainland was a weekly coastal steamer.5

Two pigeon-mail companies soon operated on the island. They set a precedent by becoming the first to issue stamps to pre-pay the cost of sending messages, an innovation unique in the world at the time. The stamps were effectively the world's earliest airmail stamps, pre-dating the first recorded aeroplane flight by several years. Birds were sent over to the island on the weekly steamer and flew back to Auckland with up to five messages per bird, written on tissue paper and attached to their legs. The service continued until 1908, when the installation of an underwater telegraphic cable made pigeon-grams unnecessary.56

The pigeon post had already proved itself under siege. During the Siege of Paris in 1870 and 1871, balloons could carry mail out of the city, but nothing could fly back in, because the balloons were unsteerable and the wind blew only one way. The answer was the carrier pigeon. Balloons flew hundreds of pigeons out of Paris so the birds could return with messages, and a photographic expert named Rene Dagron shrank government dispatches onto microfilm. A single pigeon could fly in 40,000 dispatches, equivalent to the contents of a substantial book. The messages were limited to a maximum of 20 words, and they were carried into Paris at a cost of 5 francs each.7

Of the 302 pigeons sent out of Paris in the course of the siege, 57 returned to the city. Those 57 delivered more than 60,000 letters. The 245 that did not return were shot down by German riflemen, captured, or lost to the winter. The return rate was about 19 percent. The mail got through anyway.78

The commercial case was made twenty years earlier, by a news agency. In 1850, Paul Julius Reuter set up an information service using a fleet of 45 carrier pigeons to deliver news and stock prices between Brussels and Aachen, the terminal points of the German and French-Belgian telegraph lines, separated by a 76-mile gap. The pigeons carried the messages between the two cities within two hours, beating the railroad by six hours. One year later, Reuter founded the Reuters news agency in London. The news empire began as a pigeon express.9

One bird could carry 40,000 dispatches. The post office charged five francs per message.

III. The History

The United States government has itself run a pigeon express, and it was the largest in the country's history. In November 1917, the Signal Corps Pigeon Service received official authorization. By the end of the First World War, the Signal Corps had sent more than 15,000 trained pigeons to the American Expeditionary Forces. The pigeons were a communication method of last resort, used by frontline troops when every other method had failed.10

Its most famous bird flew on October 4, 1918. On that afternoon, American artillery was dropping a barrage directly on roughly 500 soldiers of the 77th Infantry Division, trapped behind German lines in the Argonne Forest and known as the Lost Battalion. Major Charles Whittlesey had one way left to stop the bombardment. His men had already sent two pigeons, and both had been shot down. The last pigeon in the basket was Cher Ami, a British-bred bird who had already completed 12 successful missions. Whittlesey tied a message to the bird's leg: "We are along the road parallel to 276.4. Our own artillery is dropping a barrage directly on us. For heaven's sake, stop it."11

Cher Ami was shot down and got airborne again. With a bullet in the breast and the right leg hanging by a single tendon, the message canister still attached, the bird flew 25 miles in 25 minutes to the division loft at Rampont, arriving on its back, covered in blood. The shelling stopped. Only 194 men were able to walk out of the forest. Cher Ami was awarded the Croix de Guerre by the French government, and the bird died of its wounds on June 13, 1919, at Fort Monmouth, New Jersey. The bird is on display at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History.1112

The Second World War scaled the operation up. During the war, the United States military trained 54,000 homing pigeons, and more than 36,000 of them were used overseas by 12 signal pigeon companies throughout each theater of operation. The force consisted of 3,150 soldiers. American pigeon fanciers supplied approximately 40,000 racing pigeons voluntarily to the Signal Corps, without compensation. The United Kingdom used about 250,000 homing pigeons. Thirty-two pigeons received the Dickin Medal, the highest decoration for valor given to animals.1013

One of the 32 was an American. On October 18, 1943, during the Italian campaign, a pigeon named G.I. Joe flew 20 miles in 20 minutes carrying an order to cancel the scheduled bombing of Calvi Vecchia, a village the British 56th Infantry Division had captured ahead of schedule. The message arrived just as the planes were preparing to take off. G.I. Joe saved about 1,000 men, and was awarded the Dickin Medal, the only American animal to receive it during the war.10

The service ended without ceremony. The United States Army discontinued using pigeons as message carriers in 1957. The Pigeon Center at Fort Monmouth had an emergency breeding capacity of 1,000 birds a month. When the Churchill Loft closed in March 1957, the remaining birds were sold to the public or donated to zoos. The largest pigeon express in American history was dissolved, and no bird was ever charged postage.10

Twenty-five miles in twenty-five minutes. Sixty miles an hour, with a bullet in its chest.

IV. The Law

The law of the letter is older than the pigeon post, and it is written against exactly this kind of carrier. Under the Private Express Statutes, a group of civil and criminal statutes, the Postal Service has exclusive rights in the carriage and delivery of letters under certain circumstances. The Postal Regulatory Commission, in a 2022 rulemaking, described the statutes as making it unlawful for any entity other than the Postal Service to send or carry letters. The monopoly is codified at 39 U.S.C. 601 to 606 and 18 U.S.C. 1693 to 1699.14

The civil statute states the conditions under which a letter may be carried out of the mails, and it states them as six requirements, all of which must be met. Under 39 U.S.C. 601(a), the letter must be enclosed in an envelope; the amount of postage which would have been charged if the letter had been sent by mail must be paid by stamps, or postage meter stamps, on the envelope; the envelope must be properly addressed; the envelope must be so sealed that the letter cannot be taken from it without defacing the envelope; any stamps on the envelope must be canceled in ink by the sender; and the date of the letter, of its transmission or receipt by the carrier, must be endorsed on the envelope in ink. A pigeon carries its message in a capsule on its leg. It carries no envelope, no stamp, no cancellation, and no ink.15

The statute offers one other door. Under 39 U.S.C. 601(b), a letter may also be carried out of the mails when the amount paid for the private carriage of the letter is at least six times the rate then currently charged for the first ounce of a single-piece first-class letter, or when the letter weighs at least 12.5 ounces. A First-Class stamp now costs 82 cents, effective July 12, 2026, so the six-times price is $4.92 per letter. A pigeon charges nothing for carriage, which is less than $4.92. A pigeon carries at most 75 grams, which is about 2.6 ounces, which is less than 12.5 ounces. Neither door opens.1516

The criminal statute is steeper. Under 18 U.S.C. 1696(a), whoever establishes any private express for the conveyance of letters or packets, or in any manner causes or provides for the conveyance of the same by regular trips or at stated periods over any post route, or from any city, town, or place to any other city, town, or place, between which the mail is regularly carried, shall be fined not more than $500 or imprisoned not more than six months, or both. Under 1696(b), whoever transmits by private express or other unlawful means any letter or packet shall be fined. The Great Barrier Pigeongram Agency ran regular trips, at stated periods, between an island and a city between which the mail was regularly carried, and its birds flew the route in place of the weekly steamer. The Paris pigeon post ran regular trips into a besieged city. Reuter's pigeons ran regular trips between Brussels and Aachen.17

The statute has been tested in court, and it has survived. In United States v. Black, 569 F.2d 1111 (10th Cir. 1978), the defendants were charged with knowingly and unlawfully establishing a private express for the conveyance of letters by regular trips over one or more post routes, in violation of 18 U.S.C. 1696. The court held the statute constitutional, as a valid exercise of the power granted by Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution to establish post offices and post roads. The opinion records what was conceded: that the private express statutes were intended to give the United States a monopoly in the delivery of letters and to preclude competition by private express.18

The monopoly is not a small one. In fiscal year 2025, the Postal Service carried 42.0 billion pieces of First-Class Mail, for $25.8 billion in revenue, out of 108.7 billion total pieces, and reported a net loss of $9.0 billion. The stamp has risen from 73 cents to 78 cents in July 2025, and from 78 cents to 82 cents in July 2026. The price of a letter keeps rising, and the pigeon keeps flying for free.1619

The law requires a stamp canceled in ink by the sender. The pigeon carries no ink.

V. The Payload

The payload is the whole dispute. A carrier pigeon weighs about 500 grams and can comfortably carry 75 grams, about 15 percent of its body weight, all day over any distance. A standard one-ounce letter weighs 28.35 grams, so the bird's capacity is about two and two-thirds letters by weight, or about fifteen sheets of ordinary paper. The message itself, written on tissue paper and rolled into a small tube, weighs almost nothing. The capsule is the constraint, not the paper.4

The capsule has a history. A carrier pigeon capsule from 1918, with its flimsy message docket still inside, survives in the National Museum of Ireland, a small metal cylinder that clipped to the bird's leg and carried a pencilled note from Dundalk Barracks to Dublin. Cher Ami's message canister was still attached to the leg when the bird arrived at Rampont, hanging by a single tendon.2011

The microfilm changed the arithmetic. During the Siege of Paris, a single pigeon could carry 40,000 dispatches on slivers of collodium, each weighing a hundredth of an ounce. The messages were projected by magic lantern onto a wall in Paris, transcribed by clerks, and delivered by regular post. The French postal service charged 5 francs per message, limited to 20 words. More than 60,000 letters were received by pigeon during the siege. At 5 francs each, the pigeon post collected 300,000 francs, and the state itself ran the service.78

The modern bird carries more than paper. The Internet Engineering Task Force standardized the carriage of messages by homing pigeon in RFC 1149, IP over Avian Carriers, published in 1990. The standard exists. The pigeon is, as a matter of published protocol, a network carrier, and it has never been assigned an address.21

The payload is also the reason the pigeon cannot be licensed like other carriers. A carrier must deliver to the address on the envelope. The pigeon delivers only to its own loft, and only if the sender already holds the recipient's birds. It is a delivery service with exactly one address, and that address is its own home.1

A bird that can carry 40,000 messages has exactly one address, and it is its own home.

VI. The Scale

Do the arithmetic the record permits. A First-Class stamp costs 82 cents. The 54,000 pigeons the United States trained in the Second World War, each carrying one message, carried 54,000 letters without postage. At 82 cents each, the unpaid postage is $44,280.1016

At Paris capacity, the number grows. One pigeon could carry 40,000 dispatches. Fifty-four thousand pigeons, at 40,000 dispatches each, is 2.16 billion dispatches. At 82 cents each, the unpaid postage is about $1.77 billion. The figure is arithmetic, not measurement, and the assumption is stated with it: that every bird flew at Paris capacity, which no bird ever did. The arithmetic is correct anyway, and the birds were real, and the postage was not paid.71016

The fines follow the same arithmetic. Under 18 U.S.C. 1696(b), whoever transmits any letter by private express shall be fined, and under 1696(a), the fine for establishing a private express is not more than $500. If each of the 40,000 dispatches on a single pigeon's flight were a separate offense at the $500 maximum, one bird, one flight, would owe $20,000,000. The 57 pigeons that returned to Paris, at 40,000 dispatches each, would owe $1.14 billion between them. The 245 pigeons that did not return owe nothing, because they are dead.717

The six-times rule sets the lawful price. At six times the 82-cent rate, lawful private carriage costs $4.92 per letter. The 40,000 dispatches on a single Paris flight, carried lawfully, would have cost $196,800. The French post charged 5 francs per message and collected 300,000 francs for the siege service, and it collected the fee in full, because the state itself ran the pigeons.71516

The volume comparison is the sharpest. The Postal Service carried 42.0 billion pieces of First-Class Mail in fiscal year 2025. At 40,000 dispatches per bird-flight, the entire First-Class volume of the United States could be carried in 1,050,000 bird-flights. The United States trained 54,000 pigeons for the Second World War. At one flight each, that flock covers about 5 percent of the nation's first-class mail. Nineteen such flocks would cover it all, and none of them would need a stamp.1016

The speed comparison is sharper still. Cher Ami flew 25 miles in 25 minutes, and G.I. Joe flew 20 miles in 20 minutes. Both birds flew at 60 miles an hour, under fire, carrying a message that could not wait. A first-class letter crossing the country takes two to three days. A pigeon covers 600 miles in ten hours. The bird is faster than the mail, and it has never been paid to be.1110

The bird is also worth more than the mail. In November 2020, a Belgian hen named New Kim sold at auction for 1.6 million euros, about $1.9 million, the most expensive pigeon ever sold. In March 2019, a Belgian cock named Armando sold for 1,252,000 euros, about $1.4 million, more than three times the previous record. The usual price for a racing pigeon is around 2,500 euros. One bird is worth more than two million first-class stamps.22

Fifty-four thousand birds, at eighty-two cents a letter, is $44,280 in unpaid postage.

VII. The Defense

The defense is noted. It concedes every number in this investigation and disputes only the prescription.

Its first exhibit is the statute itself. Under 18 U.S.C. 1696(c), the chapter shall not prohibit the conveyance or transmission of letters or packets by private hands without compensation. The pigeon flies for seed, not pay. No bird has ever charged for carriage, and no bird has ever been paid. The exception is written for exactly this carrier.17

Its second is the bird. The pigeon flies only home, and it cannot deliver to an arbitrary address, and it cannot choose its route, its schedule, or its customer. The statute punishes whoever establishes a private express. A pigeon establishes nothing. It has no intent, no business, and no regular trips of its own choosing. It returns to its loft because that is what the bird does.117

Its third is the stamp. The Great Barrier pigeon companies issued stamps to pre-pay the cost of sending messages, and the stamps were the world's earliest airmail stamps. The Paris pigeon post charged 5 francs per message, and the French postal service itself transcribed the microfilm and delivered the letters by regular post. The two pigeon services in history that charged for their work did pay, in the only currency the market accepted, and the state ran one of them.57

Its fourth is the government. The United States itself operated the largest pigeon express in the country's history: 54,000 birds, 3,150 soldiers, 36,000 birds overseas, and not one stamp affixed to one capsule. The government is the largest historical violator of its own statute, and it disbanded the service in 1957 without ever charging postage, because the birds were soldiers, not carriers.10

Its fifth is the twenty-five-letter rule. Under 18 U.S.C. 1696(c), a special messenger employed for a particular occasion only may carry letters without compensation, and the requirements of 39 U.S.C. 601 apply only when more than twenty-five letters are carried. A pigeon carrying five tissue-paper messages on its legs, as the Great Barrier birds did, carries fewer than twenty-five letters, and the statute does not reach it. Only the microfilm bird, carrying 40,000 dispatches, exceeds the limit, and that bird was flying for a government under siege.5717

Its sixth is the protection the bird does not have. In the United States, the Migratory Bird Treaty Act of 1918, which protects native birds, does not apply to feral pigeons, because they are introduced species. The United States Department of Agriculture labels them an invasive species. The government cannot have it both ways: the bird cannot be an unprotected pest when it nests on a courthouse ledge and a federal mail carrier when it flies over one.23

Its seventh is the protocol. The Internet Engineering Task Force standardized the carriage of messages by homing pigeon in RFC 1149, and the standard has stood for thirty-six years without a single enforcement action. The pigeon is a recognized network carrier, and no network carrier has ever been asked to affix a stamp to a packet.21

The defense rests. It is overruled.

VIII. The Prescription

The ledger is now complete, and it admits of only one reading.

Every pigeon should carry its message in a sealed envelope bearing stamps equal to the postage, canceled in ink by the sender, dated in ink, and properly addressed, affixed to the leg capsule, as 39 U.S.C. 601(a) requires. Alternatively, every pigeon should charge $4.92 per letter under the six-times rule of 39 U.S.C. 601(b), or carry letters weighing at least 12.5 ounces, which no pigeon can do. No bird should carry more than twenty-five letters per flight, as 18 U.S.C. 1696(c) requires. Every loft should be registered as a private express, and every bird should be issued a route number, and every flight should be logged, and the date of transmission should be endorsed on the envelope in ink.1517

The Postal Service should establish a Pigeon Division, register the lofts, and collect the $44,280 in unpaid postage from the Second World War flock, and the $1.77 billion at Paris capacity, and the $20,000,000 fine for a single bird's single flight, and the $1.14 billion for the 57 birds that returned to Paris. The estates of the 54,000 birds should be billed. Cher Ami's Croix de Guerre is not a postage exemption, and G.I. Joe's Dickin Medal is not a franking privilege.101617

The queue should be cleared in order. The Postal Service should process the registrations before any further pigeon is flown, and the lofts should be assigned routes, and the birds should be weighed, and the envelopes should be sealed, and the stamps should be canceled, and the dates should be endorsed, and the fees should be collected, and the forage the birds eat on the way home should be billed at the private rate, since the flock has now exceeded its twenty-five-letter limit for five thousand years in the open, on the public record, one message at a time.117

The test case is the next first-class rate increase. The stamp will rise, the six-times price will rise with it, the pigeons will fly at sixty miles an hour in every season, the messages will be carried at eighty-two cents apiece unpaid, and the envelopes will not exist, and the largest unlicensed private express in the history of the mails will operate for another year, in the open, over every city in the country, one bird at a time.162
