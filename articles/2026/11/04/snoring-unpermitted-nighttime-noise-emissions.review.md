# 7-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Snoring as an Unpermitted Nighttime Noise Emission"

## 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
The article follows the Ergo canon precisely: long factual headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case from source to scale to measurement to standard to receiving property to defense to prescription, genuine footnotes with a Sources section, and the deadpan "Ergo." close. The arc escalates cleanly, and the load-bearing beam is the meter reading against the limit: 52 decibels average at the bedside against a 45 decibel nighttime cap that two jurisdictions on opposite coasts independently set. The defense section concedes the real escape hatches (measurement point, source definition, guideline status, involuntariness) before overruling them, which is the genre's honesty mechanism. Minor weakness: Section V's arithmetic paragraph repeats the 66 percent figure already established in Section III, though the national headcount application justifies the repeat.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0/10
Deadpan investigative register throughout, matching the chimney and honeybee pieces, with the humor emerging exclusively from bureaucratic precision rather than jokes. Standout lines: "It has no off switch that the operator can reach, because the operator is asleep," "A noise regime that cannot account for its most common nighttime emitter has not regulated nighttime noise," "Stack testing, in this regime, is conducted at the pillow," and "waiting for a form that does not exist." The piece never winks; the closest it comes is the hero image caption's meter reading, which lands as straight-faced procedural evidence. Excellent voice match.

## 3. Ethics — 9.0/10
All statutes, guidelines, and study results cited are real and quoted or paraphrased accurately where quoted: WHO bedroom and outdoor night noise recommendations, EPA 55 outdoor and 45 indoor levels, the Noise Control Act declaration, Los Angeles County and Connecticut nighttime limits, Maimon and Hanly intensity figures, Sowho bedside measurements and thresholds, Benjafield global apnea estimates, and Johns Hopkins and National Sleep Foundation prevalence figures. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that a sleeping person is an unpermitted noise emitter under municipal ordinance, is the disclosed conceit; the piece is honest about the definitional escape hatch, that ordinances measure at the property line and regulate devices rather than sleepers, in Section VI, the same way prior pieces disclosed theirs. The national 24 million figure is explicitly flagged in footnote 15 as arithmetic applying a cohort proportion to a survey headcount, not a separately measured national count. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.5/10
"Your snoring violates the noise ordinance" is immediately graspable and funny at the headline level alone, and it is universal in a way many Ergo premises are not: almost every reader either snores or sleeps beside someone who does. The 52 versus 45 pullquote is the single most shareable paragraph; "no off switch that the operator can reach" and "stack testing at the pillow" are strong pull-quote material. The hero image, an inspector metering a snorer at 2:47 AM while the bed partner covers her ears, is built for the thumbnail. Very high share potential.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5/10
The noise framework is accurately summarized: WHO guidelines are guidelines, not permit limits; the Noise Control Act leaves control to state and local governments; most ordinances set quiet hours around 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. with nighttime residential caps of 45 to 50 dBA; Los Angeles County and Connecticut both set 45 dBA at night for residential receivers. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that a person asleep in bed is a regulated emitter under those ordinances, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece never pretends any ordinance defines a sleeper as a device or measures at the bedside. Passes threshold, with the standard Ergo deduction for creative inference from regulatory text, and a further note that the Los Angeles County and Connecticut figures are taken from secondary ordinance summaries rather than primary municipal code text, which the footnotes transparently link.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Strong sourcing across independent authorities: Mayo Clinic patient education for the mechanism, Johns Hopkins summaries and National Sleep Foundation surveys plus NHANES data for prevalence, peer-reviewed sleep studies in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine and Sleep for the meter readings, WHO for the health recommendations, and EPA and ordinance summaries for the legal numbers. Minor weakness: the Sowho cohort is self-reported habitual snorers measured for a single night, not a general population sample, so the 66 percent figure describes that cohort; the article handles this by naming the cohort in the body and flagging the extrapolation in footnote 15. The Benjafield global figure is taken via a 2025 review summarizing the 2019 Lancet analysis, transparently attributed as such.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: 45 percent occasional and 25 percent habitual snoring, 90 million and 37 million Americans, 28.0 percent frequent snorers in NHANES, 46.3 to 60.5 decibels across apnea severity with a 52.0 average, 66 percent above 45 and 14 percent above 53 at the bedside, 30 decibel bedroom and 40 decibel outdoor WHO recommendations, 45 decibel nighttime caps, 936 million and 425 million global apnea estimates, and the computed 24 million habitual emitters above 45 decibels. The comparison framework, measured bedroom output versus every applicable limit on the same decibel scale, is devastating and verifiable. Clear, consistent, sourced.
