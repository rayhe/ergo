# 6-Critic Panel Evaluation: "Black Friday as an Unpermitted Mass Gathering"

## 1. General Editor: 9.0/10
The article follows the established Ergo format precisely: absurd-yet-rigorous headline, scholarly deck, Roman-numeral sections building a systematic case, genuine footnotes with a Sources section. Structure escalates naturally from name to doorbuster to crowd to death to ledger to arithmetic to defense to prescription. The distinctive contribution is the permit hinge, that OSHA's own guidelines tell retailers to ensure all permits and licenses are obtained, which gives the piece a real regulatory hook no prior Ergo investigation has used, and the closing line, that the fine is fifty-nine millionths of one percent of a single day of online sales, is the strongest single beat in the piece. Minor weakness: the doorbuster section leans on 2011 for three of its four case studies, and the ledger section's honest narrowing, that only one of the seventeen deaths was a trampling, slightly undercuts the thesis before the Defense restates it. The closing lands: "The test case is today."

## 2. Voice Coach: 9.0/10
Dead-serious investigative tone throughout, matching the Plymouth Colony and Macy's balloons articles. The humor emerges exclusively from deadpan precision, never from jokes. Standout lines: "The name was a complaint filed by the people who had to manage the crowd," "The store sells the fear, and the television is only the occasion for it," "The maximum fine the law allowed for a death was seven thousand dollars," and "The store was not closed by the death. The store was closed by the managers, and the customers declined to leave." The closing ("one shopper at a time") mirrors the Plymouth piece's cadence without repeating it. Excellent voice match, with a small deduction because the crowd-science pullquote, "People do not die because they panic. They panic because they are dying," is borrowed from the crowd-safety literature rather than written for this piece.

## 3. Ethics: 9.0/10
All facts are real and verified: the 1869 gold panic, the 1950s factory sick day usage, the Philadelphia police origin in the early 1960s, the Big Friday rebrand attempt, the 1961 first print and 1966 column, the 1980s profits rebrand, the 2008 Damour death with its 4:55 a.m. time, two thousand shoppers, asphyxiation, eleven injured, seven thousand dollar OSHA fine, and the Nassau County settlement, the seventeen deaths and one hundred twenty-five injuries tracked since 2006, the 2011 pepper spray incidents, the 2025 Adobe and NRF figures, the 2026 forecasts, the OSHA 2009 guidelines, and the 2015 REI closure, verified against DOL and OSHA, Britannica, Language Log, the National Retail Federation via its published survey summaries, Adobe via its published recaps, REI's own newsroom, and contemporaneous reporting. The deliberately wrong conclusion, that Black Friday is an unpermitted mass gathering and its doorbusters are unlicensed lotteries, is the genre's disclosed conceit, and the piece is honest about the counter-reading in Section V and in the full defense in Section VII, including that sixteen of the seventeen deaths were not crush injuries and that the fine was the statutory maximum. Damour is named, his age and job are stated, and his death is treated as a death, not as a punchline. No ethical concerns.

## 4. Social/Shareability: 9.0/10
"Seven thousand dollars" is immediately graspable, and the timing is perfect: the piece is dated Black Friday itself, November 27, 2026, and publishes the evening before so it is live when the doors open. The seventeen deaths and one hundred twenty-five injuries figure is a strong hook, the fifty-nine millionths of one percent arithmetic is the kind of number that travels, and the hero image, a dawn crowd at a big-box store with a sealed permit form on a clipboard in the foreground, is built for the thumbnail. Slightly less universally graspable than "no balloon has ever held an N-number" because the permit and lottery prescription requires two paragraphs of setup, but the deck does that work. Very high share potential.

## 5. Research Rigor: 8.5/10
Strong sourcing across primary and independent authorities: DOL and OSHA for the citation, the guidelines, and the letter to fourteen retail chief executives; Britannica and Language Log for the name; the University of Florida thesis for the nineteenth-century shopping push; contemporaneous Associated Press reporting for the pepper spray incident; REI's own newsroom for the 2015 closure; and Adobe and NRF figures via their published recaps and survey summaries. Minor weakness: several money figures reach the piece through secondary aggregators rather than the primary Adobe and NRF releases, and the death-count figure comes from a law-firm blog citing the tracker rather than from the tracker itself, and the Cabbage Patch and Hustle figures come through a law-firm summary of the 2019 analysis. The article is transparent about its sources, and the arithmetic assumptions are stated with the figures.

## 6. Data Presentation: 9.0/10
Numbers are presented with consistent precision and always sourced: seventeen deaths, one hundred twenty-five injuries, two thousand shoppers, 4:55 a.m., seven thousand dollars, about two million dollars in the county settlement, two hundred eighty-six times, fifty-nine millionths of one percent, one death per eleven point nine million shoppers, about seven injuries per death, fewer than one death per year, eleven point eight billion dollars online in 2025, forty-four point two billion dollars over five days, two hundred two point nine million shoppers, one hundred sixty-two million on Black Friday itself, twelve point nine billion dollars forecast for 2026, one point zero one three trillion dollars for November and December, five people per square meter, six to seven for fluid behavior, eight to ten for lethal density, one crowd manager per two hundred fifty people, sixty-five years since the naming, eighteen years since the death. All arithmetic was recomputed during review and is correct. Clear, devastating, verifiable.
