The Chemical Weapons Convention’s Own “Toxic Chemical” Definition Classifies 10.7 Million American Swimming Pools as Undeclared Chemical Weapons Stockpiles. Memorial Day Is the Annual Deployment.

Article II of the Chemical Weapons Convention defines a “toxic chemical” as “any chemical which through its chemical action on life processes can cause death, temporary incapacitation or permanent harm to humans or animals.” Chlorine meets every element of this definition. There are 10.7 million swimming pools in the United States. Memorial Day weekend is when most of them come online. The OPCW has inspected Syria for less.

By Nathaniel Hargrove · May 25, 2026 · 15 min read

I. The Treaty Text

The Chemical Weapons Convention was opened for signature on January 13, 1993, and entered into force on April 29, 1997.1 As of 2026, 193 states parties have ratified the Convention. The United States ratified it on April 25, 1997, four days before entry into force.2

Article II, Paragraph 2 of the Convention defines “Toxic Chemical” as follows: “Any chemical which through its chemical action on life processes can cause death, temporary incapacitation or permanent harm to humans or animals. This includes all such chemicals, regardless of their origin or of their method of production, and regardless of whether they are produced in facilities, in munitions or elsewhere.”3

The definition contains four operative elements. First: the substance must be a chemical. Second: it must act on life processes through chemical action. Third: this action must be capable of causing death, temporary incapacitation, or permanent harm. Fourth: these criteria apply regardless of origin, method of production, or location of production.

Elemental chlorine (Cl2) is a chemical. It acts on life processes through chemical action, specifically by reacting with water in mucosal membranes to form hydrochloric acid and hypochlorous acid, which destroy respiratory epithelial tissue.4 It is capable of causing death: the lethal concentration for 50 percent of exposed humans (LC50) over a 30-minute exposure is approximately 430 parts per million.5 At 1,000 ppm, death can occur within minutes. These properties do not change based on origin, method of production, or whether the chlorine is produced in a munitions factory or a pool supply warehouse in Scottsdale, Arizona.

The definition does not contain the word “intent.” It does not contain the word “weapon.” It does not contain qualifications about concentration, dilution, or recreational context. It says “any chemical.” Chlorine is a chemical. The Convention says what it says.

II. The Precedent

On April 22, 1915, at approximately 5:00 p.m., German forces released an estimated 150 to 168 tons of chlorine gas from 5,730 cylinders along a four-mile front at Ypres, Belgium.6 The gas cloud, yellowish-green and heavier than air, drifted across no man’s land into the trenches of the French 45th (Algerian) and 87th (Territorial) Divisions.

The immediate effects were catastrophic. Soldiers who inhaled the gas experienced violent coughing, chest constriction, and pulmonary edema as the chlorine reacted with moisture in their lungs to produce hydrochloric acid.7 The French colonial troops, having no gas masks and no warning, broke and ran. An estimated 1,100 soldiers died in the initial attack, and approximately 3,000 more were incapacitated.8

The attack was orchestrated by Fritz Haber, director of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physical Chemistry and Electrochemistry in Berlin. Haber personally supervised the placement of the chlorine cylinders at the front and waited for favorable winds.9 His wife, Clara Immerwahr, also a chemist and the first woman to earn a doctorate at the University of Breslau, shot herself with Haber’s military pistol nine days later, on May 1, 1915.10 Haber left for the Eastern Front the following morning to supervise additional gas attacks.

By the end of World War I, chemical weapons had caused an estimated 90,000 deaths and 1.3 million total casualties across all belligerents.11 Chlorine was the first, and the chemical properties that made it effective in a trench in Belgium in 1915 are identical to the chemical properties that make it effective in a swimming pool in Phoenix in 2026. The molecule does not know the difference. The Convention does not ask it to.

III. The American Stockpile

According to the Pool & Hot Tub Alliance (PHTA), the United States contains approximately 10.7 million swimming pools. Of these, 10.4 million are residential (6.1 million in-ground and 4.3 million above-ground) and 309,000 are public or commercial facilities.12 This works out to approximately one swimming pool for every 31 Americans.

Nearly all of these pools use some form of chlorine-based sanitization. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends maintaining free chlorine levels between 1 and 3 parts per million in recreational water facilities.13 Pool chlorine is delivered in several forms: sodium hypochlorite (liquid bleach, typically 10 to 12.5 percent available chlorine), calcium hypochlorite (granular or tablet, 65 to 73 percent available chlorine), and trichloroisocyanuric acid (stabilized tablets, 90 percent available chlorine).14

A standard residential in-ground pool holds approximately 20,000 gallons of water. Maintaining 2 ppm of free chlorine in 20,000 gallons requires roughly 5.3 ounces of calcium hypochlorite per treatment, or approximately 10 pounds per month during peak swimming season.15 Across 10.7 million pools operating from Memorial Day through Labor Day (approximately 100 days in most of the country), aggregate American chlorine consumption for recreational water alone is on the order of tens of thousands of tons per season.

The United States consumed approximately 1.2 million metric tons of chlorine in 2024 across all applications, including water treatment, chemical manufacturing, and pool sanitation.16 The precise share attributable to swimming pools is proprietary to the chemical industry, but the seasonal demand spike that begins each Memorial Day weekend is well documented in chlorine commodity markets. Production facilities increase output months in advance. Distribution networks pre-stage inventory at retail locations. The operation has logistics.

The CWC requires each state party to declare chemical weapons stockpiles to the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons. The United States has never declared a swimming pool.

IV. The Deployment Window

Memorial Day, observed on the last Monday of May, was established on May 30, 1868, as Decoration Day by General Order No. 11, issued by General John A. Logan, Commander-in-Chief of the Grand Army of the Republic.17 The order directed that the day be designated “for the purpose of strewing with flowers or otherwise decorating the graves of comrades who died in defense of their country during the late rebellion.” The Uniform Monday Holiday Act of 1971 moved the observance to the last Monday of May and established it as a federal holiday.18

The holiday also marks, by universal convention, the unofficial opening of the American swimming pool season.19 Millions of pools that have been winterized since September are drained, refilled, shocked with chlorine, and brought to operational chemical concentration levels during the week preceding Memorial Day. The process is called “opening the pool.” In chemical weapons terminology, the more precise description would be “activating a stockpile.”

AAA projected that 45 million Americans would travel 50 miles or more from home during the 2026 Memorial Day weekend, with 39.1 million traveling by car.20 A significant fraction of these travelers are heading toward pools. The National Swimming Pool Foundation (now PHTA) has estimated that swimming is the fourth most popular recreational activity in the United States, with approximately 91 million Americans aged six and older swimming at least once per year.21

Ninety-one million people voluntarily entering facilities containing a documented chemical weapon, on a weekend dedicated to honoring people who died in wars, some of whom died from that exact chemical, is a coincidence of such structural perfection that it cannot be a coincidence.

V. The Dosimetry

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) sets the permissible exposure limit (PEL) for chlorine gas at 1 ppm as a ceiling concentration, meaning no worker should be exposed above this level at any time during a work shift.22 The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) recommends a ceiling of 0.5 ppm.23 The immediately dangerous to life or health (IDLH) concentration is 10 ppm.24

The CDC recommends maintaining swimming pool water at 1 to 3 ppm free chlorine.25

These numbers deserve to be placed in proximity. OSHA says workers cannot be exposed to more than 1 ppm of chlorine in air. The CDC says swimmers should be immersed in 1 to 3 ppm of chlorine in water. The chlorine in pool water continuously off-gasses, particularly in heated pools and indoor facilities, producing airborne chlorine and chloramine concentrations that multiple studies have measured at levels approaching or exceeding occupational limits in poorly ventilated natatoriums.26

A 2007 study published in the journal Occupational and Environmental Medicine found that competitive swimmers had a significantly higher prevalence of airway hyperresponsiveness compared to non-swimming athletes, consistent with chronic low-level chemical irritant exposure.27 A 2003 study in Pediatrics found that infant swimming in chlorinated indoor pools was associated with increased risk of recurrent bronchitis.28 The chemical does not stop being a chemical because the context is recreational.

The German soldiers at Ypres deployed chlorine at concentrations between 200 and 500 ppm over a period of minutes.29 A typical American pool contains chlorine at 2 ppm in 20,000 gallons, but the total mass of available chlorine in a single residential pool (approximately 0.33 pounds of elemental chlorine equivalent) is a function of volume, not concentration. The aggregate tonnage of chlorine distributed across 10.7 million American pools on any given summer day is orders of magnitude greater than what was deployed at Ypres. The concentration per pool is lower. The total deployment is not.

VI. The “Peaceful Purposes” Exception

Article II, Paragraph 9 of the CWC defines “Purposes Not Prohibited Under this Convention” to include: “(a) Industrial, agricultural, research, medical, pharmaceutical or other peaceful purposes.”30

This is the provision under which every swimming pool in America technically avoids classification as a chemical weapons facility. The chlorine is used for a “peaceful purpose”: recreational water sanitation. The exemption rests on the word “peaceful.”

The question is whether the word “peaceful” can sustain the weight being placed on it.

Consider the empirical data. The Consumer Product Safety Commission reports approximately 6,300 emergency department visits per year related to pool chemical injuries in the United States, with nearly half involving children under 18.31 The CDC documented 13 outbreaks of acute illness associated with pool chemical exposures between 2003 and 2012, resulting in 566 cases of illness.32 Chlorine gas release incidents at public pools are a recurring feature of the emergency response literature.

An activity that sends 6,300 people to the emergency room annually, that produces measurable chronic respiratory effects in regular participants, and that involves a chemical whose battlefield deployment is a war crime, occupies an unusual position in the taxonomy of “peaceful purposes.” The Convention does not define “peaceful.” It does not specify a casualty threshold below which a purpose becomes peaceful. It does not explain how a chemical can be a weapon of mass destruction in Douma and a recreational amenity in Scottsdale.

VII. Fritz Haber’s Other Legacy

Fritz Haber, the architect of the Ypres attack, received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1918 for the synthesis of ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen, a process now known as the Haber-Bosch process.33 The award was controversial. Several Nobel laureates protested that a man who had pioneered chemical warfare should not receive science’s highest honor. The committee awarded it anyway, on the grounds that the ammonia synthesis process had made industrial fertilizer production possible, thereby preventing global famine.

The Haber-Bosch process is responsible for producing the nitrogen fertilizer that supports approximately half of the world’s current food production.34 An estimated 3.5 billion people alive today owe their existence, directly or through the food supply chain, to Fritz Haber’s work.35

This includes the cattle that become the hamburgers that are grilled on Memorial Day.

The National Hot Dog and Sausage Council estimates that Americans will consume approximately 7 billion hot dogs between Memorial Day and Labor Day, a rate of approximately 818 hot dogs per second sustained across the entire summer.36 Americans spent over $11 billion on hot dogs and sausages in supermarkets alone in 2025.37 The cattle and hogs that become these hot dogs were raised on feed crops grown with synthetic fertilizer produced by the process that Fritz Haber invented between sessions of weaponizing chlorine.

On Memorial Day, Americans honor soldiers who died in wars. Some of those soldiers died from chlorine gas developed by Fritz Haber. Americans honor them by grilling meat made possible by Fritz Haber’s fertilizer process, beside pools sanitized with Fritz Haber’s weapon. The holiday is, in a very real chemical sense, a Fritz Haber tribute festival. He is not credited on the invitations.

VIII. The OPCW’s Jurisdiction

The Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons, headquartered in The Hague, employs approximately 500 staff members and conducts routine inspections of declared chemical facilities worldwide.38 Since 2014, the OPCW has conducted extensive investigations into the use of chlorine as a weapon in the Syrian civil war. The OPCW’s Investigation and Identification Team concluded in 2020 that the Syrian Arab Air Force had used chlorine in attacks on Ltamenah in March 2017 and Douma in April 2018.39

The chlorine used in these attacks was industrial-grade chlorine, the same category of chemical product available at any pool supply retailer in the United States. The OPCW did not distinguish between “weapons-grade” chlorine and “pool-grade” chlorine because no such distinction exists. Chlorine is chlorine. The molecule Cl2 does not have a military variant and a civilian variant. It has one electron configuration, one set of reactive properties, and one relationship to human lung tissue.

The OPCW conducted approximately 250 inspections per year across all state parties prior to the Syrian investigations.40 None of these inspections targeted residential swimming pools. The United States contains 10.7 million sites where a Convention-defined toxic chemical is stored, maintained at operational concentration, and seasonally deployed by civilians. The OPCW’s inspection capacity would need to increase by a factor of approximately 42,800 to conduct annual inspections of each American pool.

This is, it should be acknowledged, a staffing challenge. But it is not the OPCW’s job to determine which stockpiles of toxic chemicals are inconvenient to inspect. It is the OPCW’s job to verify compliance with the Convention. The Convention does not contain a de minimis clause for suburban infrastructure.

IX. The Mattress Question

There is one additional data point that, while circumstantial, merits documentation.

Memorial Day weekend is, by long-established convention, the single largest mattress sale event in the American retail calendar. The Better Sleep Council, an arm of the International Sleep Products Association, reports that 43 percent of Americans who intend to purchase a mattress in a given year plan to do so during a holiday sale, with Memorial Day consistently ranking as the top shopping occasion.41 Mattress retailers begin advertising weeks in advance. The advertising is ubiquitous and culturally specific to the holiday.

No other national holiday in the United States has been commercially captured by a single product category with this degree of specificity. Valentine’s Day is associated with chocolate, flowers, and jewelry across multiple product categories. Christmas is associated with essentially all retail. Memorial Day belongs, in the commercial imagination, to mattresses and pools.

The obvious question is why. The stated explanation is that Memorial Day falls at a convenient point in the calendar: consumers use the long weekend for home projects, including mattress replacement. This explanation is adequate. What it does not explain is why the American consumer, on a holiday originally designated for decorating the graves of war dead, developed a specific cultural association with the two activities of lying down on a new mattress and immersing themselves in a dilute solution of a chemical weapon.

One is horizontal. The other is a controlled chemical exposure. Both involve a state of deliberate vulnerability. The pattern is noted. The implications are not pursued further here.

X. The Geneva Protocol’s Silence

The Protocol for the Prohibition of the Use in War of Asphyxiating, Poisonous or Other Gases, and of Bacteriological Methods of Warfare, commonly known as the Geneva Protocol, was signed on June 17, 1925.42 It was a direct response to the chemical horrors of World War I, including the chlorine attacks that began at Ypres. The United States signed the Protocol in 1925 but did not ratify it until January 22, 1975, a delay of nearly 50 years.43

The 50-year gap between signature and ratification (1925 to 1975) coincides almost precisely with the period of mass suburbanization in the United States, during which private swimming pool ownership expanded from a luxury of the wealthy to a middle-class norm. The first affordable residential in-ground pools became available in the late 1940s and 1950s, using fiberglass and vinyl liner technology.44 Pool construction boomed through the 1960s and 1970s.

The United States had a chemical weapons protocol on its desk, unsigned for operational purposes, for the exact decades during which it was building millions of chlorine-dependent facilities in residential backyards. The protocol was ratified in 1975, by which point the pool infrastructure was so vast and so deeply embedded in the American way of life that no conceivable interpretation of the protocol could threaten it. The ratification became safe precisely because the deployment was already complete.

This is not necessarily evidence of coordination. It is evidence of timing.

XI. The Holiday Itself

General Logan’s General Order No. 11 is specific. The purpose of Decoration Day is to honor “comrades who died in defense of their country.” The order prescribes flowers. It prescribes remembrance. It does not prescribe chlorinated water, grilled meat produced by the agricultural legacy of a chemical weapons pioneer, or mattress sales.

Approximately 116,516 American service members died in World War I.45 An estimated 1,462 of these deaths were caused by chemical weapons, primarily chlorine and its derivatives phosgene and mustard gas, on the Western Front.46 The total chemical weapons death toll across all belligerents was approximately 90,000.47

On Memorial Day 2026, approximately 91 million Americans will at some point this summer swim in water sanitized with the same element that killed those soldiers. They will do so beginning on the holiday established to honor those soldiers. The chlorine in the pool and the chlorine in the trenches share a periodic table entry (atomic number 17), a mechanism of biological harm (mucosal hydrolysis), and a man (Fritz Haber, 1868 to 1934).

The Convention draws its line at intent. Chlorine in a barrel dropped from a helicopter over Douma is a chemical weapon. Chlorine in a bucket poured into a pool in Scottsdale is a peaceful purpose. The molecule is identical. The legal distinction rests entirely on the context surrounding deployment.

Context is not a property of chlorine. Context is a property of lawyers.

XII. Conclusion

The Chemical Weapons Convention defines a toxic chemical as any chemical that can cause death through chemical action on life processes. Chlorine can cause death through chemical action on life processes. Therefore, chlorine is a toxic chemical under the Convention. This is not an interpretation. It is a syllogism.

The Convention exempts toxic chemicals used for peaceful purposes. Swimming is categorized as a peaceful purpose. The exemption is functional. It is also, on its face, a remarkable exercise in definitional generosity. A chemical that killed 90,000 soldiers, that has been the subject of multiple international investigations when deployed in Syria, and that sends 6,300 Americans to the emergency room each year from pool-related exposures alone, receives a blanket exemption because it is mixed with water and children are allowed to splash in it.

There are 10.7 million of these facilities in the United States. Most of them activate during the same 72-hour window each year: Memorial Day weekend. The weekend was established to honor soldiers who died in wars. Some of those soldiers were killed by the chemical that is being activated. The man who first weaponized that chemical also invented the fertilizer process that feeds the cattle that become the hamburgers that are grilled poolside during the activation.

The OPCW has never inspected a single one.

This is either the most significant gap in international arms control verification since the Convention entered into force, or it is a barbecue. The Convention’s text does not provide the tools to distinguish between the two. The molecule certainly does not. And the 45 million Americans hitting the road this weekend are not asking.

Happy Memorial Day.
